== A | Resistance: R = V/I | R = V|V = IR|I = V|resistance|p\.d\.|V ÷ I|V ÷ R | 7
- Resistance = potential difference (voltage) ÷ current: R = V/I (B1 where the equation is asked for).
- R = V/I, I = V/R or V = IR in any form (C1); answer (A1). In three-mark questions the stated formula (B1), the substitution (C1) and the answer (A1) are separate.
- Use the p.d. across the component itself and the current in that same component: resistance of a component = p.d. across it ÷ current in it (C1 or A1); current in a resistor = p.d. across it ÷ its resistance (C1 or B1).
- Put the current in amperes (mA to A) and the resistance in ohms (kΩ to Ω) before substituting.
- Correct units on the current and resistance answers (A and Ω) can carry a separate mark (B1).
- Where the current has to be found first from other data, that step carries its own mark (C1); then R = V/I (C1); answer (A1). The current must be the one in the component concerned: using the current in one lamp where the battery current was needed earned the first mark only.
- p.d. across one resistor, or across one part of a circuit = the current in it × its resistance (C1, A1; B1 where there is one mark); a voltmeter across a fixed resistor reads V = IR.
- For the whole circuit: current from the supply = e.m.f. (supply p.d.) ÷ total resistance of the circuit, I = V/R (C1); answer (A1). In reverse: total resistance of the circuit = e.m.f. ÷ current from the supply (C1, A1); e.m.f. = current × total resistance of the circuit (C1, A1). Identical cells in parallel count as the e.m.f. of one cell.
- A resistor or lamp connected directly across the supply has the supply p.d. across it: its current = supply p.d. ÷ its own resistance (B1 for each current, or C1).
- In a question with several steps each use of the equation carries a mark: for example the current in a fixed resistor from its p.d. and resistance (C1), then the resistance of another component from the p.d. across it and the current in it (C1 or A1). Where two routes are possible, either the p.d. across one component or the total resistance of the circuit earns the method mark.
- "Show that" a current: write each step with its numbers – p.d. across a resistor = current in it × its resistance (B1); current in the resistor with that p.d. across it = p.d. ÷ its resistance (B1).
- A two-step question: V = IR with the current in amperes (C1, A1), then Q = It (C1, A1).
* For a fixed resistor V ÷ I is constant: for 100 Ω, 1 V gives 10 mA, 2 V gives 20 mA and 3 V gives 30 mA.
* Read the meter, change mA to A, then use V = IR; change kΩ to Ω before using I = V ÷ R.
* A lightning conductor is made of copper so that it has a low resistance.
* Whole circuit: current = e.m.f. ÷ total resistance of the circuit; then the p.d. across any one resistor = that current × its resistance (10 V with a total of 50 Ω gives 0.20 A, and 0.20 × 40 = 8.0 V across a 40 Ω resistor). In reverse, total resistance of the circuit = V ÷ I.
* A resistor connected directly across the battery carries I = battery p.d. ÷ its resistance (12 V across 6 Ω gives 2.0 A; across 3 Ω, 4.0 A).
* p.d. across a resistor = IR (4 A in 6 Ω gives 24 V); another resistor with that same p.d. across it carries p.d. ÷ its resistance (24 ÷ 12 = 2 A).
* A current of a few milliamperes is best read on the range that puts it near mid-scale (4.8 mA on 0–10 mA).
== O | Ohm's law | Ohm's law | 0
- Ohm's law: the current in a conductor is (directly) proportional to the p.d. across it – voltage ÷ current is constant (B1 or M1) – provided the temperature / physical conditions remain constant (B1 or A1).
- Ohm's law is obeyed when the current–voltage graph is a straight line through the origin.
- A lamp does not obey Ohm's law: its graph is curved / not a straight line through the origin / the current is not proportional to the voltage.
- Showing Ohm's law from a table of results: calculate at least two values of V ÷ I (or I ÷ V) and show that they are the same.
* Ohm's law applies only if the temperature is constant, and only over the straight-line part of the I–V graph through the origin.
== B | Experiment to determine resistance | circuit to|circuit for|experiment|read the ammeter|range of current and voltage readings|corrections to|measuring resistance|to find a resistance|meter ranges|one complete set|to investigate how|temperature kept constant | 2
- Circuit: correct symbols for the battery, the resistor (wire or lamp), the variable resistor, the ammeter and the voltmeter (B1); the ammeter in series with the component so that it measures the current through it (B1); the voltmeter connected across – in parallel with – the component (B1).
- For a lamp alone: cell, lamp and ammeter in series and no other components (B1); voltmeter in parallel with the lamp (B1).
- To obtain a range of readings: a variable resistor (rheostat) in series, a variable power supply, a different number of cells, or a correctly connected potential divider (B1).
- Voltmeter range: 0 to a value just above the largest p.d. needed (12–20 V for readings up to 12 V) (B1).
- Readings: read the ammeter and the voltmeter (B1); change the current and repeat (B1); calculate R = V/I.
- One complete set of results for a wire (any two): measure the length of the wire, the p.d. and the current; calculate the resistance R = V/I; change the length and measure V and I again.
- The temperature is kept constant because resistance changes with temperature – and a hot wire may melt or burn (B1) – by using small currents, switching off between readings, or putting the wire in a water bath (B1).
- Corrections to a faulty circuit: swap the voltmeter and the ammeter so that the ammeter is in series and the voltmeter is across the wire (B1); all the cells must face the same way (B1).
* To find a resistance the ammeter is in series with the resistor, carrying only its current, and the voltmeter is in parallel across that resistor only; then R = V ÷ I.
* Choose meter ranges just large enough for the readings: with R ≈ 5 Ω and a p.d. up to 2 V the current is up to 0.4 A, so use a 0–2 V voltmeter and a 0–0.5 A ammeter.
== C | Current–voltage graphs for a fixed resistor and a filament lamp | graph|sketch|straight line|gradient|curv | 4
- Fixed resistor: the current–voltage graph is a straight line through the origin (B1) – the current is directly proportional to the p.d. – because its resistance (temperature) is constant (B1).
- Filament lamp: a curved line starting at the origin (C1, M1 or B1), with decreasing gradient – curving towards the voltage (p.d.) axis – and a gradient that is always greater than zero, never flat or falling (A1 or B1). Where an operating point is given it must be plotted (B1).
- A graph curving towards the p.d. axis shows that the resistance increases at higher p.d.: the gradient decreases, so the current is lower than a proportional increase would give and R = V/I is larger (B1).
- Current not proportional to p.d. is shown by the graph not being a straight line (B1), because the filament gets hotter and its resistance increases (B1).
- A constant temperature is shown by the graph being a straight line / having a constant gradient.
- The resistance of a filament lamp increases over the range where the graph is no longer a straight line through the origin.
- A component with a current–p.d. graph that curves towards the p.d. axis is a filament lamp.
- If the p.d. is directly proportional to the current the resistance is constant: the resistance–current graph is a horizontal line above the axis.
* Where the I–V graphs of two lamps coincide the lamps have the same resistance; the lamp that carries the larger current at the same voltage has the lower resistance.
* On a voltage–current graph (voltage on the vertical axis) the gradient is the resistance, so the line for a filament lamp gets steeper as the voltage increases.
* The current in a filament lamp never falls as the voltage rises.
== H | The diode: current in one direction only | diode | -1
- A diode passes current (charge) in one direction only (B1). Accepted instead: it has a very high resistance – it acts as an insulator – for current in the other direction (when reverse biased); with an alternating supply there is current for only half of each cycle, so the current is direct.
- A diode connected the other way round (reversed): there is no current – no ammeter reading – because the reversed diode does not conduct / has a very high resistance / blocks the current (B1). A reference to the current–voltage graph of the diode is accepted as the reason.
== G | Using current–voltage graphs to find current, p.d. and resistance | read from|values read|found by proportion from graph|from the straight-line graph|supported by graph values|read each | 3
- R = V/I with both values read from the same point of the line (C1); answer (A1), quoted to 2 or 3 significant figures. On a straight-line graph any pair of values may be used.
- For a lamp the resistance is found separately at each voltage: for example at a low voltage (C1, A1) and again at the working voltage (B1).
- The current in a lamp connected directly across the supply is read from its graph at the supply voltage (B1).
- The current in a lamp is read from its graph at the p.d. across it (B1); the p.d. across a resistor that carries this current = IR (B1).
- At a stated current, read each p.d. from the graph of that component (B1 each); resistance of the lamp at this current R = V/I (C1, A1).
- At a stated p.d., read each current from the graph of that component (B1 each); resistance of each = that p.d. ÷ its current (B1 or C1).
- Current–temperature graph for a thermistor circuit: read the current at the given temperature (C1); V = IR (C1, A1); with the p.d. constant, R = V/I using the current read at the new temperature (C1, A1).
- LDR: the resistance decreases in brighter light (B1), supported by values from the graphs – the resistances calculated, a larger current at the same voltage, or less voltage needed for the same current (B1).
- Resistance–length graph: find the resistance of the given length by proportion (B1); then I = V/R (C1, A1).
== F | Resistance and temperature: the filament lamp | temperature|filament|hot|cold|heated|warmer|lamp gets|switch-on|switched on|heats up | 5
- The resistance of a metal conductor increases as its temperature increases (M1), approximately linearly (A1).
- The resistance of a filament lamp changes because the temperature of the filament is not constant (C1): a higher current / voltage produces a higher temperature (B1) and so a higher resistance (B1 or A1). Also accepted as the reason: the particles / ions vibrate more vigorously and impede the electron flow.
- After a lamp is switched on: the temperature of the filament rises / the lamp heats up (1) – "the lamp releases heat" is accepted, "heat lost" and "the wires get hot" are ignored; the resistance of the filament increases, so the current falls (1) to a constant value. It must be an increase: "the resistance is high" is ignored.
- At a lower voltage the resistance of a filament lamp is (very much) smaller (B1), because the filament is cooler (B1); halving the voltage gives more than half the current.
- As a filament lamp gets brighter its resistance increases (B1), because its temperature increases (B1).
- A filament lamp often fails just after switch-on because of the current surge: the resistance is lower when the filament is cold.
- A series variable resistor turned down slowly protects a filament lamp: when cold the filament has a low resistance, so switching on suddenly would give a very high current (B1), which could melt the filament (B1).
- A metal wire (coil) heated or getting warmer: its resistance increases (B1 or M1), so the ammeter reading decreases (B1 or A1); the current is larger when the coil is cold.
- A metal conductor connected in a circuit with a fixed resistor is heated: the voltmeter reading across the fixed resistor falls (B1), because the current supplied by the battery falls (B1).
- Graph of the resistance of a metal conductor against temperature: a line of positive slope throughout (B1); a straight line with a positive intercept on the resistance axis (B1).
- Experiment on the resistance and temperature of a wire: a circuit with cell and ammeter in series with the wire and a voltmeter across the wire (B1); resistance calculated from R = V/I (B1); temperature measured with a thermometer (B1); temperature changed with a water bath / oven and the readings repeated (B1).
- Showing that a resistance does not change linearly with temperature: compare the changes in resistance for two equal changes in temperature (C1); they are not the same (A1).
* After switch-on the resistance rises from an initial non-zero value to a steady value while the current falls from a high value to a steady value.
* On a straight-line resistance–temperature graph equal temperature rises give equal increases in resistance.
== D | Resistance of a wire: length and cross-sectional area | length|cross-sectional|thick|thin|diameter|sleeve | 1
- The resistance of a wire is directly proportional to its length, for a constant cross-sectional area (B1), and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area, for a constant length (B1).
- A resistance–length graph that is a straight line through the origin shows the direct proportion.
- A wire of the same material and length with half the cross-sectional area has twice the resistance (B1); its current–p.d. graph is a straight line through the origin below the original line (M1), with half the gradient (A1).
- A wire of the same material, length and temperature with a greater resistance has a smaller cross-sectional area / diameter.
- The steeper current–voltage line belongs to the shorter wire, with a reason: its resistance is smaller (resistance is proportional to length) / a larger current for the same voltage / a smaller voltage for the same current (B1).
- At constant p.d., as the length increases the current decreases (C1): the current is inversely proportional to the length (A1).
- A thinner wire of the same length and material has a larger resistance (B1), so the power is less because the current is smaller at the same voltage (B1).
- Wires carrying a large current are made thick so that they have a low resistance (B1) and so do not get as hot or melt – less energy is lost as heat in the cable (B1).
- A filament has a higher resistance if it is (two of): longer; thinner; hotter; made of a poorer conductor.
- The same volume of metal with the length halved has double the cross-sectional area (C1); with R ∝ length and R ∝ 1/area the new resistance is a quarter of the old (A1).
- Scaling a resistance: R = old R × (new length ÷ old length) × (old area ÷ new area) – the correct figures with any power of ten (C1); answer (A1).
- Length by proportion: length = resistance ÷ resistance per unit length (B1, B1); for a lamp filament use the resistance of the cold filament (the low-voltage value).
* R ∝ length ÷ cross-sectional area: twice the length and half the area give four times the resistance; half the length and twice the area give a quarter; twice the length and four times the area give half.
* Covering a wire with an insulating sleeve does not change its resistance; a larger cross-sectional area, a shorter length or a lower temperature all reduce it.
* Thick, short wires have a low resistance and are used where the current is large; long thin wires are adequate where the current is small.
* On a voltage–current graph the steeper line is the greater resistance.
* If the length of wire in a circuit is changed, find the new resistance by proportion and then the new current from I = V ÷ R with the same p.d.
== E | Changing the resistance in a circuit: the effect on current and p.d. | switch open | 6
- Increasing a resistance in a circuit increases the total resistance of the circuit (B1), so the current decreases and the p.d. across a fixed resistor that carries this current (V = IR) decreases (B1).
- When the total resistance of a circuit decreases, the current from the supply increases (B1); the p.d. across a fixed resistor that carries this current increases (M1), because p.d. is proportional to current for a fixed resistance, V = IR (A1).
- A larger p.d. across a fixed resistor means that the current in it has increased, I = V/R (B1), so the resistance of the variable resistor has decreased – the current is larger for the same supply p.d. (B1).
- To increase the current in a coil (to make an electromagnet stronger): decrease the resistance of the variable resistor (B1), which increases the current in the coil (B1).
- The current in a lamp is smaller (B1) because the total resistance of the circuit is higher, or because the lamp has less than the full supply p.d. across it (B1).
- For the same p.d., the resistor with the smaller current has the larger resistance, R = V/I (B1).
- With the switch open there is no current, so the p.d. across a resistor is 0 V.
* Decreasing the resistance of a variable resistor decreases the total resistance of the circuit, so the current increases and a lamp is brighter; the e.m.f. is unchanged. Increasing its resistance reduces the current, so lamps are dimmer and a motor turns more slowly.
* A lamp is brightest when the current in it is largest, that is when the total resistance of the circuit is smallest.
* For the same p.d., the smaller resistance carries the larger current and the larger resistance the smaller current (I = V ÷ R).
* The current leaving a cell equals the current returning to it.
