== A | What a wave is: energy transferred without transfer of matter | energy|matter|medium|vibrat|oscillat | 7
- Wave motion (any two): the transmission of energy; without (net) movement of the medium – no transfer of matter; through the vibration (oscillation) of the particles.
- Showing that a water wave transfers energy: place a cork or small floating object on the surface (B1); it moves up and down as the wave passes (B1).
- An oscillating particle (or floating object) in a wave: its speed increases as it moves towards the middle of its movement (1); it is fastest in the middle, decelerates after the middle and stops at the top and at the bottom (1).
* A wave transfers energy in the direction of travel without transferring the water itself: a floating ball only moves up and down.
* Ripples crossing a ripple tank transfer energy by wave motion.
* A wave that transfers energy to an object loses energy, so its amplitude decreases; its frequency, speed and wavelength are unchanged.
== C | Wave terms: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, wavefront | amplitude|wavelength|frequency|period|crest|trough|wavefront|peak|pulses|its speed decreases | 6
- Amplitude: the maximum displacement from the mean (undisturbed, rest) position. On a diagram it is marked from the undisturbed level to the top of a crest or the bottom of a trough – not from crest to trough.
- Amplitude from a graph = maximum height − mean (rest) height, not peak to trough.
- Wavelength: the distance between two consecutive identical points on the wave (points in phase), such as one crest and the next. Two-mark version: the distance between neighbouring waves (C1); between neighbouring wavefronts / points of the same phase, crest to crest or trough to trough (A1).
- Wavelength from a diagram = distance spanned ÷ number of complete waves (spaces between wavefronts) in that distance; the distance between adjacent crests is one wavelength.
- Wavelength from a scale diagram: measure across several wavelengths and divide (C1); multiply by the scale factor (A1).
- Frequency: the number of (complete) waves / cycles / oscillations per unit time (per second). Two-mark version: the number of waves that pass a point, or are generated by the source (M1 or C1), in one second / per unit time (A1).
- Measuring a frequency: count the number of oscillations (crests passing a point) in a measured time, or measure the time for a given number of waves, or find the time for one wave (1); divide the number of waves by the time in seconds, or use f = 1/period (1).
- In that method v = fλ is ignored unless the wavelength and speed measurements are described; "find the period" alone is not enough; "frequency is the number of waves per second" with no measurement scores 1 mark only.
- Wavefront: a line (C1 or M1) joining points on the wave that have the same phase / are at the same point in their motion, for example a line along a crest (A1).
- Number of crests (or pulses) produced = frequency × time (C1, A1 where two marks are given).
- A constant frequency is shown by a constant period: each oscillation takes the same time even though the amplitude falls.
- A wave of smaller frequency at the same speed is drawn with a larger wavelength. To make a longer wavelength on the same rope or spring, use a lower frequency (to double the wavelength, halve the frequency: one oscillation every 0.5 s instead of 0.25 s). A later scheme accepted "move the hand more slowly"; an early scheme did not accept "move the hand slower" without the frequency.
- A wave entering a medium where it travels faster: the frequency does not change (B1); the wavelength increases (B1).
- A decrease in speed (B1) is shown by the wavelength decreasing – crests closer together – or by the direction of travel bending towards the normal (B1).
- Displacement–time graph for a point half a wavelength away: the same period and amplitude (B1), but opposite phase – inverted (B1).
- A wave drawn a time t later has the same shape moved along by the distance v × t in the direction of travel; a wavefront moves a distance speed × time.
- Comparing two waves from their height–time graphs: a difference is the amplitude or the phase (B1); a similarity is the frequency / period, or that both are transverse (B1).
- Values such as the time for the amplitude to fall to half are read from the trace by counting complete cycles.
* Amplitude = half the vertical distance between a trough and a peak = half the difference between the maximum and minimum displacement; it is the height of a crest above the undisturbed level.
* On a displacement–time graph the amplitude is the height of a peak above the zero line and the time for one complete cycle is the period; the wavelength can only be read from a displacement–distance graph.
* From one zero crossing to the next, or from a crest to the adjacent trough, is half a wavelength; from the first crest to the third crest is two wavelengths.
* The period is the time for one complete cycle (three cycles in 12 ms give a period of 4 ms).
* Frequency is the number of waves per second, so in one minute frequency × 60 waves are produced.
* For a water wave the speed is the horizontal distance travelled per second by a peak.
* A wavefront is a line along a crest, seen from above; wavefronts from a point source are circular; wavefronts are perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels.
== B | Transverse and longitudinal waves | transverse|longitudinal|perpendicular|parallel to the direction|compression|rarefaction | 3
- Transverse wave: the vibration (oscillation) is perpendicular / at right angles to the direction of propagation – the direction of travel of the wave or of the energy (B1). Longitudinal wave: the vibration is parallel to the direction of propagation (B1).
- Longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions; transverse waves have crests and troughs (credited as the first mark in some schemes).
- Longitudinal wave, two-mark definition: energy is transmitted through a medium by vibrations of the particles – compressions and rarefactions (C1); the direction of vibration is parallel to the direction of travel of the wave (A1).
- Diagrams when asked: a transverse wave showing at least one wavelength (B1); a longitudinal wave, for example the coils of a slinky spring, showing at least one wavelength (B1).
- A point on a spring carrying a longitudinal wave moves backwards and forwards (to and fro), parallel to the direction of the wave.
- Particles of a string, or a water molecule, in a transverse wave oscillate (B1) up and down, at right angles to the direction of travel (B1); the arrow is drawn up or down, not along the wave.
- Making a transverse wave on a rope or spring: the hand moves up and down, at right angles to the rope (a double-headed arrow) (B1); for a frequency of 4.0 Hz the hand makes 4 complete oscillations every second (B1).
- Compression: a region where the particles (layers of air) are closer together – higher pressure than normal (B1). Rarefaction: a region where the particles are further apart – lower pressure (B1). On a diagram they are where the lines are closest together and furthest apart.
- Wavelength of a longitudinal wave: a diagram showing compressions and rarefactions (M1); the wavelength marked from the centre of one compression to the centre of the next (A1). Also defined as the (shortest) distance between two points in phase.
- The distance between a compression and the nearest rarefaction is half a wavelength.
- Sound is longitudinal; a water surface wave and microwaves are transverse; microwaves are electromagnetic and sound is not.
- A wave on a rope is transverse and a compression wave on a spring is longitudinal (both needed for the mark).
- Difference between a sound wave and a water wave: in sound the particles vibrate backwards and forwards along the direction of travel – compressions and rarefactions, about 300–330 m/s in air (B1); in a water wave the particles move up and down at 90° to the direction of travel – crests and troughs, much slower (B1).
- A single source that generates both longitudinal and transverse waves: an earthquake (seismic waves); lightning was also accepted.
- From seismic P-waves, seismic S-waves, sound and X-rays, the transverse waves are seismic S-waves and X-rays.
* Light, radio waves, infrared, X-rays and gamma rays, waves on a rope and water ripples are transverse; sound and ultrasound are longitudinal, in solids as well as in air.
* Both longitudinal waves (sound) and transverse waves (surface water waves, light) can travel in water; only electromagnetic waves travel in a vacuum.
* A ball floating on water moves up and down as a transverse wave passes; it is not carried along.
* In a transverse wave each point only moves up and down; a point takes the displacement of the part of the wave just behind it, so a point at zero displacement with a crest approaching moves up.
== D | The wave equation v = fλ; frequency and period (f = 1/T) | =\s*fλ|v\s*=\s*f|f\s*=\s*v|λ\s*=\s*v|λ\s*=\s*c|1\s*[÷/]\s*T|1\s*[÷/]\s*f|1/period|speed = frequency × wavelength|frequency × wavelength|v ÷ f|v ÷ λ|v/λ|v/f|height-time graph for one point | 2
- v = fλ (speed = frequency × wavelength) in any form (C1 or B1); answer (A1).
- Convert the units first: kHz, MHz or GHz to Hz; cm or mm to m; km/s to m/s (a separate C1 in some three-mark questions).
- Three-mark calculations with powers of ten: the equation with the substitution (C1); the correct figures with a wrong power of ten (C1); the correct answer (A1).
- f = 1/T and T = 1/f: the period is read from the graph or trace as the time for one complete wave (C1); answer (A1).
- Frequency = number of waves ÷ time taken (C1, A1); number of pulses per second = 1 ÷ time between pulses.
- The time from the top to the bottom of a wave is half a period.
- The direction of an oscillation reverses twice in every cycle.
- The smallest wavelength goes with the highest frequency: λ = v/f using the highest frequency (C1); answer with its unit (A1).
- Time for a wave to travel a distance: v = fλ, then t = distance ÷ speed (C1, A1).
- When a wave slows down (in shallower water, or on entering another medium) the wavelength decreases (B1), because the frequency stays the same and v = fλ – v is proportional to λ – or because each complete wave travels a shorter distance before the next arrives (B1).
- The frequency is unchanged in the new region, so λ = v/f with the new speed.
- Longitudinal wave diagram: measure one wavelength between identical points (for example centre of one compression to the centre of the next) (B1); v = fλ with the wavelength in metres (C1); answer (A1).
- Height–time graph for one point on a wave: approximately sinusoidal with the correct amplitude measured from the undisturbed level (B1); one complete wave takes the period T = 1/f (B1).
* Speed = wavelength ÷ period = fλ: the wavelength comes from a displacement–distance graph and the period from a displacement–time graph.
* The frequency is the same on both sides of a boundary: f = v ÷ λ in the first region, then new speed = f × new wavelength (speed is proportional to wavelength).
* Twice the amplitude means twice the height; half the frequency at the same speed means twice the wavelength.
* Count the number of wavelengths between two marked crests before dividing.
* For electromagnetic waves f = v ÷ λ with v = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s and the wavelength in metres.
== E | The ripple tank; water waves in deep and shallow water | ripple|water wave|shallow|deep|dipper|refract|reflect|depth | 5
- Ripple tank: a tank containing water (B1); a dipper or vibrator (a bar or a point) touching the surface (B1); a light source with a screen for the projected image, or a stroboscope or video, to observe the waves (B1); the dipper moves up and down to create the wave (B1).
- Making a straight wave: move (vibrate) the bar up and down in the water continuously (1), regularly – at a constant frequency (1). "Shake the bar" and "the bar hits / disturbs the water" are accepted; "put the bar in the water", "move the support" and "stretch and release the bands" are ignored.
- To increase the frequency: move the bar up and down more often per second (stronger, shorter, tighter or more rubber bands; a lighter bar).
- Frequency increased in the same depth of water: the speed does not change (B1); the wavelength decreases (B1). A smaller wavelength is obtained by increasing the frequency of the bar.
- Shallower water: the speed decreases (B1) and the wavelength decreases; the frequency stays constant (B1). From shallow into deep water the wavelength increases, the frequency stays the same and the speed increases.
- To show refraction in a ripple tank: put a piece of plastic or a glass plate in the tank to make a shallower region.
* Water waves refract because their speed changes when the depth changes; the frequency is unchanged and only the speed and wavelength change.
* Reflection does not change the speed, the wavelength or the frequency of a wave.
* Where the wavefronts are further apart the wavelength is larger and the wave is travelling faster.
== R | Drawing reflected and refracted wavefronts | wavefronts? (drawn|in the shallow|passing into)|reflected wavefronts|refracted wavefronts|reflection of water waves at|reflection at a plane barrier|refraction of (water waves|wavefronts) (entering|into)|refraction into shallow|drawing refraction|meeting a barrier|reflected from a flat barrier | 1
- Reflection at a plane barrier: at least three reflected wavefronts (B1); drawn at the correct angle – the same angle to the barrier as the incident wavefronts but sloping the other way, angle of reflection = angle of incidence (M1 or B1); with wavelengths equal to each other and to the incident wavelength (A1); joined to the incident wavefronts at the barrier (B1).
- Refraction into shallow water, where the wave is slower: at least three wavefronts in the shallow region, straight and parallel to each other (M1 or B1); a smaller, constant wavelength (B1); turned so that they make a smaller angle with the boundary – the direction of travel bends towards the normal (A1 or B1); joined to the incident wavefronts at the boundary, with no extra wavefronts (B1).
- Refraction into deep water, where the wave is faster: a larger, constant wavelength (B1); wavefronts turned so that the direction of travel bends away from the normal (B1); joined at the boundary (B1).
- Wavefronts parallel to the boundary: no change in direction, but the wavelength still changes (smaller and constant in the shallow water).
- A full drawing shows crests parallel to the bar reaching a labelled boundary (B1), the correct change of direction (B1), and a different wavelength before and after the boundary that is constant within each region (B1).
* Plane waves that meet a barrier at an angle and cross the incoming waves on the same side of the barrier are being reflected.
* Circular water waves reflected from a flat barrier appear to come from a point as far behind the barrier as the source is in front of it.
* From deep to shallow water the wavefronts become closer together and the direction of travel bends towards the normal.
== F | Diffraction | diffract|\bgap\b|round the hill|bend round | 0
- Diffraction: the spreading out (bending) of a wave at an edge or round an obstacle, or its spreading out after passing through a (small) gap or hole.
- Demonstration in a ripple tank: use a barrier with a slit, two obstacles with a gap between them, or the edge of a barrier in the water; the waves bend round the obstacle. The edge, gap or slit must be mentioned; "create a hole", "have a gap" and using the original bar as the obstacle are ignored.
- Wavefronts after a gap: at least three curved wavefronts centred on the gap (B1); the same wavelength as before the gap, curving the correct way (B1). For a gap smaller than the wavelength the crests are semicircles centred on the gap.
- At the edge of an obstacle: the straight wavefronts continue past the block with the same wavelength (B1); they curve into the region behind the block as quarter circles centred on the corner (B1).
- Errors that lose the mark in a diffraction drawing: a changed wavelength (it must stay the same); crests in the "shadow" region not curved; central lines drawn too straight.
- Long wavelengths bend round a hill and short wavelengths do not bend as much (B1), because of diffraction (B1); a decrease in wavelength gives less diffraction.
- Sound heard round a doorway: the wavefronts spread through the gap – diffraction.
* Waves spread out after passing through a gap such as an open doorway, so sound reaches places that are not in a straight line from the source.
