HOW TO READ THE CHECKLISTS

  • Every line that starts with [ ] is one point to learn and tick off.
  • PAPER 2 MARKING POINTS are the answers that the mark schemes for the structured paper give
    credit for. The same point asked in several papers is written once, in general wording.
    Alternatives that the schemes accept, and answers they ignore or reject, are kept with the
    point.
  • PAPER 1 MULTIPLE-CHOICE POINTS are short explanations of the ideas that lead to the correct
    option in the multiple-choice paper. A multiple-choice mark scheme gives only the letter of the
    answer, so these lines are explanations written for these notes and are not official marking
    points.
  • Numbers inside a point are examples of the method; in an examination use the data of the
    question.

MARK CODES USED IN THE PAPER 2 POINTS

  • (B1) an independent mark: given for that statement or value on its own.
  • (C1) a compensation mark for a correct step of the method (formula, substitution or intermediate
    value). It is also given automatically when the final answer is correct, so working protects
    marks when the answer is wrong.
  • (M1) a method mark: the step must be seen. The (A1) that follows it cannot be given unless the
    M1 is earned.
  • (A1) an answer mark: the correct final answer or the final part of an explanation, usually with
    its unit.
  • (1) one mark, in schemes that print no letter code.
  • Codes joined by "or", such as (B1 or M1), show that different schemes used different codes for
    the same point.
  • "any two", "two of", "three points": only that number of the listed points is needed for full
    marks.
  • ecf: error carried forward – a wrong earlier answer used correctly in a later step can still
    earn the later marks.
  • "accepted", "ignored", "not accepted / rejected": ignored answers neither gain nor lose the
    mark; rejected answers lose it.

1.1 PHYSICAL QUANTITIES AND MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUES

MEASURING LENGTH: RULE, TAPE, CALIPERS AND MICROMETER

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A length of several metres measured in one measurement: tape (measure).
  [ ] A very small thickness measured accurately: micrometer (screw gauge) or calipers.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Choose the instrument to suit the size: a tape for many metres and for curved lengths such as
      a circumference; a (metre) rule for tens of centimetres up to about a metre; vernier calipers
      for a few centimetres, including the internal diameter of a pipe (inside jaws); a micrometer
      for a few millimetres or less (the diameter of a thin wire, the thickness of one sheet of
      paper).
  [ ] Vernier reading = main-scale reading just before the vernier zero + the vernier division that
      lines up with a main-scale mark.
  [ ] Vernier calipers read to 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm, so a reading in centimetres is quoted to two
      decimal places.
  [ ] The zero error of vernier calipers is read in the same way with the jaws closed.
  [ ] Micrometer reading = sleeve reading to the last visible mark (including the half-millimetre
      mark) + thimble reading (number of divisions × 0.01 mm).
  [ ] For an accurate micrometer measurement, add or subtract the zero error.
  [ ] A ruler whose zero mark is not at its end: true length = scale reading + distance from the end
      to the zero mark.
  [ ] A length found from two scale readings is the difference between them; for a diameter held
      between two blocks, use the inner faces of the blocks.
  [ ] Measure the distance for several revolutions and divide by the number of revolutions to find
      one circumference.

MEASURING VOLUME AND MASS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Weighing a heavy container is not an accurate way of finding the mass of light contents: the
      contents are a small fraction of the total mass.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Measuring cylinder: find the value of one small division, then read the level of the bottom of
      the meniscus.

MEASURING TIME: STOPWATCHES AND THE PERIOD OF A PENDULUM

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Accurate time for one swing of a pendulum: use a stopwatch or electronic timer (B1); time at
      least 5 swings and divide by the number of swings (B1).
  [ ] One complete swing is there and back (from one end to the other and back again); use a fixed
      reference mark.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The most accurate method times a larger number of complete oscillations (for example 10) with
      a digital timer and divides by that number.
  [ ] Reading an analogue stopwatch: work out the value of one small division first.
  [ ] A digital stopwatch reads minutes : seconds : hundredths of a second; convert to seconds
      before subtracting two readings.

UNITS, PREFIXES AND ESTIMATES

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Prefixes: mega (M) = 10⁶; kilo (k) = 10³; milli (m) = 10⁻³.
  [ ] Capital letters matter: MW is megawatt, mW is milliwatt; ms means millisecond.
  [ ] 1 mm = 0.1 cm = one-thousandth of a metre; one tenth of a millimetre = 0.01 cm.
  [ ] Units: frequency Hz; acceleration m/s²; energy J; power W; e.m.f. V; weight N.
  [ ] A sensible estimate of the mass of an adult is about 75 kg.

SCALARS AND VECTORS: MEANING AND EXAMPLES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A vector quantity has magnitude (size) and direction.
  [ ] A scalar quantity has magnitude only; it has no direction.
  [ ] Stating the difference (1 mark): a vector has a direction; a scalar does not.
  [ ] Vectors credited: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum.
  [ ] Scalars credited: distance, length, speed, time, mass, energy, temperature, current,
      resistance.
  [ ] Displacement involves direction (it is a vector); distance has size only (it is a scalar).
  [ ] Speed is a scalar because it has no direction (magnitude only).
  [ ] A journey that ends where it started (for example, one that returns along the same path) has
      no resultant displacement, although the distance travelled is not zero.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Further vectors: gravitational field strength. Further scalars: volume, area, density, work,
      power.
  [ ] A value given with a direction ("15 m/s due north", "600 km due East") is a vector; an average
      speed round a track or a distance travelled is a scalar.
  [ ] Speed is the magnitude of velocity.
  [ ] Weight is a force, so it is a vector; mass is a scalar.
  [ ] Displacement is the straight-line distance, with its direction, from the starting point: 3.0 m
      north then 4.0 m east is a displacement of √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 m.
  [ ] The total distance travelled is the sum of the path lengths, even when the total displacement
      is zero.
  [ ] The definition velocity = displacement ÷ time contains two vectors (velocity and displacement)
      and one scalar (time).

ADDING VECTORS: RESULTANTS AND VECTOR DIAGRAMS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Adding two vectors by drawing: draw both vectors with the correct directions and sizes so that
      they meet at a point (head-to-tail, or from a common point) (B1); complete the triangle or
      parallelogram with all three vectors in the correct directions and the resultant clearly shown
      and labelled (B1).
  [ ] Scale drawing, first mark: a scale is stated, with units (for example cm to N). Schemes have
      required a scale of at least 1.0 cm to 10 kN, or at least 1 cm to 1 m/s.
  [ ] Scale drawing, second mark: the two vectors are drawn at the correct angle with lengths in
      proportion, and the correct triangle or parallelogram (rectangle) is completed with its
      diagonal as the resultant.
  [ ] Scale drawing, third mark: the size of the resultant is inside the tolerance of the scheme
      (typically within a few per cent).
  [ ] Scale drawing, fourth mark (when a direction is asked for): the direction is given as an angle
      inside the tolerance, usually ±3°, measured from a stated reference direction (from north,
      from the horizontal, from the vertical or from one of the forces).
  [ ] Arrows are needed on all three vectors when the scheme asks for the directions to be shown.
  [ ] For two perpendicular vectors, Pythagoras (for example v² = 6.8² + 2.4²) is accepted in place
      of the scale drawing for the size, and tan θ for the angle.
  [ ] Size of a displacement from a scale diagram or map: measure the straight line from start to
      finish and multiply by the scale, or use Pythagoras with the two perpendicular components (C1
      or B1); answer (A1 or B1).
  [ ] Direction of a displacement: measure the angle from North with a protractor, or use tan θ; an
      angle measured clockwise from North (a bearing) is accepted.
  [ ] Find the scale of a diagram from a line of known length (for example 1 cm represents 5 m).
  [ ] Force arrows: weight is drawn as a vertical arrow downwards from the object; a tension is
      drawn along the chain or rope, pointing away from the object; a push up a slope is drawn along
      the line of the slope.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Vectors along one line: add those in the same direction and subtract those in the opposite
      direction (11 − 3 − 2 = 6 N).
  [ ] The resultant of two forces lies between their difference and their sum (30 N and 50 N give
      between 20 N and 80 N); it is largest when the forces act in the same direction.
  [ ] For angles from 0° to 90° the resultant of 3.0 N and 4.0 N lies between 7.0 N and 5.0 N; a
      smaller resultant needs an obtuse angle between the forces.
  [ ] Two perpendicular forces: resultant = √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 N.
  [ ] Two equal forces at right angles have a resultant at 45° to each of them.
  [ ] Parallelogram (rectangle) rule: draw both vectors from the same point; the resultant is the
      diagonal that starts at that point, with its arrow pointing away from the point. It is not the
      line joining the two arrow tips.
  [ ] Head-to-tail rule: the resultant runs from the start of the first vector to the end of the
      second, with its arrow pointing towards the end of the second.
  [ ] The resultant lies between the directions of the two forces, nearer to the larger force.
  [ ] A wind "from the west" blows towards the east: draw each velocity in the direction of
      movement.
  [ ] Three forces in equilibrium form a closed triangle with the arrows following one another head
      to tail; each side is parallel to its force.
  [ ] The force that keeps an object stationary, or moving at constant velocity, is equal in size
      and opposite in direction to the resultant of the other forces.

SPEED AND VELOCITY; AVERAGE SPEED

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Speed: distance travelled per unit time (speed = distance ÷ time).
  [ ] Velocity: change in displacement per unit time (rate of change of displacement); marked as
      change of displacement (M1), per unit time (A1).
  [ ] Accepted alternative for velocity: distance moved per unit time (speed) (M1 or C1) in a stated
      / specified direction (A1).
  [ ] Difference between speed and velocity (1 mark): velocity has a direction / is a vector /
      depends on displacement rather than distance; speed has no direction / is a scalar.
  [ ] Velocity changes at constant speed when the direction changes.
  [ ] A negative velocity is in the opposite direction to a positive velocity.
  [ ] When the direction of motion reverses, the change in velocity is the sum of the two speeds.
  [ ] Average speed = total distance ÷ total time, in any form (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Total distance travelled = sum of the lengths of the parts of the path.
  [ ] Include any rest during the journey in the total time; use the time up to arrival and do not
      include time spent stationary afterwards.
  [ ] The magnitude of the average velocity is smaller than the average speed when the displacement
      is smaller than the distance travelled along the path in the same time.
  [ ] Distance travelled at constant speed = speed × time, with the time interval read from the
      graph where necessary (C1, A1).
  [ ] Speed round a circular path or orbit = circumference ÷ time for one revolution, v = 2πr ÷ T,
      with the time in seconds (C1 for speed = distance ÷ time, C1 for the substitution, A1).
  [ ] A point that moves round a circle of smaller circumference in the same rotation time has a
      lower speed (smaller circumference B1; same time for one rotation B1).
  [ ] To compare two orbital speeds, work out circumference ÷ time for one orbit for each (C1) and
      compare the two values (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Average speed uses the distance along the path; velocity needs the displacement (straight-line
      distance and direction), which is not known for a winding path.
  [ ] Average velocity = displacement (straight line from start to finish) ÷ total time, including
      rests.
  [ ] Average speed is not the mean of the separate speeds: (100 + 20) km in (1 + ½) h gives
      80 km/h.
  [ ] Change minutes to hours (or to seconds) so that the units of distance and time agree.

MEASURING SPEED: TIMING EXPERIMENTS AND TICKER TAPE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Measuring average speed: measure the distance between two marks with a metre rule / tape
      measure (B1); measure the time taken to travel between the marks with a stopwatch / stopclock
      / timer (B1); average speed = distance ÷ time (B1).
  [ ] Showing that a speed is constant: measure at least two distances and the corresponding times
      (C1); method, for example mark the position every second and measure the distances between
      marks, or use a video with a scale, or put marks every metre and time the object past each
      (A1); the speed is constant if the same distance is covered in each equal time interval –
      distance ÷ time is constant / the distance–time graph has a constant slope (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Ticker tape: the dots are made at equal time intervals; dots far apart mean the tape is moving
      quickly, dots close together mean slowly.
  [ ] Count the spaces, not the dots: 11 dots mark 10 intervals.

ACCELERATION: DEFINITION AND CALCULATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken; a = (v − u) ÷ t in any form, algebraic or
      numerical (C1).
  [ ] Definition marks: change (increase) of velocity (B1); per unit time / rate of change of
      velocity (B1).
  [ ] Unit of acceleration: m/s² (a separate B1 in some questions; N/kg is ignored as a unit of
      acceleration).
  [ ] Final answer: 2 significant figures, correctly rounded (2.95 is accepted for 3.0, 2.9 is not);
      a negative sign is accepted for a deceleration; an error is carried forward from a wrongly
      drawn graph.
  [ ] Uniform acceleration: the same change in speed / velocity (M1 or C1) in the same time / in
      each second / per unit time (A1).
  [ ] Also accepted for uniform acceleration: the rate of change of velocity is constant; speed
      increasing at a constant rate; a straight line on a speed–time graph; any named time period in
      place of "unit time".
  [ ] Recent mark schemes ignore the formula alone, "speed increases steadily" and "accelerates at a
      constant rate" as explanations of uniform acceleration.
  [ ] Deceleration (retardation) is a negative acceleration: a decrease in speed.
  [ ] Uniform deceleration: a decrease in speed (C1) by equal amounts in equal times (A1).
  [ ] A deceleration is larger than an acceleration when the same change in speed takes place in a
      shorter time.
  [ ] Average deceleration = (v − u) ÷ t; where three marks are given they are formula (C1),
      substitution (C1), answer (A1).
  [ ] From a speed–time or velocity–time graph: acceleration = gradient of the straight section;
      read two speeds from it (C1) with the two corresponding times and use a = Δv ÷ t (C1); answer
      (A1).
  [ ] Change in speed = acceleration × time (C1); final speed = initial speed + change in speed, or
      initial speed = final speed − change in speed (A1). From rest, v = at.
  [ ] Positions recorded at equal time intervals show acceleration when the object travels further
      in each successive interval; an object that is slowing down covers less distance in each
      successive interval.
  [ ] An object moving at constant speed is accelerating if it changes direction (goes round a
      corner), because its velocity changes: velocity depends on direction.
  [ ] When the gradient of a velocity–time graph for a falling object is about 10 m/s², comment that
      this is the acceleration of free fall, because the only force acting is the weight.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Constant acceleration means equal changes of speed in equal times; speed changes of 1.0, 2.0
      and 3.0 m/s in successive seconds are not a constant acceleration.
  [ ] Equal distances in equal times mean constant speed; distances per interval that increase by
      unequal amounts mean non-uniform acceleration.
  [ ] When a body accelerates its velocity must change (the speed need not change if only the
      direction changes).
  [ ] A car that speeds up from rest and then travels at constant speed has a changing velocity and
      a changing acceleration (the acceleration falls to zero).
  [ ] Change the time to seconds before working out an acceleration in m/s².

DISTANCE–TIME GRAPHS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Speed = gradient of the distance–time graph.
  [ ] Constant speed (zero acceleration): a straight line of constant, positive gradient (B1),
      because equal distances are travelled in equal times (B1).
  [ ] A greater speed is shown by a steeper slope (larger gradient): a larger distance in the same
      time.
  [ ] At rest: a horizontal section; its duration is read from the time axis.
  [ ] Accelerating: the line curves with increasing gradient. Decelerating: the gradient decreases.
  [ ] A curve of increasing gradient that becomes a straight line: accelerating (B1), with
      decreasing acceleration until a constant speed is reached (B1).
  [ ] Speed at an instant on a curved graph: draw a tangent at that time (B1); find the gradient of
      the tangent (B1 or C1); value inside the accepted range (A1).
  [ ] Speed on a straight section (maximum speed, terminal velocity): take two pairs of coordinates
      from the straight section (C1); change in distance ÷ change in time (C1); answer inside the
      accepted range (A1).
  [ ] Plotting from a table: points correct to within half a small square (B1); a smooth curve for
      an accelerating object, not point-to-point straight lines (B1).
  [ ] Sketching: axes labelled distance against time (B1); starts at the origin with zero initial
      gradient, gradient increasing while accelerating (B1); a straight line of positive gradient at
      constant speed (B1); gradient decreasing but never negative while slowing down (B1);
      horizontal where the object is at rest (B1).
  [ ] Constant speed followed by rest: a straight line of positive slope followed by a horizontal
      line.
  [ ] Total distance: read the graph at the arrival time.
  [ ] Distance between two objects at a given time: the difference between their two distance
      readings at that time.
  [ ] Difference in finishing times: the difference between the times at which each line reaches the
      finishing distance.
  [ ] Comparing an accelerating part with a decelerating part: compare the magnitudes (a longer time
      for the same change of speed means a smaller acceleration), or say that one starts from rest
      and the other finishes at rest.
  [ ] Height–time graph of a light falling object: its speed increases at first (it accelerates),
      then becomes constant (zero acceleration).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Uniform acceleration from rest gives a curve of increasing gradient (the speed–time graph is
      then a straight line through the origin).
  [ ] If constant speed follows the acceleration, the curve becomes a straight sloping line; it does
      not level off.
  [ ] A gradient that decreases to zero shows an object decelerating to rest.
  [ ] The fastest constant speed is the steepest straight section.
  [ ] Two constant speeds: two straight lines, the second less steep for the lower speed; the
      distance keeps increasing.
  [ ] A straight-line distance–time graph means constant speed: zero acceleration, zero resultant
      force and a constant kinetic energy greater than zero.
  [ ] On a graph of distance from the start against time, a falling line means moving back towards
      the start.

SPEED–TIME AND VELOCITY–TIME GRAPHS: SHAPES AND WHAT THEY SHOW

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Constant speed: a horizontal line; the acceleration is zero where the graph is horizontal
      (gradient zero).
  [ ] Uniform acceleration: a straight line of constant positive gradient. Uniform deceleration: a
      straight line sloping downwards.
  [ ] Non-uniform acceleration or deceleration: the gradient is not constant – the graph is a curve.
  [ ] A larger acceleration: a steeper gradient, or a larger change in speed in the same time.
  [ ] Decreasing acceleration: the gradient decreases (the increase in speed in the same time is
      less at later times); when the graph becomes level the acceleration has decreased to zero.
  [ ] A gradient that increases and then becomes constant: the acceleration increases (B1) to a
      constant value (B1).
  [ ] Negative acceleration (deceleration, retardation) is shown by a negative gradient: the line
      slopes downwards.
  [ ] Reading values: a constant speed from the horizontal section; the speed at a given time; the
      time at constant speed as the difference between two time readings; the driver's reaction time
      as the time before the speed starts to fall.
  [ ] Describing motion from a graph: describe each stage with its values (for example uniform
      acceleration for the first 6 s, then constant speed / zero acceleration; stationary where the
      speed is zero).
  [ ] Sketch or plot marks: axes labelled speed and time the correct way round, with units when
      asked (B1); a straight line from the origin for uniform acceleration from rest (B1); a
      horizontal line for constant speed between the stated times (B1); a straight line down to zero
      for uniform deceleration (B1); final values marked (B1).
  [ ] Plotted points must be within half a small square; a wrongly plotted value is penalised once
      only (error carried forward from a misplotted point); labels on the lines are not needed
      unless asked for.
  [ ] Acceleration that falls gradually to zero (constant driving force with growing resistance; an
      accelerating force that falls to zero): a line from the origin with gradually decreasing
      gradient (B1), becoming horizontal at the final speed (B1).
  [ ] A constant resultant force that suddenly becomes zero: a straight line of positive gradient up
      to that time (B1), then a horizontal line (B1).
  [ ] Falling skydiver: a curve from the origin with decreasing gradient up to the terminal velocity
      (B1); a horizontal line at the terminal velocity (B1); the speed drops to the lower value when
      the parachute opens (B1); a horizontal line at the lower speed until landing (B1).
  [ ] A sudden fall in the speed of a parachutist: the parachute opens. The speed dropping to zero
      at the end: landing.
  [ ] Speed falling suddenly to zero, or an almost vertical section of a velocity–time graph: the
      object hits the ground – a very short time for the deceleration, so a large force.
  [ ] Emergency stop: axes labelled with quantity and unit (B1); a horizontal line at the initial
      speed during the reaction time (B1); then a straight line down to zero (B1).
  [ ] A lower initial speed with the same reaction time and the same deceleration: a horizontal line
      at the new speed for the same time (B1), then a braking line parallel to the original,
      reaching zero sooner (B1). A second vehicle with the same deceleration has a braking line
      parallel to the first.
  [ ] Slowing under a resistive force that depends on speed: the deceleration decreases as the speed
      falls (B1) because the resistive force decreases (B1); the sketch falls from the initial speed
      to zero with a gradient of decreasing magnitude (B1, B1).
  [ ] A steady speed that covers the same distance in the same time is a horizontal line at the
      average speed (total distance ÷ total time).
  [ ] A repeating graph (drive switched on and off): the object speeds up and slows down (B1); it
      reaches a steady or maximum speed, or slows down but never to rest, or the cycle repeats
      continuously (B1). With longer on and off periods the same top speed is held for a longer
      time, or the speed drops lower / the object comes to rest during the off period (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] An object is at rest where the graph reads zero speed, not at the peak; the greatest speed is
      the highest point on the graph.
  [ ] At the top of a curve (maximum speed) the gradient, and so the acceleration, is zero.
  [ ] The largest acceleration is the steepest upward section; a downward slope is a deceleration.
  [ ] A short stop: the speed falls to zero, stays at zero for a time, then rises again.
  [ ] The magnitude of the velocity is decreasing wherever a velocity–time graph is moving towards
      the time axis, from positive values or from negative values.
  [ ] Rolling down a slope and then along rough level ground: the speed rises steadily and then
      falls gradually (it neither stays constant nor drops at once to zero).
  [ ] Two horizontal lines are two constant speeds; the higher line is the faster object.
  [ ] Freewheeling with no friction: the speed is greatest at the bottom of a hill, where the
      gravitational potential energy is least.
  [ ] Match each section of a journey to the times on the axis.

DISTANCE FROM A SPEED–TIME GRAPH: THE AREA UNDER THE LINE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Distance travelled = area under the speed–time graph (between the line and the time axis).
  [ ] Convert the area to a distance using the scales of the axes (or count squares and convert).
  [ ] Use the area of a triangle (½ × base × height), a rectangle (speed × time) or a trapezium (½ ×
      sum of the parallel sides × height); average speed × time and ½(u + v)t are accepted.
  [ ] Typical marks: area idea or formula (C1 or B1); one section worked out correctly (C1); total
      distance (A1).
  [ ] Use the time in seconds when the speed is in m/s.
  [ ] "Show by calculation": the working must be shown and the final value calculated; at most 1
      mark if no working is shown or the distance has a wrong unit such as m² or m/s.
  [ ] Braking distance = area under the graph during braking = ½ × braking time × initial speed.
  [ ] A vehicle travels further while braking when the area under its graph is greater (higher
      initial / average speed).
  [ ] Height gained by an object thrown upwards = area between the line and the time axis up to the
      time at which the velocity is zero.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The distance travelled at constant speed is the area under the horizontal section only.
  [ ] For uniform acceleration from rest the average speed is half the final speed.

FREE FALL

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Acceleration of free fall = 9.8 m/s² (older schemes accepted 9.8–10 m/s²); it is the initial
      acceleration of an object falling from rest.
  [ ] Speed after falling from rest for a time t with constant acceleration: v = at (C1, A1).
  [ ] Speed–time graph: free fall is a straight line from the origin; when air resistance becomes
      important the gradient then decreases (but is never negative).
  [ ] Object thrown upwards with no air resistance: the velocity–time graph is one straight line
      that continues with the same gradient through zero to an equal negative velocity when it is
      caught.
  [ ] The highest point is reached when the velocity is zero (where the line crosses the time axis).
  [ ] The same throw on the Earth instead of on the Moon (any two): the graph is steeper (larger
      deceleration); the highest point is reached sooner and the object is caught sooner; the graph
      curves because of air resistance.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] With no air resistance all objects fall with the same acceleration whatever their mass, so
      objects released together from the same height land together.
  [ ] In a vacuum the acceleration is g at all times: a straight-line speed–time graph through the
      origin, and positions at equal time intervals that get steadily further apart.
  [ ] In air the air resistance grows as the speed rises, so the acceleration decreases.
  [ ] A heavy ball falling a short distance has negligible air resistance: a straight speed–time
      line through the origin.
  [ ] A dropped object that hits the ground without rebounding: a straight line rising from the
      origin, then a sudden vertical drop to zero speed.

1.3 MASS AND WEIGHT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MASS AND WEIGHT: MEANINGS AND DIFFERENCES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Mass: the amount (quantity) of matter (substance) in a body (B1).
  [ ] Mass is also credited as the property of a body that resists a change in its state of rest or
      motion – a measure of its inertia (the second B1 where two marks are given; an accepted
      alternative where one mark is given).
  [ ] Weight: the force (pull) of gravity on a body; marked as a force on a body (B1) due to a
      gravitational field (B1).
  [ ] An object has weight because it is in the gravitational field of the Earth; a mass hung on a
      spring exerts a force on it because of its weight.
  [ ] Gravitational field: a region (of space) in which a mass experiences a force (due to
      gravitational attraction).
  [ ] Ways in which weight differs from mass (any two, other than the units): weight is a force;
      weight is a vector / has a direction, mass is a scalar; weight depends on location /
      gravitational field strength, mass does not; mass is the amount of matter; mass resists a
      change of motion (inertia); weight is measured with a spring balance / newton meter, not a
      beam balance.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Mass is a scalar measured in kilograms; weight is a force measured in newtons.
  [ ] A greater mass means more matter and a greater resistance to a change in motion.
  [ ] Weight acts vertically downwards, whatever the slope of the surface.
  [ ] Mass is not changed by taking the object to the Moon, by heating it, or by a force that
      changes its shape and size. On the Moon the volume and density are also unchanged; only the
      weight changes.
  [ ] The weight of a spacecraft is zero at the point where the gravitational fields of the Earth
      and the Moon cancel.

WEIGHT = MASS × GRAVITATIONAL FIELD STRENGTH (W = mg)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Weight = mass × gravitational field strength, W = mg (C1 for the equation or substitution and
      A1 for the answer; often a single B1).
  [ ] Use the mass in kilograms.
  [ ] Use g = 9.8 N/kg, the value in the current syllabus; older papers used 10 N/kg where the
      question gave that value. For 2.0 kg the answer is 19.6 N, and 20 N is accepted.
  [ ] Mass from a weight or a newton-meter reading: m = W ÷ g.
  [ ] Weight on another planet: find the mass first from m = W ÷ g (C1) – the mass is the same on
      every planet – then weight = mass × the local g (A1).
  [ ] Where the gravitational field strength is less, the mass is unchanged and the weight is less.
  [ ] With no air resistance the acceleration of free fall is numerically equal to the gravitational
      field strength; give it in m/s², not N/kg.
  [ ] Why objects of different mass fall with the same acceleration (no air resistance): weight is
      directly proportional to mass, W = mg (B1); a larger force acts on the more massive body, and
      a = F/m = W/m (B1); so the mass cancels and a = g for both (B1).
  [ ] A mass hanging at rest: the upward force (tension) is the same size as the weight, because the
      mass is at rest.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Gravitational field strength g = W ÷ m, in N/kg; to compare planets or objects work out W ÷ m
      (or mg) for each.
  [ ] On the Moon g is about one sixth of its value on the Earth.
  [ ] Weight decreases as height above the Earth increases, because the gravitational field strength
      is smaller; the mass is unchanged.
  [ ] Mass = weight ÷ g, not weight × g.
  [ ] Weight (mg) and resultant force (ma) are different quantities: for 80 kg accelerating at
      2 m/s² the weight is 800 N and the resultant force is 160 N.

MEASURING AND COMPARING MASS AND WEIGHT: BALANCES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Mass is measured with a balance / scales.
  [ ] A lever-arm (beam) balance compares the unknown mass with known masses (B1), so it gives the
      correct mass anywhere (B1).
  [ ] A spring balance measures weight (the force of gravity) and so depends on g (B1); a spring
      balance calibrated on the Earth reads wrongly where weights are different, for example on Mars
      (B1).
  [ ] Finding mass with a spring balance marked in newtons: hang the object from the balance and
      read its weight (B1); divide the weight by g (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A newton meter (force meter) measures weight, so its reading is different on the Moon; a pan
      balance compares masses, so its reading is the same on the Earth and on the Moon.
  [ ] Spring balances and top-pan balances respond to weight, so their mass scale is wrong where g
      is different; lever-arm and two-pan balances read mass correctly anywhere.
  [ ] On a balanced equal-arm beam balance the two objects have equal masses, equal weights and
      equal moments; their volumes need not be equal.
  [ ] Mass is not measured in newtons, nor weight in kilograms.
  [ ] Bathroom scales give the mass in kg; the weight is then mg.
  [ ] A balance that reads the force as an Earth mass shows one fifth of the true mass where g is
      one fifth as large.

1.4 DENSITY
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DENSITY: MEANING, UNITS AND WHAT CHANGES IT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Density = mass ÷ volume (mass per unit volume).
  [ ] Besides the mass, the volume is needed to find a density.
  [ ] Heating a solid: the particles move further apart (B1), so the volume increases and the
      density decreases while the mass is unchanged (B1).
  [ ] Samples of the same material have the same density (mass is proportional to volume); the
      sample made of a different material is the one whose mass ÷ volume differs from the others.
  [ ] Advantage of a low-density material (for example for a bicycle): lower mass / weight (B1); a
      consequence (M1) with its reason (A1), such as greater acceleration for the same force
      (F = ma), less work needed to go uphill, or less kinetic energy and so a shorter stopping
      distance.
  [ ] Units: g/cm³ or kg/m³; an answer may be given in either (0.87 g/cm³ = 870 kg/m³).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
  [ ] Density is a property of the material: objects of the same material have the same density
      whatever their shape, size or mass.
  [ ] For one material, mass against volume is a straight line through the origin; its gradient is
      the density.
  [ ] The same mass in half the volume has double the density; a fixed mass of gas squeezed into
      half the volume has twice the density.
  [ ] A cube with sides twice as long has 2³ = 8 times the volume: with the same mass its density is
      one eighth; made of the same material its mass and weight are 8 times as large.
  [ ] When a fixed mass of air is heated and expands, its mass stays the same and its density
      decreases.
  [ ] Equal masses of two liquids: the one with one third of the volume has three times the density.
  [ ] On a plot of mass against volume, the point with the smallest mass and the largest volume has
      the smallest density.

DENSITY CALCULATIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Use ρ = m ÷ V, m = ρV or V = m ÷ ρ in any form (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Volume of a rectangular block = length × breadth (width) × height (thickness); volume of a
      cube = side³ (C1 for the volume in three-mark questions).
  [ ] Volume of a column or pipe = cross-sectional area × length (height).
  [ ] Thickness of a thin sheet = volume ÷ (length × width).
  [ ] Volume of liquid lost from a tank = cross-sectional area × fall in level.
  [ ] Mass of liquid in a container: m = ρV (C1); add the mass of the container for the total (C1 or
      A1); then W = mg if the weight is asked for (A1).
  [ ] Mass of liquid = mass of the full container − mass of the empty container; then V = m ÷ ρ.
  [ ] Mass flowing in each second = density × volume flowing in each second.
  [ ] The same material with a different volume: find the density from the first pair of values,
      then mass = density × new volume (the mass is proportional to the volume).
  [ ] Density from a weight–volume graph: take two pairs of readings from the line, separated by at
      least half its length (B1); use the gradient, change in weight ÷ change in volume – not a
      single reading, because the line does not start at zero when the container has weight (C1);
      change weight to mass with W = mg, so density = gradient ÷ g (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] "Show that" questions: the equation in any form (C1); the unrounded value clearly seen (A1).
  [ ] Give the answer to 2 or 3 significant figures, to match the data.
  [ ] Two liquid columns that balance (equal masses above equal areas): density is inversely
      proportional to the height of the column.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Density of a liquid = (mass of beaker and liquid − mass of empty beaker) ÷ volume of liquid.
  [ ] Change the units first so that they agree (2.0 mm = 0.20 cm; 0.11 mm = 0.011 cm) before using
      volume = area × thickness.
  [ ] Overall density of two materials together = total mass ÷ total volume, not the average of the
      two densities.
  [ ] Number of identical small objects = total volume ÷ volume of one (volume of one = mass ÷
      density).
  [ ] Volume of an object in a closed box = volume of the box − volume of air left.
  [ ] To find which block has the greatest density, work out mass ÷ volume for each.
  [ ] Mixing liquids: add the masses (density × volume) of each liquid.

MEASURING DENSITY: REGULAR SOLIDS, LIQUIDS, AND IRREGULAR SOLIDS BY DISPLACEMENT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Regular solid: measure its mass (M1) using a (top-pan) balance (A1); measure its length,
      breadth and height (M1) using a ruler / calipers / micrometer (A1); volume = length × breadth
      × height (A1); density = mass ÷ volume (B1). Volume by displacement of water in a measuring
      cylinder is the accepted alternative.
  [ ] Volume of an irregular solid: read the volume of water alone in a measuring cylinder (B1 or
      C1); lower the object in until it is totally immersed and read the new level; volume of the
      object = new reading − original reading (B1 or A1).
  [ ] Accepted alternative: a displacement can filled to the spout; collect the overflow and measure
      it with a measuring cylinder.
  [ ] Density of an irregular solid of known mass (4 marks): record the volume of water in a
      measuring cylinder; lower the object in; record the new reading; subtract to find the volume;
      density = mass ÷ volume.
  [ ] An object that will not fit into an ordinary measuring cylinder (its volume is too large)
      cannot be measured this way.
  [ ] Density of a liquid: pour the liquid into a measuring cylinder and read the volume (B1); find
      the mass of the cylinder with the liquid on a balance (B1); subtract the mass of the empty
      cylinder, or zero the balance with the empty cylinder on it first (B1); density = mass of
      liquid ÷ volume (B1).
  [ ] Checking the density of water: mass of water = mass of container with water − mass of
      container without it (B1); volume from a measuring cylinder and density = mass ÷ volume (B1);
      a calibrated hydrometer is an accepted alternative.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Irregular solid: a balance for the mass and a measuring cylinder of water for the volume by
      displacement. Regular solid: a balance and a ruler. Liquid: a balance and a measuring
      cylinder.
  [ ] Find the mass of the object first, while it is dry; remove air bubbles before reading the new
      level.
  [ ] In calculations: mass = difference between two balance readings; volume = rise in the
      measuring-cylinder reading; then density = mass ÷ volume.
  [ ] For several identical objects measured together, use the total mass and the total volume.
  [ ] If the weight is given, find the mass from m = W ÷ g before dividing by the volume.

1.5.1 BALANCED AND UNBALANCED FORCES
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NAMING AND DRAWING FORCES; WHAT A FORCE CAN CHANGE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Forces on a moving train or car, with directions: weight (gravitational force) downwards;
      normal reaction (contact force from the track or road) upwards; driving force (thrust of the
      engine) forwards; air resistance (drag) and friction / braking force backwards.
  [ ] Besides changing its velocity, a force applied to a solid can change its shape (B1) and its
      size / volume / density (B1).
  [ ] Effects of a large force on a solid surface (any two of different kinds): it distorts /
      changes shape (stretches, bends, deforms); it is compressed / changes size, volume or density;
      its temperature rises.
  [ ] Force diagram of a standing animal carrying a rider: weight drawn downwards from the middle of
      the body and labelled (B1); upward contact forces from the ground on at least two legs,
      labelled (B1); downward force from the rider on the saddle area (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A force can change the shape, size (volume) and velocity (speed or direction) of a body, but
      not its mass.
  [ ] The normal contact force from a surface acts at right angles to the surface (not vertically on
      a slope, and not along it).
  [ ] Tension acts along a string; weight acts vertically downwards; a magnet exerts a magnetic
      force on steel.
  [ ] Rocket in flight: thrust acts forwards along the direction of travel, air resistance backwards
      along that line, weight vertically downwards.

NEWTON'S FIRST LAW: BALANCED FORCES, REST AND CONSTANT VELOCITY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Newton's first law, simple statement: an object remains at rest or keeps moving (in uniform
      motion) unless a force acts on it (C1). The word "force" is needed; "a force causes the motion
      / speed / velocity to change" is accepted; "remains at rest" alone and F = ma are ignored.
  [ ] Newton's first law, full statement: the object moves at constant velocity (constant speed in a
      straight line, zero acceleration) unless acted on by a resultant / net / unbalanced force; or,
      applied, constant velocity means there is no resultant force (A1). "Constant motion" and
      "external force" are ignored for this mark.
  [ ] With no resultant force: the speed is constant (B1) and the motion is in a straight line (B1).
  [ ] Balanced forces: equal and opposite forces / no resultant force.
  [ ] With balanced forces an object is either stationary or moving with constant velocity (constant
      speed and direction).
  [ ] Travelling at constant speed in a straight line: the resultant force is zero / the forces
      balance (B1); the forward (driving or pulling) force is cancelled by a backward force –
      friction, drag, air resistance or water resistance, which must be named (B1).
  [ ] Constant speed on a straight road: the forward and backward forces are equal in size and
      opposite in direction.
  [ ] Falling at constant velocity: the resistive (drag) force equals the weight (B1) and acts
      upwards (B1).
  [ ] Moving up at constant speed: the upward force (tension in the cable) equals the weight, mg.
  [ ] An object moving at constant speed has no resultant force and is not accelerating, so the
      force needed to move it does not change.
  [ ] A mass hanging at rest from a spring: the weight acts downwards along the vertical line
      through the mass (B1); the tension in the spring acts upwards along the same line (B1).
  [ ] An object at rest under three forces: the third force is equal in size to the resultant of the
      other two.
  [ ] An object at rest hanging from a spring and partly supported by water: weight = upward force
      from the spring + upward force from the water. As more of it is immersed (any three): the
      upward force from the water increases; the pressure at its base increases; the force from the
      spring decreases; the two upward forces always add up to the weight.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The resultant force is zero only for rest or for constant speed in a straight line; turning a
      corner, falling freely and speeding up all need a resultant force.
  [ ] Constant velocity means no resultant force in any direction: the force opposing the motion
      equals the driving force, and at constant height the vertical forces are equal too. The weight
      is not involved in the horizontal balance.
  [ ] Balanced forces on a moving body: neither its speed nor its direction changes.
  [ ] Free-body diagram of an object at rest: weight downwards and contact force upwards, with
      arrows of equal length.
  [ ] A stationary object: the upward forces equal the downward forces (for example F + T = W).
  [ ] A train at constant speed whose coupling breaks: the engine now has a forward resultant force
      and speeds up; the truck has only the resistive force and slows down gradually.

INERTIA

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A passenger jerks forwards when the brakes are applied: mass is mentioned (B1); mass resists a
      change in its state of motion – inertia – so the passenger tends to continue moving (B1).
  [ ] A loaded vehicle is harder to stop: greater mass (greater momentum) (B1); more inertia, so a
      larger force is needed for the same deceleration, or there is a smaller deceleration for the
      same force (B1). Accepted alternative: greater kinetic energy (B1), so more work must be done
      to stop it in the same distance (B1).
  [ ] Braking car, driver slows but a loose bag slides forwards (any three): a backward force acts
      on the driver / car; it is provided by the seat belt or friction with the seat (friction
      between the tyres and the road for the car); there is little or no backward force (friction)
      on the bag; the bag resists a change in its motion – it carries on in a straight line at
      constant velocity.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Inertia: the property of an object that resists a change in its state of rest or motion; it is
      determined by the mass.

RESULTANT FORCE AND ACCELERATION: THE IDEAS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Accelerating: the forward (driving / thrust) force is larger than the backward force; a
      resultant force forwards is accepted.
  [ ] Decelerating: the backward force is larger than the forward force, or the resistive force acts
      alone; the backward force may be named as resistive force, force from the brakes, air
      resistance or resistance with the road.
  [ ] Recent mark schemes ignore "the forces are unbalanced", "a resultant force acts" and "the
      backward force increases" on their own.
  [ ] Three states: accelerating – unbalanced, forward force greater (B1); constant speed –
      balanced, forward force equals backward force (B1); decelerating – unbalanced, backward force
      greater (B1).
  [ ] On a force diagram of an accelerating object the forward arrow is drawn longer than the
      backward arrow.
  [ ] The forward force from the engine is greater than the resultant force because friction / air
      resistance acts on the car (B1) and opposes the force due to the engine (B1).
  [ ] A resultant force acts whenever the velocity changes: forwards (in the direction of movement)
      while speeding up (B1); backwards while slowing down (B1); while travelling round a curve at
      constant speed (B1), perpendicular to the motion, towards the centre of the curve (B1).
  [ ] At the lowest point of a bounce on an elastic cord the velocity is zero (changing direction)
      (B1); the weight acts downwards (B1); the stretched cord exerts an upward tension (B1); the
      upward force is greater than the weight, so the resultant force and the acceleration are
      upwards (B1).
  [ ] An air bag reduces the force by increasing the time taken to stop (B1), so the deceleration is
      smaller, F = ma (B1).
  [ ] Why the acceleration of a water rocket changes (any two): the mass of water and rocket
      decreases; the air pressure decreases, so there is less thrust; air resistance acts; the
      gravitational field strength decreases with height; less water is expelled per second, or at a
      slower speed.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The acceleration of a body is always in the direction of the resultant force on it.
  [ ] A resultant force that falls steadily to zero gives a decreasing acceleration: the speed–time
      graph rises with decreasing gradient and is then horizontal.
  [ ] Forces can balance in one direction but not in another: a car accelerating on a level road has
      balanced vertical forces while the driving force exceeds the air resistance; an aircraft
      gaining speed at constant height has lift = weight and thrust greater than air resistance.
  [ ] A falling apple has unbalanced forces (weight greater than air resistance), so it accelerates.
  [ ] All masses fall with the same acceleration because weight is proportional to mass: twice the
      mass has twice the force, so a = F ÷ m is the same.
  [ ] An object brought to rest almost instantly pushes down on the ground with a force larger than
      its weight: the ground must supply the decelerating force as well as support the weight.
  [ ] Balanced forces leave the velocity constant; unbalanced forces change the velocity.
  [ ] A constant resultant force gives a constant acceleration, not a constant speed.

RESULTANT FORCE AND F = ma: CALCULATIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] F = ma with the resultant force (C1); answer (A1). Where three marks are given: equation (C1),
      substitution or rearrangement (C1), answer (A1).
  [ ] Find the resultant force first when two forces act along a line: resultant = larger force −
      smaller force (C1), then a = F ÷ m (C1), answer (A1).
  [ ] Resultant force = driving force − resistive force; the vertical forces cancel.
  [ ] To find one of the forces: resultant force = ma (C1 with the value); backward force = forward
      force − resultant force (A1).
  [ ] Use the total mass being accelerated (for example person + vehicle, or engine + trucks).
  [ ] Falling object: resultant force = weight − air resistance, then a = F ÷ m. At the instant of
      release there is no air resistance, so the weight is the only force (B1) and a = weight ÷ mass
      = g (B1).
  [ ] A resultant force read from a graph: resultant = air resistance at that speed − weight.
  [ ] Accelerating upwards: upward force (thrust, or the force from the floor) = weight + resultant
      force.
  [ ] Change kN to N before substituting.
  [ ] If the acceleration is not given: a = (v − u) ÷ t (C1); then m = F ÷ a, or F = ma (C1); answer
      (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Forces along a line: resultant = sum of the forces one way − sum of the forces the other way.
  [ ] For an upward acceleration F − mg = ma, so the upward force = m(g + a).
  [ ] Change the time to seconds before finding the acceleration.
  [ ] From a known resultant force and acceleration, m = F ÷ a.
  [ ] Use a = F ÷ m and then v = at to find the speed reached from rest.

NEWTON'S THIRD LAW: PAIRS OF FORCES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Newton's third law: when object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts an equal and opposite
      force on A; forces occur in pairs (to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction).
  [ ] A third-law pair of forces: equal in magnitude (B1); opposite in direction, acting on
      different bodies (B1).
  [ ] Applied answers name both forces and what each acts on: the rocket exerts a downward force on
      the hot gas (B1) and the gas exerts an equal and opposite, upward force on the rocket (B1);
      the pump or engine pushes the water backwards and the water pushes it forwards.
  [ ] Pushing off a wall: the wall exerts a force on the swimmer (B1), opposite in direction to the
      force of the swimmer on the wall (B1) and equal in size to it (B1); this force on the swimmer
      causes the acceleration.
  [ ] The partner force acts on the other object (for example the rider pushes down on the horse and
      the horse pushes up on the rider).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A third-law pair is two forces of the same type, equal in size and opposite in direction,
      acting on two different bodies and between the same two bodies (the force on the Moon due to
      the Earth and the force on the Earth due to the Moon).
  [ ] The weight of an object and the normal contact force on it are not a third-law pair: both act
      on the same object.
  [ ] The partner of a person's weight (the Earth pulls the person down) is the gravitational pull
      of the person on the Earth.
  [ ] The forces of a third-law pair are equal even at constant speed: a tractor pulling a trailer
      forwards with 1600 N is pulled backwards by the trailer with 1600 N.
  [ ] If a propeller pushes water backwards with 2000 N, the water pushes the propeller forwards
      with 2000 N; the resistive force is not part of this pair.
  [ ] A man pushing a box forwards is pushed backwards on his hands by the box, and the floor pushes
      forwards on his feet because his feet push backwards on the floor.
  [ ] A stretched spring pulls two trolleys with equal and opposite forces; a = F ÷ m, so the
      trolley with half the mass has twice the acceleration.

1.5.2 FRICTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FRICTION: DIRECTION AND EFFECTS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Friction: a force (B1) that acts when surfaces slide over (rub against) one another and
      opposes the motion (B1).
  [ ] Friction produces heating only when the object moves / slides.
  [ ] Pressing harder increases the friction (resistive) force, so more work is done and more heat
      is produced.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Friction acts along the surface in the direction opposite to the motion (down the slope on a
      box that is moving up it; backwards on a skidding tyre).
  [ ] An object that is pushed but stays at rest, or moves at constant speed, has a frictional force
      equal in size and opposite in direction to the push.
  [ ] Friction can act in the direction of motion: a driven wheel pushes backwards on the road and
      the road pushes the wheel forwards (the force that accelerates the car); a conveyor belt
      accelerates a suitcase forwards.
  [ ] Friction in brakes transfers energy from the kinetic store to the thermal store.
  [ ] Frictional effects in air increase with speed; smoother or oily surfaces reduce friction.

FALLING THROUGH AIR OR A LIQUID: AIR RESISTANCE AND TERMINAL VELOCITY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The forces on a falling object: weight (force of gravity) downwards; air resistance (drag)
      upwards.
  [ ] Air resistance acts upwards because it opposes the motion.
  [ ] Air resistance is zero at the instant of release (speed zero) and increases as the speed
      increases; the weight stays constant.
  [ ] Reaching terminal velocity, full explanation (one mark for each step, up to 5): both forces,
      weight and air resistance, mentioned; at first the weight is larger than the air resistance
      (resultant force downwards), so the object accelerates; as the speed increases the air
      resistance increases; so the resultant force / the acceleration decreases; eventually the air
      resistance equals the weight / the resultant force is zero; then the acceleration is zero /
      the speed is constant (terminal velocity).
  [ ] The initial acceleration of a body falling from rest is g, 9.8 m/s² (10 m/s² in older papers),
      given with its unit.
  [ ] Part credit where the scheme gives two marks for "the acceleration decreases because the air
      resistance increases as the speed increases": the resultant force gets smaller, or the
      gradient of the speed–time graph decreases (C1).
  [ ] Falling at constant speed: the forces balance – weight equals air resistance, no resultant
      (net) force; where two marks are given the two forces must be named.
  [ ] Terminal velocity is the speed at which the air resistance equals the weight; it can be read
      from an air resistance–speed graph at the value of the weight.
  [ ] Force arrows on falling objects: at constant speed the air resistance equals the weight, so
      there is no resultant force; while accelerating the weight is larger than the air resistance,
      so there is a resultant force downwards.
  [ ] Approaching terminal velocity (two of): the weight stays the same; the air resistance
      increases; the forces become equal and opposite.
  [ ] Air resistance is a maximum when the speed is a maximum (the steepest part of a distance–time
      graph).
  [ ] Air resistance changes during a flight because the speed changes (the density of the air or
      the cross-sectional area of the object may also change); in a diagram it is drawn opposite to
      the direction of travel, and the gravitational force is drawn towards the Earth's surface.
  [ ] With air resistance, the air-resistance force is a smaller proportion of the weight for a
      heavier object (B1), so the heavier object has the greater acceleration / is less affected
      (B1).
  [ ] Velocity–time graph of a throw when air resistance matters (any two): the line is not straight
      / the gradient is not constant; the initial downward gradient is steeper; less time to reach
      zero velocity; a smaller area under the first part (less height gained); a smaller final
      speed.
  [ ] Falling in a vacuum compared with air (any two): the objects fall faster / reach the bottom
      sooner; both accelerate at g; a coin and a piece of paper fall together and reach the bottom
      at the same time.
  [ ] Falling through a liquid: the same explanation with the drag / friction / resistive / viscous
      force of the liquid: the weight causes the acceleration at first (B1); the drag increases as
      the speed increases, or the resultant force decreases (B1); the acceleration decreases to
      zero, or the drag eventually equals the weight (B1).
  [ ] Falling at constant speed through a liquid: the weight acts down and a force from the liquid
      acts up (B1); the forces are balanced (B1).
  [ ] An object released under water that accelerates upwards and then reaches a constant speed: at
      first there is a net upward force (B1); the drag (downward) force increases as it speeds up
      (B1); until the downward and upward forces balance (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] At terminal velocity the object is still moving but no longer accelerating; two forces still
      act, weight and air resistance, equal and opposite.
  [ ] Before terminal velocity the velocity increases while the acceleration decreases; the weight
      is constant; the kinetic energy increases.
  [ ] Terminal velocity is the horizontal section of a speed–time graph: the speed rises from zero
      with decreasing gradient and then levels off.
  [ ] Over a short fall the weight of a heavy object always exceeds the air resistance, so it keeps
      accelerating and never reaches terminal velocity.
  [ ] A light ball dropped in air, or a ball released in thick oil: the resistive force grows as the
      speed rises, so the acceleration decreases from the start.
  [ ] Falling at a steady speed: the upward force equals the weight, mg.

PARACHUTES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Opening a parachute increases the air resistance (B1) because of the larger area (B1).
  [ ] When the parachute opens: the air resistance (upward force) increases (B1), so the upward
      force is larger than the weight – the resultant force is upwards (B1).
  [ ] The skydiver decelerates (slows down – does not move upwards) (B1) because there is a net
      upward force (B1).
  [ ] After the parachute opens, full explanation: air resistance acts upwards (B1); at first the
      upward force is larger than the weight, so the resultant force is upwards and the parachutist
      decelerates (B1); the air resistance decreases as the speed falls (B1); until the air
      resistance equals the weight – the forces balance and the speed is steady (B1).
  [ ] With the parachute open the air resistance equals the weight at a lower speed.
  [ ] Forces on a falling parachutist: downward force weight (gravitational force) (B1); upward
      force air resistance (drag) (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] On a parachutist's speed–time graph the parachute opens where the speed starts to fall sharply
      from the first terminal velocity.
  [ ] Just after the parachute opens the resultant force and the acceleration are both upwards while
      the parachutist is still moving down.
  [ ] A parachutist falls more slowly than a person in free fall because the open parachute
      experiences much greater air resistance.

VEHICLES, CYCLISTS AND SWIMMERS: DRIVING FORCE AGAINST RESISTANCE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The backward force on a moving vehicle is caused by air resistance (drag) or friction.
  [ ] A car slowing with the engine off and no brakes is slowed by friction with the road and air
      resistance (both needed for the mark).
  [ ] While accelerating, the driving (forward) force is larger than the air resistance.
  [ ] Constant driving force but decreasing acceleration: the backward force (air resistance /
      friction / drag) increases (M1 or B1) as the speed increases (A1 or B1); so the resultant
      force decreases (B1); finally the resistance equals the driving force and the resultant force
      is zero (B1).
  [ ] A constant (maximum) speed is reached when the resistive force has grown to equal the constant
      forward force: the forces balance, no resultant (net) force.
  [ ] Swimmer or cyclist with a constant forward force: a resistance (water resistance, air
      resistance, friction) acts (B1); the resistive force increases with speed (B1); at constant
      speed the forward force equals the resistive force (B1).
  [ ] To keep the acceleration constant the driving force must increase: friction / air resistance
      acts (M1) and increases as the speed increases (A1), so the driving force must increase to
      keep the resultant force constant (B1).
  [ ] Rolling down a slope until the speed levels off: acceleration with gravity / weight mentioned
      (B1); air resistance / friction increases with speed (B1); terminal (constant) velocity is
      reached when the resistance equals the accelerating force (B1).
  [ ] Constant engine power and a speed that levels off: drag acts (B1); the drag increases with
      speed, so the resultant force and the acceleration decrease (B1); until the resultant force is
      zero – drag equals thrust (B1).
  [ ] Air resistance changes during a journey because the speed changes.
  [ ] The speed–time graph is a curve while coasting, because the resistive forces decrease as the
      speed decreases, so the deceleration is not constant.
  [ ] In a circular turn at constant speed the direction of motion is changing (B1); velocity is a
      vector (B1); so the velocity is changing, which is an acceleration, and the resultant force is
      towards the centre of the circle (B1).

STOPPING DISTANCE: THINKING DISTANCE AND BRAKING DISTANCE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Thinking distance: the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time – between seeing
      the hazard and (starting to) apply the brakes.
  [ ] Braking distance: the distance travelled while the brakes are applied (while the car
      decelerates).
  [ ] Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.
  [ ] Thinking distance = speed × reaction time (the area under the horizontal part of the
      speed–time graph) (C1, A1).
  [ ] Braking distance = area under the sloping part of the graph = average speed × time (C1, A1).
  [ ] Reaction time = duration of the constant-speed (horizontal) section before braking begins.
  [ ] A constant reaction time is shown by the thinking distance being proportional to the speed
      (C1), supported by at least two calculated values of thinking distance ÷ speed, or "doubling
      the speed doubles the thinking distance" (A1).
  [ ] At a higher speed the thinking distance increases (B1), because the reaction time is unchanged
      but the speed is greater (B1).
  [ ] A tired driver: the thinking distance increases (B1); the braking distance does not change
      (B1).
  [ ] The thinking distance is unchanged by the road conditions and by the tyres, because the time
      taken to react (and the speed) is the same.
  [ ] Factors that increase the braking distance (usually any two): higher speed; a wet, icy or oily
      road (less friction); worn tyres; a larger mass of car and load; a smaller braking force; the
      slope of the road; wind / air resistance.
  [ ] A factor that affects the braking distance only (three linked marks): the factor – mass of
      load or car, condition or wear of the tyres, state of the road, brakes, friction (B1); the
      matching change – heavier load, worn tyres, slippery or smooth road, reduced friction (B1);
      the effect – reduced deceleration, or increased time taken to stop (B1).
  [ ] Wet (icy, slippery) road: less friction between tyres and road / the tyres do not grip / the
      car skids (B1); so there is a smaller backward force and less deceleration – the graph is less
      steep (B1); so a longer time to stop / a larger area under the speed–time graph / a longer
      braking distance (B1).
  [ ] Heavier load: the larger mass gives less deceleration for the same braking force (B1); longer
      braking distance (B1). Greater inertia, kinetic energy or momentum is also credited.
  [ ] New tyres instead of badly worn ones: more friction (grip), so greater deceleration (B1);
      shorter braking distance (B1).
  [ ] The same stopping distance with a larger thinking distance means that the braking distance has
      decreased.
  [ ] Fair test of the effect of load on braking distance: keep the speed the same (and the braking
      force, tyres and brake condition).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] During the thinking distance the car continues at uniform velocity; during the braking
      distance it decelerates.
  [ ] Thinking distance = stopping distance − braking distance; it depends on the speed and the
      driver's reaction time, not on the load.
  [ ] Pressing the brake pedal harder changes the braking force, the braking distance and the
      stopping distance, but not the thinking distance.
  [ ] On a wet road the braking distance, and therefore the stopping distance, is greater; the
      thinking distance is unchanged.
  [ ] Stopping distance is shortest on a dry road with new tyres and longest on a wet road with worn
      tyres.
  [ ] On a speed–time graph of an emergency stop, the area under the constant-speed section is the
      thinking distance, the area under the sloping section is the braking distance, and the two
      together are the stopping distance.

1.5.3 ELASTIC DEFORMATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LOAD–EXTENSION GRAPHS AND THE LIMIT OF PROPORTIONALITY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Extension directly proportional to load is shown by a straight-line graph (B1) that passes
      through the origin (B1).
  [ ] Limit of proportionality: the point beyond which the extension is no longer directly
      proportional to the load / force (where two marks are given: the idea that proportionality
      stops C1, "the point at which" it stops A1).
  [ ] On a graph the limit of proportionality is the point where the line stops being straight and
      begins to curve (the end of the straight section, where the gradient starts to change) – not
      the breaking point.
  [ ] Beyond the limit of proportionality the spring extends by a different amount for the same
      extra load: the load is no longer proportional to the extension; the spring may become easier
      to pull.
  [ ] Extension–load graph beyond the limit: the line continues from the end of the straight section
      (B1) and curves upwards – a larger extension for each extra newton (B1).
  [ ] Sketch of an extension–load graph taken well beyond the limit: a straight line of positive
      gradient from the origin, for at least half the line (B1); then a curve of increasing gradient
      starting at the end of the straight line, never becoming vertical, with the limit of
      proportionality labelled where the curve begins (B1).
  [ ] Length–load graph: a straight line up to the limit of proportionality (B1), starting from a
      non-zero length on the length axis, not from the origin (B1); beyond the limit a curve of
      changed gradient – it curves upwards when the spring becomes easier to stretch, and is less
      steep if it becomes harder to stretch (B1).
  [ ] The limit of proportionality has not been reached if the graph is a straight line throughout
      (constant gradient); it has been reached where a graph is no longer straight.
  [ ] To show where the limit of proportionality is: add loads above that value (B1); the increase
      in length for the same increase in load is then different / the graph does not continue as a
      straight line (B1).
  [ ] From a table: the spring that has passed its limit is the one whose extensions for equal
      increases in load are not equal (M1); quote the data that show it stretches more for the later
      loads (A1).
  [ ] "Easier to stretch": a smaller force for the same extension, or a larger extension for the
      same force – shown with a pair of values from the graph.
  [ ] Plotting an extension–load graph: axes labelled with quantity and unit (B1); linear scales
      (B1); a straight line from (0, 0) to the given point when the limit of proportionality is not
      exceeded (B1).
  [ ] A stiffer (stronger) spring compresses or stretches less for the same force.
  [ ] When a mass on a spring is pulled down, the upward force (tension) increases (B1) because the
      spring is stretched more (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The limit of proportionality is the end of the straight-line section through the origin – the
      last point on the straight line before the graph curves; it is before the maximum load.
  [ ] From a table: equal steps of load give equal steps of extension up to the limit; the first
      larger step of extension contains the limit of proportionality.
  [ ] The spring constant is the gradient of the straight section of a load–extension graph (load ÷
      extension).
  [ ] A spring balance has a linear scale only over the straight-line (proportional) part of the
      graph.
  [ ] The fibre, thread or spring that is hardest to stretch has the smallest extension for a given
      load (the lowest, least steep line on an extension–load graph).
  [ ] Extension is proportional to the load (weight = mg): if the same mass stretches a spring three
      times as far on another planet, g there is three times as large.

EXTENSION, LENGTH AND SPRING-CONSTANT CALCULATIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Extension = stretched length − original (unstretched) length (e = l − l₀); stretched length =
      unstretched length + extension.
  [ ] The unstretched length is the length at zero load, read where the line meets the length axis;
      or the length at a known load − the extension for that load.
  [ ] Use the extension, not the total length, in calculations.
  [ ] Spring constant = load ÷ extension (for example 7.0 N ÷ 0.17 m = 41 N/m); F = kx (C1), answer
      (A1).
  [ ] Below the limit of proportionality the extension is directly proportional to the load: use
      proportion, or "load ÷ extension is constant" (C1); answer with its unit (A1).
  [ ] New length = original length + new extension.
  [ ] Reading a graph: find the extension (new length − original length) (C1), then read the load,
      weight or mass from the graph at that extension (A1).
  [ ] A force read from a spring graph can then be used in a = F ÷ m (C1, A1) or m = W ÷ g.
  [ ] Comparing two springs on the same axes: compare the two lines at the same force.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Spring constant k = force ÷ extension, with extension = length − unstretched length: 8 N ÷ (30
      − 10) cm = 0.40 N/cm.
  [ ] Use the total load on the spring, and add the extension to the original length.
  [ ] Doubling the load doubles the extension (not the length).

EXPERIMENT: MEASURING THE EXTENSION OF A SPRING

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Procedure for an extension–load graph: measure the unstretched length of the spring with a
      rule, or attach a marker / pointer and read its position (B1); attach a load, measure the new
      length and calculate extension = new length − original length, or read the extension directly
      against a zeroed rule (B1); repeat for different loads to obtain further results (B1).
  [ ] Readings: hang different weights on the spring and read the pointer position (length) each
      time (B1); extension = reading with the load − reading with no load (B1).
  [ ] Using a calibrated spring to find a mass: hang the object from the spring, measure the length
      / extension and find the weight from the table or graph, or by proportion (B1); mass = weight
      ÷ g (B1).

SPRINGS SIDE BY SIDE AND END TO END

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Two identical springs side by side supporting the same load: half the extension (B1), because
      the springs share the load – each carries half the weight (B1).
  [ ] The limit of proportionality of the pair is not reached at a load that would exceed it for one
      spring, because the load is shared equally: the tension in each spring is half the load (the
      limit for the pair is doubled).
  [ ] Two identical springs in series: the upper spring also supports the weight of the lower
      spring, so its extension is larger.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Identical springs side by side share the load equally (three springs carry one third each);
      work out the load on one spring and use proportion for its extension.
  [ ] Two identical springs in series each carry the full load, so each extends as much as one
      spring alone and the total extension is doubled; in parallel each carries half the load and
      extends half as much.

WIRES, RUBBER AND PERMANENT DEFORMATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A stretched rubber band becomes longer (B1) and thinner / smaller in cross-sectional area
      (B1).
  [ ] When the load is removed after stretching far beyond the limit, the spring has a permanent
      extension: it is longer than it was originally.
  [ ] Beyond the limit of proportionality the extra extension is more than the proportional amount;
      some of the extension may be permanent.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Beyond the limit of proportionality a wire continues to extend, but the extension is no longer
      directly proportional to the force; where the extension–load graph curves upwards the wire has
      become easier to extend.
  [ ] Removing the load from a stretched wire changes its length (extension); its mass does not
      change.
  [ ] An elastic solid returns to its original shape after being deformed (a football when it is
      kicked); bullets, damaged cars and sawn metal are permanently deformed.

1.5.4 CIRCULAR MOTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MOTION IN A CIRCLE: FORCE AND ACCELERATION TOWARDS THE CENTRE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A force is needed to change the direction of the motion (velocity); without it the object
      would move in a straight line.
  [ ] The force is perpendicular to the motion (velocity) and directed towards the centre of the
      circle; it keeps the object in the circular path and accelerates it towards the centre.
  [ ] An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating: the direction of its motion is
      changing (B1), so its velocity is changing, which is an acceleration and requires a resultant
      force (B1).
  [ ] Stating that the velocity changes (M1) because its direction changes, even though the speed is
      constant (A1).
  [ ] Velocity is a vector – it includes direction; speed does not (B1); on a bend the direction of
      motion changes, so the velocity changes even at constant speed (B1); the force acts towards
      the centre of the curve, inwards (B1).
  [ ] The acceleration is towards the centre of the circle.
  [ ] In diagrams the force (or acceleration) arrow is drawn from the object towards the centre of
      the circle or orbit (towards the Sun for a planet, towards the centre of the Earth for a
      satellite), perpendicular to the motion.
  [ ] At the lowest point of a vertical circular path the resultant force is upwards, towards the
      centre of the circle; so the weight is less than the upward (contact) force.
  [ ] In a circular orbit at constant speed the kinetic energy remains constant, because the speed
      is constant (kinetic energy is a scalar).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The force has a constant size and always points towards the centre, at right angles to the
      velocity.
  [ ] The velocity is along the tangent to the circle; the acceleration and the resultant force
      point towards the centre.
  [ ] The acceleration is constant in size but continually changes direction, so the velocity is not
      constant.
  [ ] If the force towards the centre disappears, the body moves off in a straight line along the
      tangent.
  [ ] A car rounding a bend at constant speed has unbalanced forces, with a resultant towards the
      centre of the bend; constant velocity (or rest) means no resultant force.
  [ ] On a curved path the resultant force points towards the concave side of the path.

WHAT PROVIDES THE FORCE TOWARDS THE CENTRE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Planet or satellite: the force is caused by gravitational attraction / the gravitational field
      (B1) of the Sun, or of the Earth for a satellite (B1); its direction is towards the centre of
      the circle (B1).
  [ ] The force of gravity on a satellite is centripetal: at right angles to its motion (B1).
  [ ] A vehicle turning a corner: the force is friction with the ground (B1), acting on the tyres
      towards the centre of the turn; on a banked track, the reaction force (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] An object carried round on a turntable is kept in its circle by friction acting towards the
      centre.
  [ ] An electron is kept moving in a circle round the nucleus by an electrostatic force directed
      towards the nucleus.

1.5.5 TURNING EFFECT OF FORCES
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MOMENT OF A FORCE: DEFINITION AND CALCULATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Moment of a force = force × distance (M1); the distance is the perpendicular distance from the
      pivot (axis) to the line of action of the force (A1).
  [ ] In a calculation: moment = force × (perpendicular) distance in any form (C1); answer (A1).
      F1d1 = F2d2 written algebraically, or any listed force × any listed distance, is accepted for
      the first mark.
  [ ] Unit of moment: N m (a separate B1 in some questions); N cm when the distance is left in
      centimetres – N m only if the distance has been converted to metres.
  [ ] Use the perpendicular (horizontal) distance from the pivot to the vertical line of the weight,
      not a slant distance; where it has to be measured on the diagram the scheme gives a tolerance.
  [ ] Moment of a weight = weight × perpendicular distance from the pivot (support, hinge, toes) to
      the centre of mass; the weight of a uniform lid acts at its centre.
  [ ] Two equal forces on opposite sides of an axis (the two sides of a coil) turn it the same way:
      total moment = 2 × force × distance of each force from the axis = one force × perpendicular
      distance between the forces.
  [ ] Total moment of several equal forces turning the same way = number × force × distance.
  [ ] Force = moment ÷ distance from the axle or pivot.
  [ ] The moment of a constant force decreases as an arm rotates, because the perpendicular distance
      from the axis to the line of action of the force is reduced.
  [ ] The moment is less than the maximum when the force is not applied at right angles to the
      handle; a force in any other direction must be larger, because its perpendicular distance from
      the hinge is less.
  [ ] The turning effect on a coil is greatest when the perpendicular distance between the forces
      and the axis is largest (coil horizontal); it becomes smaller as the perpendicular distance
      between the lines of action of the forces decreases.
  [ ] A force has a moment about a pivot because it does not act at the pivot.
  [ ] The weight of the object itself (for example a ladder) is another force with a turning effect
      about the pivot.
  [ ] Why the real force or moment is greater than the calculated one (any two where asked):
      friction at the axle or hinge; the weight of the rope; the force is not perpendicular to the
      handle; air resistance; an initial acceleration has to be given.
  [ ] Moment–time sketch for a rotating coil: axes labelled and a repeating shape that is always the
      same sign (B1); the time for one revolution spans two repeats of the shape (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The moment of a force is the measure of its turning effect.
  [ ] The distance is the one that meets the line of the force at a right angle – not the distance
      along the pole, up the wall or along the chain, and not the straight-line distance from the
      pivot to the point where the force is applied.
  [ ] Total moment = sum of force × perpendicular distance for forces that turn the same way (the
      weight of a uniform board acts at its centre).
  [ ] Compare moments in consistent units (N m).
  [ ] A suspended lamina: moment of the weight about the point of suspension = weight × horizontal
      distance from the pivot to the vertical line through the centre of mass; it turns the lamina
      back towards the position with the centre of mass below the pivot.

LEVERS; HOW A MOMENT CHANGES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A longer handle needs a smaller force for the same moment (force inversely proportional to
      distance).
  [ ] A smaller force can turn a door against a larger force when its moment is greater (B1),
      because its distance from the hinge is greater than the distance from the hinge to where the
      larger force acts (B1).
  [ ] Lever: the force acting nearer the pivot is the larger one, because its distance to the pivot
      is smaller (B1) and force × distance (the moment) is the same for both (B1).
  [ ] Both people on a see-saw move equal distances further from the pivot: both moments increase
      (C1); the moment of the heavier person increases more (A1); so the see-saw tips down on the
      heavier person's side (B1).
  [ ] A load partly submerged in water: the water exerts an upward force on the load, so the force
      the load exerts on the rule (the tension in the string) is reduced (B1); so its moment about
      the pivot is reduced (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A longer spanner gives a larger turning moment for the same force.
  [ ] A force close to the pivot must be much larger than the load (the muscle of the forearm:
      tension × 5.0 = 40 × 35).
  [ ] Moving a load towards the pivot reduces its moment, so the other end of the rule moves down.

PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS AND CONDITIONS FOR EQUILIBRIUM

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Principle of moments: (sum of / total) clockwise moments = (sum of / total) anticlockwise
      moments (M1) for a body in equilibrium / balanced / stationary / not turning (A1). "CW = ACW"
      is accepted as long as moments are mentioned.
  [ ] Accepted alternative: the sum / total / net / resultant moment is zero (M1) when the object is
      in equilibrium (A1).
  [ ] The moments are taken about the same pivot (about any point).
  [ ] Conditions for equilibrium: no resultant force – upward force = downward force (B1); no
      resultant moment – clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment (B1).
  [ ] An object rotates when the moment of one force about the support becomes greater than (is no
      longer equal to) the opposing moment.
  [ ] Pivot moved away from the centre of mass: the object rotates / tilts (C1), turning so that the
      side containing the centre of mass goes down (A1), because the weight acting at the centre of
      mass now has a moment about the pivot (B1).
  [ ] If the weight of a beam is not negligible the supporting force is greater, because the weight
      of the beam adds to the moment that has to be balanced.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Even when the forces on a rod add up to zero, unequal moments give a resultant moment and the
      rod starts to rotate.
  [ ] Two equal forces keep a body in equilibrium only if they are opposite in direction and act
      along the same line; equal and opposite forces along different lines form a turning pair.
  [ ] If a weight has to be hung on one side of the pivot to balance an object, the centre of mass
      of the object lies on the other side of the pivot.
  [ ] A balanced rod stays balanced where g is lower, because both weights (and so both moments) are
      reduced in the same proportion.
  [ ] A beam balanced at its centre with loads at equal distances has equal downward forces at the
      ends and an upward force at the pivot equal to their sum.
  [ ] An upward force on one side of the pivot turns the beam the same way as a downward force on
      the other side.

CALCULATIONS WITH THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Moments about the pivot: F1 × d1 = F2 × d2 (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Measure every distance from the pivot (add lengths where necessary) and use perpendicular
      distances.
  [ ] Three-mark versions: the moment of the known force, or the distances from the pivot, worked
      out (C1); clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] With masses on both sides g cancels: m1x1 = m2x2.
  [ ] The weight of a uniform rule acts at the 50 cm mark; a load resting on a rule acts at the
      centre of the load.
  [ ] Include the moment of the weight of the plank or beam itself when it is given.
  [ ] Change a mass to a weight first, W = mg (g = 9.8 N/kg; the scheme allows 10).
  [ ] On the point of tipping: clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment about the edge; "=" or ">" is
      accepted in the moment equation.
  [ ] Finding a third force: (difference between the two known moments) ÷ its distance from the
      pivot (C1); answer (A1); its direction is the one that makes its moment oppose the larger
      moment (B1).
  [ ] In equilibrium the forces also balance: force from the pivot = upward force − total downward
      force (C1, A1).
  [ ] When the result has to be compared with a limit, state the comparison (B1 for the moments
      equation, B1 for the value and the comparison).
  [ ] A horizontal force that cancels a moment = moment ÷ its perpendicular (vertical) distance from
      the pivot.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Uniform rule pivoted away from its centre: the weight acts at the centre; moments give one
      force (W × 25 = F × 75, so F = W/3) and "no resultant force" gives the other (P + F = W, so
      P = ⅔W).
  [ ] Three or more forces: add the moments each way; the extra force must supply the difference, on
      the side of the smaller moment (or acting upwards on the heavier side).
  [ ] A block about to tip about a corner: the weight acts through the centre, half the base width
      from the pivot.
  [ ] With masses, mass × distance is equal on both sides of the pivot (400 × 12 = 300 × 16).
  [ ] The ratio of two balancing weights is the inverse of the ratio of their distances from the
      pivot.

EXPERIMENT TO VERIFY THE PRINCIPLE OF MOMENTS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Diagram or apparatus: a metre rule (ruler), a pivot (fulcrum) and at least two weights
      (loads), labelled (B1).
  [ ] Balance the rule on the pivot on its own first; place a weight on each side and adjust the
      positions until it balances (B1).
  [ ] Measure the distance of each weight from the pivot and calculate force (or mass) × distance
      for each side (B1).
  [ ] The clockwise and anticlockwise moments are found to be equal – the net moment is zero (B1).
  [ ] Vary the weights or positions and repeat (B1).

1.5.6 CENTRE OF GRAVITY
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CENTRE OF GRAVITY (CENTRE OF MASS): MEANING AND POSITION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Centre of gravity (centre of mass): the point where the whole weight of the object acts, or
      can be taken to act (B1).
  [ ] Also accepted: the place where the entire mass seems to be located / appears to be
      concentrated; the point where the object balances.
  [ ] The centre of mass of a uniform block, slab or rectangle is at its centre (half the height;
      half-way between the edges).
  [ ] For a shape that is wider at the base, the centre of mass is on the centre line but lower than
      half the height.
  [ ] The centre of mass of a symmetrical object is in the middle.
  [ ] The centre of mass is not half-way along when the mass is not uniformly distributed along the
      length (there is more mass at one end).
  [ ] An object balances at its centre of mass; for an object balanced on a pivot the centre of mass
      is marked immediately above the tip of the pivot.
  [ ] The weight arrow is drawn vertically downwards through the centre of mass.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The centre of mass of a balanced or suspended object is vertically below (or above) the point
      of support; it need not lie within the material of the object.
  [ ] The centre of mass is nearest the base in the shape that has most of its area (mass) close to
      the base; it is lowest when a block rests on its largest face.
  [ ] Adding mass above the centre of gravity raises it (a weight placed on top of a block; sand
      added on top of a layer of sand in a container).

FINDING THE CENTRE OF GRAVITY OF A LAMINA BY EXPERIMENT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Hang the lamina (card, thin sheet) freely from a pin / nail through a hole and wait until it
      is stationary (B1).
  [ ] Hang a plumb line (a mass on a string) from the same pin and mark its line on the lamina (B1).
  [ ] Repeat using another hole; the centre of mass is where the lines cross (B1).
  [ ] Repeat from a third hole as a check (credited in the six-mark version, one mark for each
      step).
  [ ] Accepted alternative: balance the sheet on a sharp edge, mark the line of the edge and repeat
      in a new position – the centre of mass is at the intersection; or balance it on a sharp point.
  [ ] A lamina released with its centre of gravity to one side of the pivot: it rotates about the
      pivot (B1); it speeds up then slows down / overshoots / oscillates with decreasing amplitude
      (B1); it stops with the centre of gravity vertically below the pivot (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A freely suspended lamina hangs with its centre of gravity vertically below the pin: it lies
      on that vertical line, inside the card, nearer the part where most of the area (mass) is – not
      at the pin and not at the edge.
  [ ] The centre of mass of an irregular lamina can be found with a pin (to hang or balance the
      lamina).

STABILITY AND TOPPLING

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Features that make an object stable: a wide base / large base area (B1); a low centre of mass
      (B1).
  [ ] More stable: a lower centre of gravity or a wider / longer base; a thicker piece (wider base)
      is more stable than a thinner one.
  [ ] A wider base is more stable: when tilted the object does not topple / falls back, or it must
      be tilted through a larger angle before toppling (B1), because the centre of mass is still
      above (inside) the base, so the weight turns it back (B1).
  [ ] An object does not fall over while the line of its weight passes inside its base.
  [ ] Toppling: when the object is tilted far enough (or stands on a steep slope) the centre of mass
      falls outside the base – the line of action of the weight passes beyond the pivoting corner or
      edge (B1); the weight then causes a moment (turning effect) about that point which turns the
      object over (B1).
  [ ] An object resting on a corner falls over because its weight has a moment about the corner
      (C1): clockwise when the centre of mass is to the right of the corner, anticlockwise when it
      is to the left (A1).
  [ ] An object is stable when its centre of mass lies within the base (in the middle horizontally).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Stability depends on the area (width) of the base and the height of the centre of mass.
  [ ] For the same height of centre of gravity a wider base is more stable; for equal bases the
      object with the highest centre of gravity (most of its mass near the top) is the least stable.
  [ ] The least stable object has a high centre of mass above a narrow base and topples after only a
      small tilt (a wide bowl on a tall stem); the most stable has a wide base and a low centre of
      gravity (a conical flask).
  [ ] An object is just about to topple when its centre of mass is vertically above the edge of its
      base (the pivot).
  [ ] A vehicle is most stable with a low centre of mass and a wide wheel base; on a slope the
      centre of mass should be as low as possible and between the wheels.
  [ ] A balancing toy is stable because the added hanging weights lower its centre of mass to below
      the point of support.

1.6 MOMENTUM
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MOMENTUM: DEFINITION, UNIT AND CALCULATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Momentum = mass × velocity (the product of mass and velocity), p = mv.
  [ ] Unit of momentum: kg m/s or N s (kg/m/s is not accepted).
  [ ] In a calculation the formula mv alone is ignored – the value must be worked out. Typical
      marks: p = mv with the substitution (C1), answer with unit (A1); or the value (1) and the unit
      (1).
  [ ] Change in momentum = m × (v − u) (C1); answer with unit kg m/s or N s (A1).
  [ ] A momentum may be read from a momentum–time graph (B1).
  [ ] "Show that" p²/2m = Ek: substitute p = mv to give m²v²/2m (B1); simplify to ½mv² and state
      that this is the kinetic energy (B1).
  [ ] Mass from momentum and kinetic energy: m = p² ÷ (2Ek) (C1); answer (A1).

IMPULSE AND FORCE: RESULTANT FORCE = RATE OF CHANGE OF MOMENTUM

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Resultant force = change in momentum per unit time / rate of change of momentum.
  [ ] F = change in momentum ÷ time taken, in any form (C1); answer (A1). F = ma is accepted as an
      alternative route.
  [ ] From a momentum–time graph: the change in momentum read from the graph (C1); force = change in
      momentum ÷ time taken (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Impulse = change in momentum, so force = impulse ÷ time (C1 for the change in momentum or
      impulse, C1 for F = Δp/Δt, A1).
  [ ] A force is needed to change momentum (C1); for a stream that is stopped, the momentum arriving
      in 1.0 s is the rate of change of momentum, which is the force (A1).
  [ ] When the speed of the stream doubles, the mass arriving per second also doubles, so the
      momentum arriving per second (the force) increases by a factor of 4.
  [ ] A particle hitting a wall: on collision there is a change of momentum / direction / velocity,
      or momentum is transferred to the wall (B1); the force is the change in momentum ÷ time, or
      equal-sized forces act on the wall and on the particle – Newton's third law (B1).
  [ ] Why the force is larger in a collision without a cushioning material (any two of three): the
      contact surfaces are more rigid / less pliable; the time of contact decreases; the
      acceleration increases, or force = (mv − mu)/t, or force = impulse/t.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Impulse = force × time = change in momentum; unit N s.
  [ ] Change the time to seconds (for example ms to s) before dividing.

CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM: COLLISIONS, EXPLOSIONS AND ROCKETS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Rocket propulsion in terms of momentum: the expelled water or gas gains (downward) momentum
      (B1); momentum is conserved (B1); so the rocket gains an equal upward momentum and accelerates
      upwards (B1).
  [ ] Vehicles that move together after a collision: m1 × u1 = (m1 + m2) × v (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] "Show that momentum is conserved": p = mv, the initial momentum worked out (B1); the final
      momentum of the objects together worked out and seen to be the same value (B1).
  [ ] Explosion or separation into two parts: momentum is conserved, mv = m1v1 + m2v2 (C1);
      rearranged with the values (C1); answer (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Opposite directions have opposite signs: 2.0 × 2.0 − 1.0 × 1.0 = (2.0 + 1.0)v gives
      v = 1.0 m/s.

1.7.1 ENERGY
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ENERGY STORES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Energy stored in a battery or cell: chemical (potential) energy; the useful energy output from
      a battery is electrical.
  [ ] Energy stored in a fuel (coal, oil, a fuel gas) before it is burnt: chemical (potential)
      energy – with no other energy named.
  [ ] Energy used by a person to do work, or stored in muscles: chemical (potential) energy.
  [ ] Energy stored in a stretched spring or a stretched rubber band: elastic (strain) potential
      energy.
  [ ] A turning turbine possesses kinetic energy.
  [ ] Water has least gravitational potential energy at its lowest point.
  [ ] A load pulled down on a spring and held: the energy stored in the spring is larger at the
      lower position; the gravitational potential energy of the load is larger at the higher
      position.
  [ ] When the formulas are asked for together: gravitational potential energy = mgh and kinetic
      energy = ½mv² (both needed for the mark).
  [ ] Total energy emitted in pulses = number of pulses × energy of each pulse (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Batteries, fuels and food are chemical energy stores; the energy stored is measured in joules.
  [ ] Gravitational potential energy is greatest at the highest point; for equal masses the highest
      object has the most.
  [ ] A ball on a curved track has its maximum kinetic energy at the lowest point, where its
      gravitational potential energy is least.

ENERGY TRANSFERS: DESCRIBING THE CHANGES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Falling object: gravitational potential energy to kinetic energy (a change must be stated;
      "GPE to KE" is accepted).
  [ ] Falling at constant (terminal) speed: gravitational potential energy (B1) is transferred to
      thermal / internal energy – heat, kinetic energy of the air (B1) – not to kinetic energy of
      the falling object, whose kinetic energy does not change.
  [ ] Falling water whose speed is unchanged at the bottom: kinetic energy to internal (thermal)
      energy; "energy to sound" and "back to GPE in splashes" are ignored.
  [ ] Falling pieces: gravitational potential energy is transferred to (any two) kinetic energy;
      heat / thermal / internal energy; sound.
  [ ] Braking: from kinetic energy (B1) – and from gravitational potential energy on a slope – to
      thermal (heat / internal) energy (B1), with no intermediate energy stated.
  [ ] Brakes heat up because friction produces thermal energy / kinetic energy is changed to heat /
      work is done against friction.
  [ ] A ball stopping on the ground, or an inelastic collision: kinetic energy becomes internal /
      thermal energy (of the ball and ground, of the vehicles or the air); in a collision kinetic
      energy of one vehicle may go to kinetic energy of the other.
  [ ] Energy forms that increase as a skateboarder descends: kinetic energy (B1); thermal / internal
      energy or sound (B1). A vehicle running downhill: some energy is transferred to heat (B1) and
      some to kinetic energy (B1).
  [ ] Collapsing gas cloud, or matter falling together under gravity: gravitational potential energy
      (it decreases) to kinetic energy (B1 or C1), then to internal (thermal) energy and light (B1
      or A1).
  [ ] Accelerating car, or aeroplane accelerating at constant height: chemical (potential) energy of
      the fuel (B1) to kinetic energy (B1), with thermal energy also produced (B1). The store that
      decreases is the fuel / chemical energy.
  [ ] Car accelerating up a hill: chemical energy of the fuel decreases (B1); gravitational
      potential energy increases (B1); kinetic energy increases (B1).
  [ ] Rocket accelerating upwards: chemical (potential) energy at the start (B1), to gravitational
      potential energy (B1), heat (B1) and kinetic energy (B1).
  [ ] A ship or vehicle at constant speed: chemical (potential) energy (B1) to thermal energy / heat
      – no gain in kinetic energy (B1).
  [ ] Pedalling uphill at constant speed, or lifting a load at constant speed by hand: chemical
      energy of the person decreases (B1); gravitational potential energy increases (B1); thermal
      (internal) energy is produced, from friction (B1).
  [ ] A stretched rope or spring released: elastic (strain) potential energy (B1) to kinetic energy
      / gravitational potential energy (B1).
  [ ] Bungee jumper while the rope stretches (no air resistance): gravitational potential energy
      (B1) and kinetic energy at the start (B1) are transferred to elastic (strain) potential energy
      by the lowest point, with no intermediate form (B1).
  [ ] Battery, motor and pulley lifting a load (any three): reduction in the chemical energy store
      of the battery; electrical work done by the motor; mechanical work done on the weight;
      increase in the gravitational potential energy store of the weight; increase in thermal energy
      (in the motor or the air); electrical heating by the current.
  [ ] How energy becomes thermal when a motor lifts a load (any one): friction / air resistance;
      electrical heating.
  [ ] Electric pump: electrical energy supplied (B1); kinetic energy of the water and moving parts
      produced (B1); some thermal (internal) energy produced (B1).
  [ ] Water wheel driving a generator (any two): potential energy of the water to kinetic energy (of
      the water or wheel); kinetic energy of the wheel to electrical energy.
  [ ] Filament lamp: electrical energy in (B1), to heat (thermal / internal) energy (B1) and light
      (B1).
  [ ] Cells: chemical (potential) energy (B1) to electrical energy (B1). Charging a rechargeable
      battery: electrical energy (B1) converted to chemical energy stored in the battery (B1).
  [ ] Rubbing hands together: chemical (or kinetic) energy (B1) is converted to heat (B1).
  [ ] Useful energy outputs from a mobile phone (any two): light; sound; radio / microwave /
      electromagnetic waves.
  [ ] Solar charger: nuclear energy in the Sun, then light, then electrical (B1); chemical energy in
      the battery (B1).
  [ ] An oscillating load stops because of friction / air resistance: thermal energy (heat) is
      produced.
  [ ] The extra kinetic energy after an explosion comes from chemical (potential) energy stored in
      the fuel.
  [ ] Roller coaster: going downhill the gravitational potential energy decreases (B1) and kinetic
      energy is gained (B1); going uphill kinetic energy changes back to potential energy (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Coal-fired power station (boiler, turbine, generator): chemical → heat → kinetic → electrical;
      the turbine gives out kinetic energy and the generator electrical energy.
  [ ] Hydroelectric power station: gravitational potential energy → electrical energy. Wind turbine:
      kinetic energy → electrical energy.
  [ ] An electric heater or kettle transfers energy by electrical work to a thermal store; a motor
      transfers energy electrically to a kinetic store; a current in a resistor transfers energy to
      internal (thermal) energy.
  [ ] A battery-driven motor lifting a load at constant speed: chemical → gravitational potential
      (the kinetic energy does not change).
  [ ] A dropped ball that bounces: gravitational potential → kinetic → elastic potential (while
      squashed against the ground) → kinetic.
  [ ] A diver slowing down while moving down through water loses both kinetic and gravitational
      potential energy; both end up as thermal energy.
  [ ] A ball rolling down a track and stopping lower on the other side: potential → kinetic + heat →
      potential + heat (heat is produced on the way down as well as on the way up).
  [ ] Wasted transfer: kinetic energy to thermal energy by friction. A useful chain: chemical
      (person) → elastic → kinetic → electrical → sound.
  [ ] A spacecraft in a circular orbit at constant height keeps the same kinetic and gravitational
      potential energy, so no energy is transferred; a car at constant speed still transfers energy
      from the fuel to thermal energy.
  [ ] At constant speed on level ground the work done by a cyclist equals the thermal energy
      produced (work against friction and air resistance).

KINETIC ENERGY (Ek = ½mv²)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Kinetic energy = ½mv², where m is the mass of the object.
  [ ] Calculation marks: KE = ½mv², algebraic or numerical (C1); the numerical substitution (C1) – a
      correct substitution also earns the formula mark; answer (A1).
  [ ] Use the total mass that is moving (the combined mass and common velocity after a collision;
      rider plus vehicle).
  [ ] Speed from kinetic energy: v² = 2Ek ÷ m (C1), then the square root (A1). Mass from kinetic
      energy: m = 2Ek ÷ v² (C1 for the equation or rearrangement, C1 for the substitution, A1).
  [ ] Use the mass in kilograms.
  [ ] The speed may have to be read from a graph (for example the terminal speed) or found first
      from distance ÷ time.
  [ ] If all the kinetic energy becomes internal energy: ½mv² = energy needed, then solve for v (C1,
      A1).
  [ ] An object of twice the mass at the same speed has twice the kinetic energy; if twice the
      energy is needed to melt it (M1) and it has twice the kinetic energy, the mass cancels (M1),
      so the result is unchanged (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Kinetic energy is proportional to v²: doubling the speed multiplies it by four; halving the
      mass and doubling the speed doubles it. The speed matters more than the mass because it is
      squared.
  [ ] Use SI units: 56 g = 0.056 kg; 1500 m/minute = 25 m/s.
  [ ] v = √(2Ek ÷ m).

GRAVITATIONAL POTENTIAL ENERGY (ΔEp = mgΔh)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Change in gravitational potential energy: ΔEp = mgΔh (C1 with the substitution); answer (A1).
      It equals weight × height.
  [ ] Use the vertical height, the mass in kilograms and the height in metres.
  [ ] Use the mass of the object that is raised only (for example the mass of the water only).
  [ ] Rearranged: h = Ep ÷ (mg) and m = Ep ÷ (gh) (C1, A1). When the total height is asked for, add
      the initial height (C1, C1, A1).
  [ ] Change in gravitational potential energy per kilogram = g × h.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The change in gravitational potential energy = weight × vertical height moved, whatever path
      the load takes – not the distance along the slope and not the horizontal distance.
  [ ] Only the mass left at the top has gained potential energy overall (a lorry that returns to the
      bottom has not).

CONSERVATION OF ENERGY; ENERGY THAT IS WASTED

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Principle of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed (B1); it can only
      be transferred from one store (form) to another (B1). "The total energy of an isolated system
      is constant" is also credited.
  [ ] Applied to a motor lifting a load: the total energy is constant (B1); energy from the power
      supply = energy gained by the weight + thermal energy, to the air or due to friction (B1).
      Electrical energy changes to potential energy (and kinetic energy and heat).
  [ ] A journey that starts and ends at rest at the same height: the total stays constant and all
      the chemical energy used ends up as heat.
  [ ] With no friction or air resistance, loss in gravitational potential energy = gain in kinetic
      energy: ½mv² = mgΔh (C1); v = √(2gΔh) (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Height from a speed: mgh = ½mv² or the use of h = v² ÷ 2g (1); answer (1) – the value for
      g = 10 N/kg is accepted.
  [ ] Kinetic energy at a lower point = loss of gravitational potential energy = mg × height fallen
      (C1, C1, A1 with the potential energy at the top).
  [ ] Minimum height for a given kinetic energy: mgh = kinetic energy needed, so h = Ek ÷ (mg) (C1,
      A1). If all the kinetic energy becomes gravitational potential energy: h = KE ÷ mg; give 2 or
      3 significant figures to match the data.
  [ ] At the lowest point of a bungee jump all the energy is elastic potential energy; part-way,
      kinetic energy = loss of gravitational potential energy − energy stored.
  [ ] Smooth track, released from rest: the speed is greatest at the lowest point and least at the
      highest (a lower height means more kinetic energy).
  [ ] Frictionless track: the car cannot rise above its starting height – the gravitational
      potential energy gained cannot be greater than the original; it travels back and returns to
      its starting point.
  [ ] Objects of different mass dropped from the same height land at the same speed (no air
      resistance), because both gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy are proportional
      to mass / the mass cancels / they have the same acceleration g.
  [ ] Why the gain in kinetic energy is less than the loss of gravitational potential energy: air
      resistance / friction (B1); heat (thermal energy) or sound is produced – work is done against
      friction or air resistance (B1).
  [ ] Why the gravitational potential energy gained is less than the electrical energy supplied (any
      two): the motor / mass has kinetic energy; heat is generated in the motor / wires; heat is
      generated in the air due to drag; work is done to lift the string.
  [ ] Why the work done is much larger than the gravitational potential energy gained: friction at
      the axle, drag, or a chain is lifted as well (B1); so heat is produced / work is done against
      friction or drag / work is done raising the chain (B1).
  [ ] Why the electrical output is much less than the energy in the fuel: some chemical energy is
      transferred to thermal energy (and sound and light) (B1); thermal energy is lost to the air /
      water / buildings / equipment (B1).
  [ ] Why a temperature rise is less than calculated, or the real speed must be greater than
      calculated (any one or two as asked): some thermal energy is transferred to the ground, air or
      wall; work is done deforming or shattering the object or compressing the ground; the object
      bounces; air resistance reduces the speed; heat is also needed to raise the object to its
      melting point.
  [ ] Work is done by the driving force but the kinetic energy does not increase, because an equal
      amount of work is done against the drag: the energy becomes heat.
  [ ] A load lowered on a spring by a person: the chemical energy of the person / the gravitational
      potential energy of the load decreases while the energy stored in the spring increases (B1);
      the increase in spring energy is greater than the decrease in gravitational potential energy,
      and the difference is the work done by the person (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] At terminal velocity the kinetic energy is constant, so the loss of gravitational potential
      energy equals the thermal energy produced (work done against air resistance).
  [ ] With no air resistance the total energy (gravitational potential + kinetic) is the same at
      every point; part-way down the object has both.
  [ ] A bouncing ball rises less each time because some kinetic energy is converted to thermal
      energy at each bounce; a pendulum has most kinetic energy the first time it passes its lowest
      point.
  [ ] Final kinetic energy = gravitational potential energy lost − work done against friction.

1.7.2 WORK
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WORK DONE = FORCE × DISTANCE MOVED IN THE DIRECTION OF THE FORCE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force (formula B1 or C1; answer B1
      or A1).
  [ ] Work is done because a force moves through a distance (in the direction of the force).
  [ ] When the force acts along a slope, use the distance moved along the slope.
  [ ] Use the distance in metres (for example a change in length of 0.93 − 0.70 = 0.23 m).
  [ ] The distance may have to be worked out first: the distance for a quarter turn is the
      circumference ÷ 4; the distance travelled in 1.0 s is the speed × 1.0 s.
  [ ] Number of movements needed = total work ÷ work done in one movement (C1 for work = force ×
      distance, C1 for the work in one movement, A1).
  [ ] Work done stretching a spring = average force × distance in metres (C1, A1).
  [ ] Useful work done raising a load = mgh (force × distance) (C1, A1).
  [ ] A force perpendicular to the direction of motion does no work: there is no distance moved
      along its line of action (M1 or C1), so no work is done by the force that keeps an object in a
      circular path or orbit (A1).
  [ ] Because no work is done on it, the (kinetic and gravitational potential) energy of a satellite
      in a circular orbit remains constant.
  [ ] From battery to moving scooter in terms of work done: electrical work is done / a current is
      made to flow (B1); mechanical work is done by the motor / on the wheel (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The unit of work is the joule (J).
  [ ] Work done by or against a weight = weight × vertical height moved – not the distance along the
      slope, the length of the ramp or plank, or the horizontal distance.
  [ ] Work done by a push along a ramp = force × distance along the ramp (the direction of the
      push).
  [ ] No work is done against gravity on an object carried horizontally at constant height.
  [ ] The contact force and the weight do no work on an object moving horizontally, because they are
      perpendicular to the motion; the pulling force does the work that increases the kinetic energy
      store.

WORK AND ENERGY TRANSFERS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Work is done by gravity / the weight (B1); energy is transferred from the gravitational
      potential energy store (B1) to the kinetic energy store (B1).
  [ ] Pushing a vehicle up a slope: work is done against the weight (gravitational force) (B1),
      producing gravitational potential energy (B1), and against friction / air resistance (B1),
      producing heat (thermal / internal energy) (B1).
  [ ] Falling at terminal velocity: energy is transferred by doing work against friction / drag /
      the resistive force (B1), to the internal (thermal) energy store of the surrounding fluid
      (B1).
  [ ] The kinetic energy gained is less than the work done because of friction / drag (B1): work is
      done against the resistance and energy is lost as heat (B1).
  [ ] The work done is larger than the gravitational potential energy gained because a larger force
      is needed (B1), because of friction (for example between wheel and axle or tyre and plank) /
      heat produced, the weight of the rope, or the load gaining kinetic energy (B1).
  [ ] Work is done in tipping a block on to its end because its centre of mass rises (B1), so
      gravitational potential energy is gained (B1). Alternative route: the block has weight (B1)
      and a force moves through a distance in its own direction (B1).
  [ ] A lift balanced by a counterweight of equal mass needs very little work from the motor: work
      is done by the falling counterweight, which loses gravitational potential energy (B1), and
      this raises the lift; the motor only does work against friction and to accelerate the lift
      (B1).
  [ ] Average stopping (braking) force: work done = force × distance = kinetic energy lost, so
      F = kinetic energy ÷ distance (C1, A1).
  [ ] Work done lifting a load = mgh (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Work done by a force = energy transferred to the object it acts on.
  [ ] Work done increasing the kinetic energy = resultant force × distance.
  [ ] Work done against the braking force = kinetic energy lost: F × d = ½mv².
  [ ] On a ramp: total work done = force × distance along the ramp; gain in gravitational potential
      energy = weight × vertical height; work done against friction = the difference.
  [ ] Lifting at constant speed with no friction: work done = gain in gravitational potential energy
      = mgh, using the vertical height.

1.7.3 ENERGY RESOURCES
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RENEWABLE AND NON-RENEWABLE RESOURCES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Renewable energy source: will not run out / is being replaced. "Can be reused" and "can be
      used again" are not accepted.
  [ ] Non-renewable energy source: is not being replaced or renewed (it takes very many years to
      form) / will run out / is finite / gets used up. "Cannot be reused" is not accepted.
  [ ] Renewable sources credited: solar (sunlight, solar panels or cells), wind (wind turbines),
      tidal, wave, geothermal, hydroelectric, biomass (biofuel, biogas). Nuclear is not accepted.
  [ ] Wood: older schemes accepted "wood" as biomass; the most recent scheme accepts "wood from a
      managed source" and ignores "wood" on its own.
  [ ] Non-renewable sources credited: coal, oil, gas, peat, nuclear (uranium).
  [ ] The sources likely to run out first: coal and oil.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Non-renewable means the resource is being used up faster than it is replaced.
  [ ] A nuclear power station uses a non-renewable source; a battery is also a non-renewable source.
  [ ] Biofuels are renewable and available at all times; solar and wind are renewable but
      intermittent.

GENERATING ELECTRICITY: POWER STATIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Oil-fired power station, in order: burner, boiler, turbine, generator, then transformer (one
      placed correctly C1; all correct A1).
  [ ] Geothermal power: the energy comes from hot rocks underground (B1); the hot rocks heat water
      so that it boils / turns to steam (B1); the pressure (force) of the steam turns the turbine
      (B1).
  [ ] Places where energy is lost as heat between the fuel and the user (any two): furnace / boiler;
      turbines; generator; cooling water / cooling towers; chimney (waste gases); transformer;
      transmission cables.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Coal-fired power station: burning coal heats water in the boiler; the steam turns a turbine;
      the turbine turns a generator; electromagnetic induction produces electrical energy (chemical
      → heat → kinetic → electrical).
  [ ] A nuclear power station has no furnace in which fuel is burned, but it still heats water to
      steam that turns a turbine and generator; an oil-fired station burns fuel and emits waste
      gases into the atmosphere.
  [ ] Solar cells (panels) produce electricity directly, without a turbine or an a.c. generator;
      hydroelectric, nuclear and wind generation all turn a turbine.
  [ ] Flowing river water turning a turbine is a hydroelectric source.
  [ ] A geothermal power station transfers thermal energy from hot rocks (through hot water and
      steam) into electrical energy.

ADVANTAGES, DISADVANTAGES AND ENVIRONMENTAL EFFECTS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Burning a fossil fuel (coal, oil, natural gas) to generate electricity causes much global
      warming (B1), because it produces carbon dioxide / greenhouse gases (B1).
  [ ] Environmental consequence of coal- or oil-fired power stations, as one cause with its effect:
      carbon dioxide / greenhouse gases emitted (B1), leading to global warming / an increased
      greenhouse effect / melting ice caps / a rise in sea level (B1).
  [ ] Alternative cause and effect: sulfur dioxide / nitrogen oxides emitted, giving acid rain (B1),
      which causes breathing difficulty, damage to buildings, acidified lakes and harm to plants and
      animals (B1).
  [ ] Alternative cause and effect: oil spills (B1) that damage or kill plants, sea-life and animals
      (B1).
  [ ] Also credited as an environmental problem of burning oil or coal: pollution by smoke and toxic
      gases such as carbon monoxide; warming of lakes, rivers or the sea. Damage to the ozone layer
      is not accepted.
  [ ] Reducing the carbon dioxide produced reduces global warming.
  [ ] Nuclear compared with oil, advantage: no CO₂, SO₂, nitrogen oxides or greenhouse gases are
      emitted, so it does not contribute to global warming or acid rain (no toxic gases or oil
      spills).
  [ ] Nuclear compared with oil, disadvantage: radioactive (nuclear) waste causes storage problems,
      or an explosion / meltdown / leak emits radioactivity.
  [ ] Nuclear or coal compared with wind, advantage (any one): reliable / not intermittent – the
      wind is not always blowing and is not available in some places; a smaller land area for the
      same power; less harm to birds; less visual effect; no interference with TV or radar signals.
  [ ] Hydroelectric compared with coal, advantage: no fuel cost; less pollution (no smoke, dust,
      harmful gases or CO₂); no coal to transport; renewable; responds quickly to demand.
  [ ] Harmful effect of a hydroelectric scheme: flooding of land / destroys habitats; fish cannot
      pass or are killed by the turbines; uses a large area of land; spoils the scenery; the lake or
      river silts up.
  [ ] A hydroelectric station does not run at full capacity all year: there is not enough water /
      the rainfall varies / there are periods of low demand.
  [ ] A solar charger on a cloudy day: less (or no) light reaches the charger, so less (or no)
      current is produced.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Nuclear fuel: advantage – no carbon dioxide and not dependent on the weather; disadvantage –
      radioactive waste; it is not renewable.
  [ ] A gas-fired power station burns a fossil fuel and produces carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas;
      geothermal, nuclear and wind stations do not.
  [ ] A fossil fuel such as natural gas is available constantly; solar, tidal and wind sources are
      not available all the time. A nuclear reactor gives a continuous, steady output for the
      longest time.

RESOURCES THAT DEPEND ON ENERGY FROM THE SUN

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Wind and hydroelectric energy are renewable and are caused by energy from the Sun (B1);
      geothermal energy is renewable and is not caused by energy from the Sun (B1).

1.7.4 EFFICIENCY
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EFFICIENCY: DEFINITION AND CALCULATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input (or the same ratio of powers), as a
      fraction or a percentage. Definition marks: output ÷ input (C1); useful work (energy) obtained
      ÷ total work (energy) put in (A1).
  [ ] To find an efficiency the input energy or power is needed as well as the output.
  [ ] Calculation: efficiency = (useful) power output ÷ power input, or energy output ÷ energy
      input, in any form (C1); answer (A1), as a decimal or a percentage.
  [ ] Input = output ÷ efficiency; useful output = input × efficiency.
  [ ] A correct number with a wrong unit prefix (2.6 × 10⁶ written with the unit MW) scores 1 mark
      only.
  [ ] Total energy input = wasted energy + useful output (C1); efficiency = useful output ÷ total
      input (A1).
  [ ] Wasted fraction = 1 − efficiency (C1); rate of energy wasted = input power × wasted fraction
      (A1).
  [ ] Energy wasted = wasted power × time in seconds (C1, A1).
  [ ] Two devices with the same useful output: useful output = input × efficiency for the first
      (C1); efficiency of the second = that output ÷ its input (A1).
  [ ] Hydroelectric scheme: efficiency = electrical energy output in a given time ÷ gravitational
      potential energy input in the same time (C1, A1).
  [ ] Useful (light) energy = efficiency × energy supplied; the fraction of the energy supplied that
      becomes useful output = output ÷ input.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] When a load is lifted at constant speed, efficiency = gravitational potential energy gained
      per second ÷ power supplied (no kinetic energy is being gained).
  [ ] Efficiency = (input energy − wasted energy) ÷ input energy; for a machine that is 50 %
      efficient the wasted energy equals the useful output.
  [ ] Overall efficiency of a chain of transfers = final useful energy output ÷ original energy
      input.
  [ ] Find the useful output first where necessary: useful work = weight × height; useful power =
      work ÷ time; time = useful work ÷ useful power.
  [ ] Put both powers (or both energies) in the same unit before dividing.
  [ ] Area of solar panel needed = total energy received ÷ energy received per m².

WHY EFFICIENCY IS BELOW 100 %; WASTED ENERGY; IMPROVING EFFICIENCY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Efficiency is below 100 % because the useful output energy is less than the input energy.
  [ ] Reasons the efficiency of a lifting motor is below 100 % (any two): work is done lifting the
      cage, cable, rope or board as well as the load; energy is transferred thermally in the motor
      (coils, wires); work is done overcoming friction (at the axle or bearings) / air resistance.
  [ ] A motor is less than 100 % efficient because of friction in the bearings, electrical
      resistance in the motor or air resistance: thermal energy (heat) is produced / lost.
  [ ] A lifting method is inefficient when the wheelbarrow or the worker's own body has to be lifted
      as well as the load (in that question friction and air resistance were not credited).
  [ ] A filament lamp has a low efficiency because heat / thermal energy is produced (energy is
      wasted).
  [ ] More efficient: converts more of the input energy / power into useful energy / power – less
      energy is wasted for the same input. Very efficient: most of the energy output is useful and
      little is wasted.
  [ ] An equally bright but more efficient lamp on the same battery: the same light, less heat, so
      less electrical / chemical energy is used (2 marks).
  [ ] Energy lost between the pedals and the wheel of a bicycle: friction in the chain / axles (B1);
      it produces heat (B1).
  [ ] Water turbine: not all the kinetic energy of the water is transferred, because the water is
      still moving after hitting the turbine / some water misses the turbine / energy is lost as
      heat due to friction.
  [ ] Ways a water-driven generator wastes energy (any two): friction in the bearings of the wheel
      or generator, or of the water with the pipes (heat); the water leaving still has kinetic
      energy; heat in the resistance of the generator windings / wires.
  [ ] A calculated efficiency is too large when some thermal energy is lost to the surroundings, so
      that the measured temperature rise is smaller than it should be (B1): the true wasted energy
      and the true energy input are larger than calculated (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] For the same power input a more efficient lamp transfers more energy usefully (light) and
      wastes less as thermal energy; a more efficient solar cell gives the same useful output from a
      smaller energy input.
  [ ] An electric motor wastes energy as thermal energy and sound.
  [ ] Efficiency is increased by reducing the energy losses (for example friction) in the motor.

1.7.5 POWER
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

POWER: DEFINITION, UNIT AND CALCULATION (P = E ÷ t)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Power = work done, or energy transferred, per unit time (B1). P = E ÷ t in any form (C1);
      answer (A1).
  [ ] The watt: a rate of transfer of energy (energy ÷ time) (C1) of 1 joule in one second (A1).
  [ ] A power rating of 100 W means that 100 J (B1) of energy is transferred (converted) every
      second (B1).
  [ ] Use the time in seconds (2 minutes = 120 s), reading it from the graph where necessary.
  [ ] Useful power = useful energy (work done) ÷ time (C1, A1).
  [ ] Average rate of using or releasing energy = energy ÷ time, in W or J/s (C1, A1).
  [ ] Time needed: t = E ÷ P (C1, A1).
  [ ] When only part of the energy is passed on: find that energy (C1); P = E ÷ t with the time in
      seconds (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Constant force, increasing speed: the distance moved in one second increases as the speed
      increases (B1), so the work done in one second – the power – increases (B1).
  [ ] Average power from repeated actions: count the number of times the action is done in the time
      (B1); power = total work ÷ time = work for one action × number ÷ time (B1).
  [ ] The appliance that supplies more useful energy per minute (greater useful power output) heats
      water to boiling first: it transfers energy at a faster rate.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Power = work done ÷ time = energy transferred per second = rate of doing work; force ×
      distance is work, not power.
  [ ] Unit of power: the watt, W = J/s; energy and work are measured in joules.
  [ ] Change the time to seconds before dividing (24 h = 24 × 3600 s); an answer in seconds may need
      changing to minutes.

POWER WHEN LIFTING OR MOVING A LOAD

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Power when lifting: work = force × distance and power = work ÷ time (C1); power = mass × g ×
      height ÷ time, i.e. weight × height ÷ time (A1).
  [ ] Work done = force × distance (C1); P = work ÷ time (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] The power is larger when a load is lifted faster: the same work is done (B1) in a shorter time
      (B1).
  [ ] Measuring the power of a person running upstairs – readings: the mass (or weight) of the
      person (B1); the time taken to run up the stairs (B1); the vertical height of the stairs, or
      the number of steps and the height of each (B1).
  [ ] A precaution for one of the readings: the balance reads zero before the person steps on; time
      with two timers, or repeat and average; hold the rule vertical / the tape taut when measuring
      the height (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Power = weight × vertical height ÷ time = mgh ÷ t; a metre rule (height) and a stop-watch
      (time) are needed to measure the power output when a known load is lifted.
  [ ] For the same distance and time the largest force gives the greatest power; to compare, work
      out m × h ÷ t for each.
  [ ] Use the total mass or total weight lifted.
  [ ] Power of falling water = mgh per second; power output = efficiency × power input.
  [ ] For a horizontal movement, work = force × distance in the direction of the force (the weight
      does no work), then power = work ÷ time.

ENERGY FROM POWER AND TIME (E = Pt); THE KILOWATT-HOUR

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] E = P × t in any form (C1); answer (A1). P = W/t is accepted for the first mark, as is the
      product with unconverted units or a wrong power of ten.
  [ ] Convert first: MW or kW to W, and minutes or hours to seconds; where three marks are given
      they are the formula (B1 or C1), the time in seconds (C1) and the answer (A1).
  [ ] Energy in kW h = power in kW × time in hours: change W to kW (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] The same energy in joules: E = Pt with watts and seconds; 1 kW h = 3.6 × 10⁶ J (C1, A1).
  [ ] Time from energy and power with consistent units: t = E ÷ P, for example kW h ÷ kW gives hours
      (C1, A1).
  [ ] Useful energy = efficiency × power × time.
  [ ] Energy not transferred to the object being heated = energy supplied − energy used.
  [ ] For the energy output in a year use t = 365 × 24 × 3600 s.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy; the watt and kilowatt are units of power (the kN is a
      unit of force).
  [ ] Total power in use = sum of the powers of the appliances that are switched on; energy = total
      power × time.
  [ ] To compare appliances work out power × time for each; work done = power × time.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 GENERAL PROPERTIES OF WAVES
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT A WAVE IS: ENERGY TRANSFERRED WITHOUT TRANSFER OF MATTER

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Wave motion (any two): the transmission of energy; without (net) movement of the medium – no
      transfer of matter; through the vibration (oscillation) of the particles.
  [ ] Showing that a water wave transfers energy: place a cork or small floating object on the
      surface (B1); it moves up and down as the wave passes (B1).
  [ ] An oscillating particle (or floating object) in a wave: its speed increases as it moves
      towards the middle of its movement (1); it is fastest in the middle, decelerates after the
      middle and stops at the top and at the bottom (1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A wave transfers energy in the direction of travel without transferring the water itself: a
      floating ball only moves up and down.
  [ ] Ripples crossing a ripple tank transfer energy by wave motion.
  [ ] A wave that transfers energy to an object loses energy, so its amplitude decreases; its
      frequency, speed and wavelength are unchanged.

WAVE TERMS: AMPLITUDE, WAVELENGTH, FREQUENCY, PERIOD, WAVEFRONT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Amplitude: the maximum displacement from the mean (undisturbed, rest) position. On a diagram
      it is marked from the undisturbed level to the top of a crest or the bottom of a trough – not
      from crest to trough.
  [ ] Amplitude from a graph = maximum height − mean (rest) height, not peak to trough.
  [ ] Wavelength: the distance between two consecutive identical points on the wave (points in
      phase), such as one crest and the next. Two-mark version: the distance between neighbouring
      waves (C1); between neighbouring wavefronts / points of the same phase, crest to crest or
      trough to trough (A1).
  [ ] Wavelength from a diagram = distance spanned ÷ number of complete waves (spaces between
      wavefronts) in that distance; the distance between adjacent crests is one wavelength.
  [ ] Wavelength from a scale diagram: measure across several wavelengths and divide (C1); multiply
      by the scale factor (A1).
  [ ] Frequency: the number of (complete) waves / cycles / oscillations per unit time (per second).
      Two-mark version: the number of waves that pass a point, or are generated by the source (M1 or
      C1), in one second / per unit time (A1).
  [ ] Measuring a frequency: count the number of oscillations (crests passing a point) in a measured
      time, or measure the time for a given number of waves, or find the time for one wave (1);
      divide the number of waves by the time in seconds, or use f = 1/period (1).
  [ ] In that method v = fλ is ignored unless the wavelength and speed measurements are described;
      "find the period" alone is not enough; "frequency is the number of waves per second" with no
      measurement scores 1 mark only.
  [ ] Wavefront: a line (C1 or M1) joining points on the wave that have the same phase / are at the
      same point in their motion, for example a line along a crest (A1).
  [ ] Number of crests (or pulses) produced = frequency × time (C1, A1 where two marks are given).
  [ ] A constant frequency is shown by a constant period: each oscillation takes the same time even
      though the amplitude falls.
  [ ] A wave of smaller frequency at the same speed is drawn with a larger wavelength. To make a
      longer wavelength on the same rope or spring, use a lower frequency (to double the wavelength,
      halve the frequency: one oscillation every 0.5 s instead of 0.25 s). A later scheme accepted
      "move the hand more slowly"; an early scheme did not accept "move the hand slower" without the
      frequency.
  [ ] A wave entering a medium where it travels faster: the frequency does not change (B1); the
      wavelength increases (B1).
  [ ] A decrease in speed (B1) is shown by the wavelength decreasing – crests closer together – or
      by the direction of travel bending towards the normal (B1).
  [ ] Displacement–time graph for a point half a wavelength away: the same period and amplitude
      (B1), but opposite phase – inverted (B1).
  [ ] A wave drawn a time t later has the same shape moved along by the distance v × t in the
      direction of travel; a wavefront moves a distance speed × time.
  [ ] Comparing two waves from their height–time graphs: a difference is the amplitude or the phase
      (B1); a similarity is the frequency / period, or that both are transverse (B1).
  [ ] Values such as the time for the amplitude to fall to half are read from the trace by counting
      complete cycles.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Amplitude = half the vertical distance between a trough and a peak = half the difference
      between the maximum and minimum displacement; it is the height of a crest above the
      undisturbed level.
  [ ] On a displacement–time graph the amplitude is the height of a peak above the zero line and the
      time for one complete cycle is the period; the wavelength can only be read from a
      displacement–distance graph.
  [ ] From one zero crossing to the next, or from a crest to the adjacent trough, is half a
      wavelength; from the first crest to the third crest is two wavelengths.
  [ ] The period is the time for one complete cycle (three cycles in 12 ms give a period of 4 ms).
  [ ] Frequency is the number of waves per second, so in one minute frequency × 60 waves are
      produced.
  [ ] For a water wave the speed is the horizontal distance travelled per second by a peak.
  [ ] A wavefront is a line along a crest, seen from above; wavefronts from a point source are
      circular; wavefronts are perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels.

TRANSVERSE AND LONGITUDINAL WAVES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Transverse wave: the vibration (oscillation) is perpendicular / at right angles to the
      direction of propagation – the direction of travel of the wave or of the energy (B1).
      Longitudinal wave: the vibration is parallel to the direction of propagation (B1).
  [ ] Longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions; transverse waves have crests and
      troughs (credited as the first mark in some schemes).
  [ ] Longitudinal wave, two-mark definition: energy is transmitted through a medium by vibrations
      of the particles – compressions and rarefactions (C1); the direction of vibration is parallel
      to the direction of travel of the wave (A1).
  [ ] Diagrams when asked: a transverse wave showing at least one wavelength (B1); a longitudinal
      wave, for example the coils of a slinky spring, showing at least one wavelength (B1).
  [ ] A point on a spring carrying a longitudinal wave moves backwards and forwards (to and fro),
      parallel to the direction of the wave.
  [ ] Particles of a string, or a water molecule, in a transverse wave oscillate (B1) up and down,
      at right angles to the direction of travel (B1); the arrow is drawn up or down, not along the
      wave.
  [ ] Making a transverse wave on a rope or spring: the hand moves up and down, at right angles to
      the rope (a double-headed arrow) (B1); for a frequency of 4.0 Hz the hand makes 4 complete
      oscillations every second (B1).
  [ ] Compression: a region where the particles (layers of air) are closer together – higher
      pressure than normal (B1). Rarefaction: a region where the particles are further apart – lower
      pressure (B1). On a diagram they are where the lines are closest together and furthest apart.
  [ ] Wavelength of a longitudinal wave: a diagram showing compressions and rarefactions (M1); the
      wavelength marked from the centre of one compression to the centre of the next (A1). Also
      defined as the (shortest) distance between two points in phase.
  [ ] The distance between a compression and the nearest rarefaction is half a wavelength.
  [ ] Sound is longitudinal; a water surface wave and microwaves are transverse; microwaves are
      electromagnetic and sound is not.
  [ ] A wave on a rope is transverse and a compression wave on a spring is longitudinal (both needed
      for the mark).
  [ ] Difference between a sound wave and a water wave: in sound the particles vibrate backwards and
      forwards along the direction of travel – compressions and rarefactions, about 300–330 m/s in
      air (B1); in a water wave the particles move up and down at 90° to the direction of travel –
      crests and troughs, much slower (B1).
  [ ] A single source that generates both longitudinal and transverse waves: an earthquake (seismic
      waves); lightning was also accepted.
  [ ] From seismic P-waves, seismic S-waves, sound and X-rays, the transverse waves are seismic
      S-waves and X-rays.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Light, radio waves, infrared, X-rays and gamma rays, waves on a rope and water ripples are
      transverse; sound and ultrasound are longitudinal, in solids as well as in air.
  [ ] Both longitudinal waves (sound) and transverse waves (surface water waves, light) can travel
      in water; only electromagnetic waves travel in a vacuum.
  [ ] A ball floating on water moves up and down as a transverse wave passes; it is not carried
      along.
  [ ] In a transverse wave each point only moves up and down; a point takes the displacement of the
      part of the wave just behind it, so a point at zero displacement with a crest approaching
      moves up.

THE WAVE EQUATION v = fλ; FREQUENCY AND PERIOD (f = 1/T)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] v = fλ (speed = frequency × wavelength) in any form (C1 or B1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Convert the units first: kHz, MHz or GHz to Hz; cm or mm to m; km/s to m/s (a separate C1 in
      some three-mark questions).
  [ ] Three-mark calculations with powers of ten: the equation with the substitution (C1); the
      correct figures with a wrong power of ten (C1); the correct answer (A1).
  [ ] f = 1/T and T = 1/f: the period is read from the graph or trace as the time for one complete
      wave (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Frequency = number of waves ÷ time taken (C1, A1); number of pulses per second = 1 ÷ time
      between pulses.
  [ ] The time from the top to the bottom of a wave is half a period.
  [ ] The direction of an oscillation reverses twice in every cycle.
  [ ] The smallest wavelength goes with the highest frequency: λ = v/f using the highest frequency
      (C1); answer with its unit (A1).
  [ ] Time for a wave to travel a distance: v = fλ, then t = distance ÷ speed (C1, A1).
  [ ] When a wave slows down (in shallower water, or on entering another medium) the wavelength
      decreases (B1), because the frequency stays the same and v = fλ – v is proportional to λ – or
      because each complete wave travels a shorter distance before the next arrives (B1).
  [ ] The frequency is unchanged in the new region, so λ = v/f with the new speed.
  [ ] Longitudinal wave diagram: measure one wavelength between identical points (for example centre
      of one compression to the centre of the next) (B1); v = fλ with the wavelength in metres (C1);
      answer (A1).
  [ ] Height–time graph for one point on a wave: approximately sinusoidal with the correct amplitude
      measured from the undisturbed level (B1); one complete wave takes the period T = 1/f (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Speed = wavelength ÷ period = fλ: the wavelength comes from a displacement–distance graph and
      the period from a displacement–time graph.
  [ ] The frequency is the same on both sides of a boundary: f = v ÷ λ in the first region, then new
      speed = f × new wavelength (speed is proportional to wavelength).
  [ ] Twice the amplitude means twice the height; half the frequency at the same speed means twice
      the wavelength.
  [ ] Count the number of wavelengths between two marked crests before dividing.
  [ ] For electromagnetic waves f = v ÷ λ with v = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s and the wavelength in metres.

THE RIPPLE TANK; WATER WAVES IN DEEP AND SHALLOW WATER

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Ripple tank: a tank containing water (B1); a dipper or vibrator (a bar or a point) touching
      the surface (B1); a light source with a screen for the projected image, or a stroboscope or
      video, to observe the waves (B1); the dipper moves up and down to create the wave (B1).
  [ ] Making a straight wave: move (vibrate) the bar up and down in the water continuously (1),
      regularly – at a constant frequency (1). "Shake the bar" and "the bar hits / disturbs the
      water" are accepted; "put the bar in the water", "move the support" and "stretch and release
      the bands" are ignored.
  [ ] To increase the frequency: move the bar up and down more often per second (stronger, shorter,
      tighter or more rubber bands; a lighter bar).
  [ ] Frequency increased in the same depth of water: the speed does not change (B1); the wavelength
      decreases (B1). A smaller wavelength is obtained by increasing the frequency of the bar.
  [ ] Shallower water: the speed decreases (B1) and the wavelength decreases; the frequency stays
      constant (B1). From shallow into deep water the wavelength increases, the frequency stays the
      same and the speed increases.
  [ ] To show refraction in a ripple tank: put a piece of plastic or a glass plate in the tank to
      make a shallower region.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Water waves refract because their speed changes when the depth changes; the frequency is
      unchanged and only the speed and wavelength change.
  [ ] Reflection does not change the speed, the wavelength or the frequency of a wave.
  [ ] Where the wavefronts are further apart the wavelength is larger and the wave is travelling
      faster.

DRAWING REFLECTED AND REFRACTED WAVEFRONTS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Reflection at a plane barrier: at least three reflected wavefronts (B1); drawn at the correct
      angle – the same angle to the barrier as the incident wavefronts but sloping the other way,
      angle of reflection = angle of incidence (M1 or B1); with wavelengths equal to each other and
      to the incident wavelength (A1); joined to the incident wavefronts at the barrier (B1).
  [ ] Refraction into shallow water, where the wave is slower: at least three wavefronts in the
      shallow region, straight and parallel to each other (M1 or B1); a smaller, constant wavelength
      (B1); turned so that they make a smaller angle with the boundary – the direction of travel
      bends towards the normal (A1 or B1); joined to the incident wavefronts at the boundary, with
      no extra wavefronts (B1).
  [ ] Refraction into deep water, where the wave is faster: a larger, constant wavelength (B1);
      wavefronts turned so that the direction of travel bends away from the normal (B1); joined at
      the boundary (B1).
  [ ] Wavefronts parallel to the boundary: no change in direction, but the wavelength still changes
      (smaller and constant in the shallow water).
  [ ] A full drawing shows crests parallel to the bar reaching a labelled boundary (B1), the correct
      change of direction (B1), and a different wavelength before and after the boundary that is
      constant within each region (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Plane waves that meet a barrier at an angle and cross the incoming waves on the same side of
      the barrier are being reflected.
  [ ] Circular water waves reflected from a flat barrier appear to come from a point as far behind
      the barrier as the source is in front of it.
  [ ] From deep to shallow water the wavefronts become closer together and the direction of travel
      bends towards the normal.

DIFFRACTION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Diffraction: the spreading out (bending) of a wave at an edge or round an obstacle, or its
      spreading out after passing through a (small) gap or hole.
  [ ] Demonstration in a ripple tank: use a barrier with a slit, two obstacles with a gap between
      them, or the edge of a barrier in the water; the waves bend round the obstacle. The edge, gap
      or slit must be mentioned; "create a hole", "have a gap" and using the original bar as the
      obstacle are ignored.
  [ ] Wavefronts after a gap: at least three curved wavefronts centred on the gap (B1); the same
      wavelength as before the gap, curving the correct way (B1). For a gap smaller than the
      wavelength the crests are semicircles centred on the gap.
  [ ] At the edge of an obstacle: the straight wavefronts continue past the block with the same
      wavelength (B1); they curve into the region behind the block as quarter circles centred on the
      corner (B1).
  [ ] Errors that lose the mark in a diffraction drawing: a changed wavelength (it must stay the
      same); crests in the "shadow" region not curved; central lines drawn too straight.
  [ ] Long wavelengths bend round a hill and short wavelengths do not bend as much (B1), because of
      diffraction (B1); a decrease in wavelength gives less diffraction.
  [ ] Sound heard round a doorway: the wavefronts spread through the gap – diffraction.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Waves spread out after passing through a gap such as an open doorway, so sound reaches places
      that are not in a straight line from the source.

3.2.1 REFLECTION OF LIGHT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LAW OF REFLECTION; ANGLES MEASURED FROM THE NORMAL

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Angle of incidence: the angle between the incident ray and the normal, at the point where the
      ray meets the mirror. Angle of reflection: the angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
  [ ] On a diagram: the normal is drawn at right angles to the mirror at the point where the rays
      meet it (C1); i is marked between the incident ray and the normal and r between the reflected
      ray and the normal (A1); the reflected ray is drawn at an equal angle on the other side of the
      normal.
  [ ] Angle of reflection = angle of incidence. When the angle between the ray and the mirror is
      given: angle of incidence = 90° − that angle (B1); angle of reflection = angle of incidence
      (B1).
  [ ] A ray that strikes a mirror along the normal (angle of incidence 0°) is reflected back along
      its initial path.
  [ ] Seeing an object in a mirror: a ray drawn from the object to the mirror and from the mirror to
      the eye (M1), with the angles of incidence and reflection equal (A1).
  [ ] A mirror at 45° lets a viewer see something overhead while looking horizontally.
  [ ] A mirror behind a lamp reflects light back into the room, so less light is wasted and the room
      is brighter.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The angles of incidence and reflection are measured from the normal, not from the mirror
      surface.
  [ ] The angle between the incident and reflected rays is 2 × the angle of incidence; the ray is
      turned through 180° − 2i from its original direction.
  [ ] Rotating a mirror through 10° rotates its normal through 10°, so the angle of incidence
      changes by 10°.
  [ ] A plane mirror reflects a parallel beam as a parallel beam; the reflected ray stays on the
      same side of the mirror.
  [ ] A mirror at 45° to a ray turns it through 90°. In a periscope the ray from the object is
      turned through 90° by the top mirror and through 90° again by the lower mirror, leaving
      parallel to its original direction; the arrows point from the object to the eye.
  [ ] Light is reflected back along its own path only when it meets the mirror along the normal.

THE IMAGE IN A PLANE MIRROR: CHARACTERISTICS AND POSITION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Characteristics of the image in a plane mirror (any two): virtual; the same size as the object
      (magnification 1); the same distance from the mirror as the object; laterally inverted;
      upright.
  [ ] The image is behind the mirror, on the opposite side to the object (B1), the same distance
      from the mirror as the object (B1); it is marked on the line through the object perpendicular
      to the mirror.
  [ ] Virtual image: it cannot be formed on a screen / there is nothing at the image position (B1);
      the rays do not come from the image – they only appear to come from it (B1).
  [ ] Distance from the object to its image = twice the distance of the object from the mirror.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The image lies on the line through the object at right angles to the mirror (extended if
      necessary), as far behind the mirror as the object is in front – wherever the observer stands,
      and even when the object is beyond the end of the mirror.
  [ ] Moving an object 1.0 m further from the mirror increases the object–image distance by 2.0 m.
  [ ] To find how far an image is from an observer, add the distance of the image behind the mirror
      to the distance of the observer from the mirror.
  [ ] The image is laterally inverted (left and right reversed) but upright.

RAY DIAGRAMS: LOCATING THE IMAGE AND WHAT CAN BE SEEN

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Locating the image with rays: at least one ray drawn from the object to the mirror (M1 or C1);
      two rays with correct reflections, i = r (A1); the reflected rays traced back behind the
      mirror to where they meet (B1); the image marked where the traced-back rays intersect (B1).
  [ ] The image found this way is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, on the
      perpendicular from the object to the mirror.
  [ ] The limit of what can be seen in a plane mirror: a ray drawn from the edge of the mirror to
      the eye (B1); the incident ray to that edge drawn so that the angle of incidence equals the
      angle of reflection (B1).
  [ ] The smallest mirror in which a person sees their whole image: a ray from the top of the head
      to the mirror, reflected to the eye (B1); the mirror must reach from where that ray meets it
      to where the ray from the toes meets it – half the person's height (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A ray from the top of an object reflects from the mirror as though it came from the top of the
      virtual image behind the mirror.
  [ ] Two rays that spread out from a point on the object are still spreading out after reflection,
      as if from the image; reflected rays drawn parallel, or converging to a point at the eye, are
      wrong.
  [ ] An image can be seen only if the straight line from the eye to the image passes through the
      mirror surface.
  [ ] To see your feet the reflecting point is halfway between eye level and the floor; for people
      the same distance from a mirror, the reflection point is midway between the observer and the
      person seen.

EXPERIMENTS: THE LAW OF REFLECTION AND FINDING AN IMAGE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Verifying the law of reflection: a ray box (or laser) and a plane mirror – or pins and a
      mirror (B1); shine a ray at the mirror, or place two pins in front of it (B1); mark the
      incident and reflected rays, or line up two more pins with the images of the first two (B1);
      draw the normal and measure the angles of incidence and reflection – they are equal (B1);
      repeat for other angles (B1).
  [ ] Finding the position of the image: a diagram with the object, mirror and image in roughly the
      correct positions (B1); at least one ray from the object (or ray box) reflected correctly at
      the mirror (B1); at least two reflected rays traced back behind the mirror to where they meet
      – or a search pin moved behind the mirror until there is no parallax between it and the image
      (B1).

3.2.2 REFRACTION OF LIGHT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REFRACTION: DIRECTION, SPEED, WAVELENGTH AND FREQUENCY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Refraction: the change of direction of light as it passes from one medium into another.
  [ ] Why light refracts on entering glass (any two): it travels more slowly / at a different speed
      in glass; it enters an optically denser medium / a medium of different refractive index; its
      wavelength changes while the frequency stays the same; the angle of incidence is greater than
      0.
  [ ] Light entering glass (a denser medium) from air: the speed decreases; the wavelength
      decreases; the frequency does not change. Schemes give one mark each, or 2 marks only when all
      three are stated.
  [ ] Light entering a denser medium deviates towards the normal as its speed decreases; on
      returning to the air the speed returns to its original value (3.0 × 10⁸ m/s).
  [ ] A lens or prism: the light bends towards the normal on entering the glass (B1) and away from
      the normal on leaving it (B1).
  [ ] Evidence that light slows on entering a block: the ray bends towards the normal / the angle of
      incidence is greater than the angle of refraction (B1); the change of direction shows a change
      of speed at the boundary (B1).
  [ ] Light meeting a surface along the normal, angle of incidence 0° (B1), is not refracted – angle
      of refraction 0° – because the whole wavefront slows down together (B1).
  [ ] Wavefronts changing direction on entering glass: light travels more slowly in glass (B1); one
      side of each wavefront reaches the glass and slows down first (B1); so the wavelength
      decreases / that part of the wavefront travels a shorter distance in the same time (B1).
  [ ] Time for light to travel through glass = distance ÷ speed in the glass (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Light passing from glass into air bends away from the normal (the angle of refraction is
      greater than the angle of incidence); its speed and wavelength increase and its frequency is
      unchanged.
  [ ] At an air–glass surface some light is reflected back into the air (angle of reflection = angle
      of incidence) and some is refracted into the glass, bending towards the normal; at a glass–air
      boundary the partially reflected ray obeys the same law and the refracted ray bends away from
      the normal.

NORMALS, ANGLES AND RAY PATHS THROUGH BLOCKS AND PRISMS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The normal is drawn at the point of incidence, perpendicular to the surface, correct by eye
      (B1).
  [ ] The angle of incidence is marked between the incident ray and the normal (B1); the angle of
      refraction is marked between the refracted ray and the normal, inside the glass (B1).
  [ ] Angle of refraction: an angle measured from the normal (C1) – the angle between the refracted
      ray and the normal (A1).
  [ ] When the angle between the ray and the surface is given, the angle of incidence = 90° − that
      angle.
  [ ] The normal to a circular surface is the line through the point of incidence and the centre of
      the circle (M1); the angle of incidence is marked between the ray and this normal (A1).
  [ ] A ray that enters a semicircular block along a radius does not change direction: its angle of
      incidence is 0° – it travels along the normal. A reflected ray that leaves along a radius is
      not refracted either.
  [ ] Parallel-sided block: the ray continues straight inside the block and refracts away from the
      normal on leaving (B1); the emergent ray is parallel to the original incident ray (B1).
  [ ] Path through a prism: in the glass the ray bends towards the normal – it is drawn between the
      normal and the continuation of the incident ray (B1); on leaving it bends away from the normal
      (B1).
  [ ] A second parallel ray entering the same face is refracted along a path parallel to the first
      ray inside the glass (B1), and is then reflected and refracted correctly at the faces it meets
      (B1).
  [ ] A ray that meets the second face at the same angle to the normal as its angle of refraction at
      the first face emerges at the original angle of incidence; the angle between the ray and the
      face is 90° − the angle to the normal.
  [ ] Partial reflection at a boundary: the reflected ray is drawn back into the block with the
      angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The angles of incidence and refraction are both measured between the ray and the normal, not
      between the ray and the surface; the angle of refraction is on the far side of the boundary.
  [ ] A ray that meets a boundary along the normal passes straight on without changing direction.

REFRACTIVE INDEX: n = sin i ÷ sin r

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] n = sin i ÷ sin r, with both angles measured from the normal and the angle in air (the larger
      angle) on top (C1, M1 or B1); the substitution (C1); the answer (A1).
  [ ] Refractive index is also the speed of light in air (vacuum) ÷ the speed of light in the
      medium.
  [ ] Refractive index has no unit; give more than 1 significant figure.
  [ ] Identify the angles from the normal first (C1 in some schemes): i = 90° − the angle between
      the ray and the surface.
  [ ] Finding the angle of refraction: sin r = sin i ÷ n (C1); r, with its unit (A1). Finding the
      angle of incidence: sin i = n × sin r (C1, A1).
  [ ] If the angle between the ray and the surface is asked for, subtract the angle of refraction
      from 90° (the third mark).
  [ ] The angle of refraction in glass can never exceed the critical angle, because the angle of
      incidence can never be bigger than 90° (B1): the largest r is sin⁻¹(1 ÷ n) (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] For light leaving the medium, sin(angle in air) = n × sin(angle in the medium).
  [ ] The deviation of a ray from its original direction on entering the glass is i − r.
  [ ] A graph of sin i against sin r is a straight line through the origin with gradient n.

CRITICAL ANGLE AND TOTAL INTERNAL REFLECTION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Critical angle: an angle of incidence, in the (optically) denser medium (B1); the smallest
      angle of incidence for total internal reflection / the angle of incidence for which the
      refracted ray travels along the surface, angle of refraction 90° (B1).
  [ ] One scheme also gave both marks for "the angle of refraction when the angle of incidence is
      90°" (light entering the denser medium along the surface).
  [ ] On a diagram the critical angle is marked between the ray in the denser medium and the normal
      – not the angle to the surface – for the ray whose refracted ray runs along the boundary.
  [ ] At the critical angle the ray continues along the surface, with a reflected ray inside the
      glass.
  [ ] Total internal reflection: all of the light is reflected back into the denser medium –
      reflection with no refraction out into the air.
  [ ] Conditions for total internal reflection: the angle of incidence is greater than the critical
      angle (B1); the light is travelling from a more dense to a less dense medium / from a higher
      to a lower refractive index (B1).
  [ ] Explaining why no light emerges at a face: the angle of incidence there (give its value,
      measured from the normal) is greater than the critical angle (B1), so total internal
      reflection occurs (B1).
  [ ] Angle of incidence less than the critical angle: the light is refracted out into the air,
      bending away from the normal (B1). Greater than the critical angle: total internal reflection
      with angle of reflection = angle of incidence and no refracted ray (B1). Along the normal: the
      ray passes straight on undeviated (B1).
  [ ] Total internal reflection compared with reflection in a mirror (any two): mirror reflection
      occurs at any angle, total internal reflection only when the angle of incidence is greater
      than the critical angle; total internal reflection occurs in the denser medium, for light
      travelling from dense to less dense; mirror reflection is not total – not all of the light is
      reflected.
  [ ] sin c = 1/n in any form (C1 or B1); answer (A1 or B1). n = 1 ÷ sin c is the same relation (it
      is sin i ÷ sin r with r = 90°).
  [ ] 45° prism, critical angle about 42°: the ray meets the sloping face at 45°, greater than the
      critical angle, so the reflected ray is drawn with i = r and no refracted ray (B1 or M1); it
      strikes the next face perpendicularly and passes into the air undeviated (B1). In a long block
      a second correct reflection is drawn at the opposite surface before the ray leaves through the
      end face (A1).
  [ ] After total internal reflection the ray is drawn with the angle of reflection equal to the
      angle of incidence, travelling to the next face where it leaves the block.
  [ ] Light that meets a water surface along the surface (angle of incidence 90°) (B1) is refracted
      at the critical angle, the largest possible angle of refraction (B1). For light from a source
      at water level reaching an observer under water, the ray runs along the surface and then down
      through the water to the eye (B1), with the critical angle marked between the ray in the water
      and the normal (B1).
  [ ] As the angle of incidence is reduced below the critical angle, light eventually emerges into
      the air (B1); the emerging white light is separated into colours (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A larger refractive index gives a smaller critical angle.
  [ ] The critical angle lies between the largest angle of incidence that still gives a refracted
      ray and the smallest that gives total internal reflection.
  [ ] Total internal reflection at 70° shows that the critical angle is less than 70° and that the
      refractive index of the material is greater than that of its surroundings.
  [ ] Light entering a 45° prism along a normal meets the next face at 45°, greater than the
      critical angle of 42°, so it is totally internally reflected whichever face it enters by.
  [ ] For light leaving a medium along its surface, n = sin 90° ÷ sin c, with the angle measured
      from the normal.

OPTICAL FIBRES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Light in an optical fibre meets the side at a large angle of incidence and is totally
      internally reflected (B1), because the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle (B1).
  [ ] The ray is drawn with repeated reflections along the fibre, the angle of reflection equal to
      the angle of incidence each time.
  [ ] Advantages of optical fibres over copper (metal) wires (one or two as asked): more calls or
      data per second – greater bandwidth, faster; less attenuation (loss of signal), so longer
      distances and fewer repeater (boosting) stations; more secure – harder to tap or hack, no
      cross-talk; less noise / interference; less heat or power wasted; thinner / lighter.
  [ ] Read the question: where it says "other than cheaper and lighter", those two are not credited.
  [ ] Advantage of an optical-fibre link over a satellite link: less likely to be hacked or
      intercepted (more secure); more channels / greater bandwidth / more data; transmission less
      likely to be interrupted; no expensive satellites needed.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The ray stays inside the fibre on a regular zig-zag path: at each wall it is reflected back
      into the fibre with angle of reflection equal to angle of incidence, measured from the normal
      to the wall – it does not pass out through the wall.

EXPERIMENTS: MEASURING THE CRITICAL ANGLE; TRACING A RAY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Measuring the critical angle: a suitable glass or perspex block, for example semicircular
      (B1); a source of rays – a ray box or laser (or pins), labelled (B1); a diagram showing the
      ray inside the block meeting the flat face and refracting out into the air (B1); adjust the
      angle of incidence until the refracted ray travels along the surface / just no longer emerges
      (B1); measure the angle between the incident ray in the block and the normal – this is the
      critical angle (B1).
  [ ] Tracing a ray through a block or prism on paper: mark the points where the ray enters and
      leaves the glass, by marking the rays outside and tracing them to the faces (B1); remove the
      block and join the entry and exit points with a straight line (B1).

3.2.3 THIN LENSES
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LENS TERMS: PRINCIPAL AXIS, PRINCIPAL FOCUS, FOCAL LENGTH; ACTION ON A BEAM

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Principal focus (focal point): the point where parallel rays meet after refraction (C1); full
      statement – the point where rays parallel to the principal axis meet after refraction by the
      lens (A1).
  [ ] Focal length: the distance between the principal focus (focal point) and the lens (M1),
      measured from the (optical) centre of the lens (A1).
  [ ] Both principal foci are marked on the principal axis, one focal length either side of the
      lens.
  [ ] The focal length is read from a diagram as the distance from the centre of the lens to the
      principal focus – the point where a refracted ray that was parallel to the axis crosses the
      axis; on a scale diagram multiply by the scale and give 2 significant figures (C1, A1).
  [ ] A ray entering a lens refracts towards the normal / changes speed (B1); on leaving it bends
      away from the normal / speeds up / its wavelength increases (B1).
  [ ] Rays that pass through the principal focus before reaching a converging lens emerge parallel
      to the principal axis (one ray correct C1; all rays parallel A1).
  [ ] A parallel beam through a converging lens narrows to a point at the principal focus and then
      spreads out again: its width is less than the original at any distance between 0 and twice the
      focal length, and wider than the original beyond twice the focal length.
  [ ] Model of a converging lens made from two prisms and a central glass block: the rays bend
      towards the base of each prism so that the outer rays converge – three correct refractions on
      entering and leaving the prisms, with one wrong refraction at one boundary allowed (B1); the
      central ray passes straight through the block (B1). Dispersion is ignored, but no credit if
      the ray is drawn splitting.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A converging lens bends rays towards the principal axis: a parallel beam is brought to a focus
      at the principal focus, and a diverging beam leaves less divergent or parallel.
  [ ] The focal length is not the object distance or the image distance: it is the distance from the
      lens to where a ray that was parallel to the principal axis crosses the axis.

RAY DIAGRAMS FOR A CONVERGING LENS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The object is drawn to scale as a vertical arrow of the stated height, at the stated distance
      from the lens on the correct side, standing on the principal axis (B1).
  [ ] At least one principal focus is marked at the correct distance and used (B1).
  [ ] Any two construction rays from the tip of the object (B2 or M2): a ray parallel to the
      principal axis, refracted through the principal focus on the far side; a ray straight through
      the optical centre, undeviated; a ray through (or appearing to come from) the principal focus
      on the object's side, emerging parallel to the axis.
  [ ] The image arrow is drawn from the intersection of the refracted rays to the principal axis and
      labelled (B1 or A1). An unlabelled arrow at the image position, either way up, has been
      accepted.
  [ ] Where one mark is given per ray: one correct ray (C1 or M1); a second correct ray meeting the
      first at the image (A1 or M1).
  [ ] Object inside the focal length: the two refracted rays are extended backwards to where they
      meet to locate the image (A1).
  [ ] Finding the focal length from a diagram that shows the object and the image: continue the ray
      parallel to the principal axis from the lens to the tip of the image (B1 or M1); the principal
      focus is where it crosses the axis and the focal length is its distance from the lens (B1 or
      A1); other rays from the top of the object, such as the ray straight through the centre, also
      go to the top of the image (B1).
  [ ] Locating the lens from the object and its image: draw a straight ray from the top of the
      object to the top of the image (B1); the lens is where that ray crosses the principal axis,
      because it passes through the optical centre undeviated (B1).
  [ ] Locating the object from the image: two of the three construction rays used in reverse (M2);
      the object tip is marked where they cross (A1).
  [ ] Values measured from a ray diagram (object distance, image distance, image height) are
      accepted within a tolerance of the calculated value.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A parallel beam at an angle to the principal axis is brought to a focus in the focal plane,
      off the axis and level with the principal focus: the ray through the centre of the lens is
      undeviated and the other rays meet it in that plane.

REAL IMAGES; THE CAMERA AND THE PROJECTOR

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Real image: the rays actually meet at (converge to) the image, so it can be formed on a
      screen.
  [ ] Object beyond 2f: the image is real, inverted (B1) and smaller than the object – diminished
      (B1); on a scale diagram it is between half and the full height of the object.
  [ ] Object between f and 2f: the image is inverted, magnified and real.
  [ ] Object just outside the principal focus: the two refracted rays are almost parallel and meet a
      long way from the lens.
  [ ] Image in a camera, apart from its size: inverted / real / on the other side of the lens from
      the object. The lens in a camera is converging (convex).
  [ ] Converging lens in a camera (any three): light from the object is focused / refracted by the
      lens; focusing is done by moving / adjusting the lens; a real image is formed; on the film /
      digital light detector.
  [ ] Ray diagram for a camera: a straight ray from the top of the object through the centre of the
      lens to the film (B1 or M1); a second ray from the same point refracted to meet the first at
      the film (M1 or A1); the image marked on the film, inverted (A1).
  [ ] Focusing a camera: the lens is moved to produce a sharp image (B1), so that the rays converge
      on the film when the object is at a different distance (C1); for a more distant object the
      image would otherwise form in front of the film, so the lens is moved towards the film (A1).
      When the object moves closer the lens is moved away from the film.
  [ ] Image formed by a projector lens on a wall, besides inverted and magnified (any two): real;
      less bright; further from the lens than the object (beyond 2f).
  [ ] An optical device that gives a magnified real image: a projector / photographic enlarger.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] An object at twice the focal length gives a real, inverted image of the same size on the other
      side of the lens; an object between F and 2F forms its image beyond 2F.
  [ ] A camera forms a real, inverted, diminished image on the sensor.
  [ ] If the rays cross before reaching the screen the image is in front of the screen; a lens of
      longer focal length (less converging) in the same position forms the image further from the
      lens.

THE MAGNIFYING GLASS: VIRTUAL IMAGE OF AN OBJECT INSIDE THE FOCAL LENGTH

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Magnifying glass: a converging (convex) lens with the object within the focal length – closer
      to the lens than the principal focus. The image is upright and virtual.
  [ ] Virtual image: the rays only seem to come from the image / do not actually pass through it.
      Also credited as reasons: the object distance is less than the focal length; the image is
      upright; the rays leaving the lens diverge and, traced back, meet at the image; the rays do
      not meet on a screen.
  [ ] Real and virtual images compared (any two): a real image can be projected on to a screen and a
      virtual image cannot; light actually passes through a real image; a virtual image formed by a
      lens is on the same side as the object.
  [ ] To obtain a virtual image with a converging lens: place the object within the focal length
      (B1) and look through the lens from the other side – the image is on the same side as the
      object (B1).
  [ ] Ray diagram for the virtual image: a ray from the top of the object through the optical
      centre, undeviated (B1); a ray from the top of the object parallel to the axis, refracted
      through the principal focus (B1); both emergent rays extended back as construction lines to
      the top of the image, where the image is marked (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The refracted rays diverge and never meet; traced back they appear to come from a virtual,
      upright, enlarged image on the same side as the object and further from the lens than the
      object.
  [ ] A magnified image formed on a screen is real and inverted, so the object must be between f and
      2f; a photographic enlarger or projector needs a real image, a magnifying glass gives a
      virtual one.
  [ ] The focal length can be found from a magnifying-glass diagram: the ray that arrives parallel
      to the axis passes through the principal focus and, traced back, through the top of the
      virtual image.

LINEAR MAGNIFICATION

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Linear magnification = image height (size) ÷ object height (size); image distance ÷ object
      distance is also accepted (C1 or B1).
  [ ] The value is taken from the heights or distances on the candidate's own ray diagram and is
      accepted within a range around the calculated value (A1). It has no unit; a diminished image
      gives a value below 1.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Magnification = image length ÷ object length = image distance ÷ object distance, for a virtual
      image as well as a real one.
  [ ] Magnifying glass with the object at 2.0 cm and f = 3.0 cm: the virtual image is 6.0 cm from
      the lens on the same side, so the magnification is 3.0.

DIVERGING LENSES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A diverging lens is drawn thinner in the middle than at the edges (a vertical biconcave shape)
      (B1).
  [ ] A parallel beam through a diverging lens: the middle ray through the centre continues in a
      straight line (B1); the other rays diverge away from it (B1).
  [ ] Ray diagram for a diverging lens: the principal focus marked on the axis at the focal length
      from the centre of the lens (B1); any two rays from the tip of the object (B2) – a ray
      parallel to the axis that after refraction seems to come from the principal focus on the
      object's side; a ray through the optical centre, undeviated; a ray aimed at the principal
      focus on the far side that emerges parallel to the axis; the refracted rays traced back to
      where they meet (B1); that point labelled as the image, drawn down to the principal axis (B1).
  [ ] The image is virtual because the light only seems to come from it / does not pass through it;
      it is smaller than the object, upright, and between the object and the lens.
  [ ] To see the image the eye must be on the opposite side of the lens from the object, so that
      light passes through the lens to the eye.
  [ ] Focal length = distance from the principal focus to the centre of the lens; it may be given as
      negative for a diverging lens.
  [ ] Model of a diverging lens made from prisms: each prism is inverted, with its apex towards the
      central block (B1); the rays diverge after the prisms – the top ray upwards and the bottom ray
      downwards (B1). No marks if no prisms are shown or if the rays are drawn splitting.
  [ ] A use of a diverging lens: the correction of short sight.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A diverging lens spreads rays out: it makes a parallel or diverging beam more divergent and a
      converging beam less convergent or parallel.
  [ ] A parallel beam leaves as if from a point in the focal plane on the incident side; for an
      oblique beam that point is where the undeviated central ray crosses the focal plane.
  [ ] The image formed by a diverging lens is always upright, diminished and virtual, on the same
      side as the object and between the principal focus and the lens.

LONG SIGHT AND SHORT SIGHT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] In a normal eye the image of a distant object is sharp (focused) on the retina.
  [ ] Short-sighted eye and parallel rays from a distant object: the central ray passes through
      undeviated (M1); the outer rays meet it at a point inside the eye in front of the retina and
      carry on, diverging, to strike the retina at different places (A1).
  [ ] Short sight is corrected by a diverging lens, drawn thinner at the centre than at the edges
      (B1): the light diverges before entering the eye, so the meeting point is moved back on to the
      retina (B1).
  [ ] An eye that focuses distant objects but not near ones has long sight.
  [ ] Long sight: a near object is blurred because the rays do not converge on the retina – the
      image would be formed behind the retina (B1); on a diagram the rays reach the retina before
      they meet (B1).
  [ ] Long sight is corrected with a converging (convex) lens (B1), drawn in front of the eye so
      that the rays meet on the retina (B1).
  [ ] An image is blurred when the rays do not meet at a point on the retina: light from a single
      point is spread over an area of the retina.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Normal eye: parallel rays from a distant object are focused on the retina; in front of the
      retina is short sight, behind it is long sight.
  [ ] The eye's converging lens forms a real, inverted, diminished image on the retina.

3.2.4 DISPERSION OF LIGHT

DISPERSION BY A PRISM: RAY DIAGRAMS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] White light through a prism: refraction in the correct direction at both faces for every ray
      drawn – towards the normal on entering and away from the normal on leaving, with no incorrect
      rays (B1).
  [ ] The white ray splits into at least two rays at the first face, not only at the second – the
      colours separate inside the glass (B1).
  [ ] Red is deviated least and violet (blue) most; the colours are labelled on the diverging rays
      leaving the prism (B1). For a prism with its apex up, red is at the top of the screen and
      violet at the bottom.
  [ ] Producing a spectrum from a lamp: a prism (B1); a slit to produce a narrow beam (B1);
      dispersion shown at the first or second face – one ray becoming at least two (B1); correct
      refraction at both faces for all the rays shown (B1).
  [ ] A single colour sent through the prism: two correct refractions (M1) with no dispersion (A1);
      red light arrives at the red end of where the spectrum was.
  [ ] Blue light through a prism is refracted in the correct direction at both faces (B1) and
      deviated more than red, so the blue and red rays diverge (B1).
  [ ] Red light sent along the same path as blue: refracted in the same direction at both faces
      (M1), but deviated less than the blue at each face (A1) – refracted less at the first face,
      and away from the normal at the second face so that the two rays diverge.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Dispersion begins where the white light enters the prism and the colours spread further on
      leaving; each colour bends towards the base of the prism at both faces.
  [ ] The ray that lands nearer the top of the screen (deviated less towards the base of the prism)
      is red.

THE COLOURS OF THE VISIBLE SPECTRUM IN ORDER

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The colours of the visible spectrum in order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet
      (B1). Partial credit when two marks are given: all the colours named, or all but one in order
      (C1); the correct order (A1 or B1).
  [ ] On the screen a spectrum – a band of colours, the colours of the rainbow – is seen (B1), in
      the order red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet (B1).
  [ ] The least-deviated end of the spectrum from a prism is red.
  [ ] In order of increasing wavelength: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red – violet
      has the smallest wavelength and red the largest.
  [ ] The colours between red and blue: orange, yellow, green.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Red to violet is also the order of increasing frequency and of increasing deviation by a
      prism.
  [ ] Infrared and ultraviolet are not visible colours.

WHY THE COLOURS SEPARATE: SPEED, REFRACTIVE INDEX, FREQUENCY AND WAVELENGTH

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Dispersion: different colours have different speeds in glass / different refractive indices
      (B1), so they refract through different angles (B1).
  [ ] Blue (violet) light deviates more than red (B1); blue slows down more than red on entering the
      glass (B1); so the refractive index is greater for blue than for red (B1).
  [ ] The refractive index is smaller for red light than for violet light (B1), so for the same
      angle of incidence red has the larger angle of refraction – it bends less towards the normal
      (B1).
  [ ] Both red and violet light travel more slowly in glass than in air (B1); red light travels more
      quickly than violet light in glass (B1).
  [ ] From red to violet the frequency increases and the wavelength decreases (both needed for the
      mark).
  [ ] No spectrum is produced by reflection, because all colours are reflected at the same angle
      (i = r for every colour).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Red light has a lower frequency and a longer wavelength than violet (or blue) light; yellow
      has a lower frequency and longer wavelength than blue.
  [ ] All colours travel at the same speed in a vacuum and in air; in glass red is slowed down less
      and refracted less than blue.
  [ ] The deviation by a prism increases with frequency: green is deviated more than yellow.

3.3 ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

THE REGIONS OF THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM IN ORDER

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The seven regions in order of increasing wavelength (decreasing frequency): gamma rays,
      X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light, infrared, microwaves, radio waves. Typical credit: some of
      the missing regions named correctly (C1 or one mark each); all in the correct order (A1).
  [ ] Changes across the spectrum from radio waves to gamma rays (any two): the wavelength
      decreases; the frequency increases; the energy / ionisation / damage increases. "Radio has the
      largest wavelength" and "gamma has the highest frequency" are accepted; saying that the speed
      changes contradicts; both trends stated with the direction reversed scores 1 mark only.
  [ ] Gamma rays have the greatest frequency and the smallest wavelength.
  [ ] Regions with wavelengths shorter than ultraviolet: X-rays and gamma rays. Shorter than violet
      light (frequencies greater than visible light): ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
  [ ] Between visible light and microwaves: infrared. Between gamma rays and ultraviolet: X-rays.
  [ ] The invisible radiation just beyond the red end of the visible spectrum is infrared and just
      beyond the violet end is ultraviolet (both needed where both are asked).
  [ ] Infrared falls beyond the red end, on the side away from the violet (deviated less than red)
      (B1); a blackened thermometer bulb detects it because black surfaces are good absorbers of
      infrared (B1).
  [ ] The three regions emitted most by the Sun: infrared, visible light and ultraviolet (B1) – this
      is the order of increasing frequency (B1).
  [ ] Red has the largest wavelength of the visible colours; blue light has a shorter wavelength and
      a higher frequency than red light.
  [ ] Errors to look for in a list of the spectrum: infrared and microwaves reversed (infrared has
      the shorter wavelength); visible light or ultraviolet omitted; ultrasound included – it is not
      an electromagnetic wave.
  [ ] X-rays belong to the electromagnetic spectrum.
  [ ] A wavelength just longer than that of red light (7.0 × 10⁻⁷ m) is infrared; the wavelength of
      maximum brightness is read from the peak of an intensity–wavelength graph.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] X-rays and microwaves are electromagnetic waves; sound, ultrasound and water waves are not.
  [ ] Infrared, microwaves and radio waves have longer wavelengths than visible light; ultraviolet,
      X-rays and gamma rays have greater frequencies.

PROPERTIES COMMON TO ALL ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Properties common to all electromagnetic waves (any two): they travel at the same (high) speed
      in a vacuum – the speed of light; they can travel through a vacuum (need no medium); they are
      transverse; they are oscillating electric and magnetic fields; they transfer energy; they can
      be reflected, refracted and diffracted (counts once only). "No mass" and "no charge" are
      ignored.
  [ ] Similarities between infrared and microwaves, besides being electromagnetic (any two): the
      same speed in a vacuum; can travel in a vacuum; travel in straight lines; can be reflected /
      refracted / diffracted; carry energy / cause heating; transverse; both have a longer
      wavelength (lower frequency) than visible light. A difference: the wavelength or frequency –
      infrared has the shorter wavelength and higher frequency.
  [ ] A light wave is electromagnetic and transverse, not longitudinal.
  [ ] Energy from the Sun reaches the Earth as electromagnetic radiation – infrared, light or
      ultraviolet (B1) – which travels through a vacuum and needs no medium, whereas conduction and
      convection need a medium (B1).
  [ ] Thermal radiation is energy transmitted by electromagnetic (infrared) waves, which can travel
      through a vacuum (B1); infrared has a wavelength just longer than visible light, between
      visible light and microwaves (B1). It is produced by hot objects.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum (and in air); they differ in
      frequency and wavelength and most cannot be seen.
  [ ] Infrared is an invisible transverse electromagnetic wave that travels at the speed of light,
      can travel in a vacuum and through gases, is not ionising and has a lower frequency than
      ultraviolet.

THE SPEED OF ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES; CALCULATIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The speed of light – of all electromagnetic waves – in a vacuum is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, and it is
      approximately the same in air (B1 when the value is asked for).
  [ ] Blue and red light have equal speeds in a vacuum.
  [ ] f = v/λ (f = c/λ) or λ = v/f with v = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s (C1); answer (A1). In three-mark versions
      the correct figures with any power of ten earn a C1.
  [ ] Inside a medium use the speed in that medium: λ = v/f with the reduced speed.
  [ ] Time = distance ÷ speed with consistent units – the distance in metres when the speed is in
      m/s (C1, C1, A1).
  [ ] Reflected signals (radar, pulses to the Moon or a satellite): distance = speed × half the
      there-and-back time (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and the lowest frequencies; gamma rays have the
      shortest wavelengths.
  [ ] The speed of light in air is about 10⁶ times the speed of sound in air (3 × 10⁸ ÷ 330).

X-RAY IMAGES

HARMFUL EFFECTS OF ELECTROMAGNETIC RADIATION

  [ ] A microwave oven switches off when its door is opened to prevent microwaves escaping (B1);

COMMUNICATION WITH SATELLITES

3.4 SOUND
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HOW SOUND IS PRODUCED AND TRAVELS: VIBRATIONS, LONGITUDINAL WAVES, COMPRESSIONS AND RAREFACTIONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Motion of the air particles (molecules) in a sound wave: they oscillate / vibrate – backwards
      and forwards, to and fro (B1 or M1); parallel to the direction of travel of the wave (the
      direction of propagation or of energy travel) – longitudinal – or forming compressions and
      rarefactions (B1 or A1). Any clear oscillation earns the first mark; for the second mark "left
      and right" and "up and down" are ignored – the motion must be related to the direction of
      travel of the wave.
  [ ] Sound is a longitudinal wave.
  [ ] Compression: where the particles are close together / the pressure or density is high.
      Rarefaction: where the particles are further apart than average / the pressure or density is
      lower than average (B1).
  [ ] On a diagram a compression is marked at the centre of a region where the particles are closest
      together and a rarefaction at the centre of a region where they are furthest apart.
  [ ] A molecule at the centre of a compression and one at the centre of a rarefaction move in
      opposite directions at any instant.
  [ ] Loudspeaker: the cone (coil) vibrates – moves to and fro (B1); moving forwards it compresses
      the air (a compression) and moving backwards it produces a rarefaction (B1); the air molecules
      next to the cone are made to vibrate, and the vibration / pressure changes are passed on from
      molecule to molecule – a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions travels outwards
      (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Each air molecule oscillates to and fro along the direction in which the wave travels and
      returns to its starting position after one period; it does not travel along with the wave.
  [ ] The pressure is greatest in a compression and lowest (below atmospheric) in a rarefaction. On
      a pressure–distance graph the wavelength is the distance between two adjacent peaks, not the
      height of a peak.
  [ ] The wavelength of a sound wave is the distance from the centre of one compression to the
      centre of the next; a compression to the next rarefaction is half a wavelength, and half a
      period later in time.
  [ ] On a spring, sound is modelled by compressions (coils close together) and rarefactions (coils
      spread out) travelling along the spring.
  [ ] Sound is not an electromagnetic wave.

SOUND NEEDS A MEDIUM

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Sound is transmitted by vibrating matter / particles (B1); in a vacuum there are no particles
      – no medium (B1).
  [ ] Space is a vacuum – there is no medium (C1); sound cannot travel in a vacuum / without a
      medium (A1).
  [ ] Experiment showing that sound needs a medium: a sound source such as an electric bell inside a
      container (bell jar) that can be evacuated (B1), with a vacuum pump connected (B1); with the
      bell ringing the sound is heard, then the air is pumped out (B1 or M1); the sound decreases to
      nothing although the source can still be seen working (B1 or A1).
  [ ] Removing the air makes the sound quieter: there are fewer (or no) molecules, so less (or no)
      vibration is transferred.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Sound cannot travel through the vacuum of space; infrared, light and radio waves can.

THE SPEED OF SOUND IN GASES, LIQUIDS AND SOLIDS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Approximate speed of sound in gases: 150–1000 m/s (B1). In solids: 2000–8000 m/s in one
      scheme, any value from 1000 to 10 000 m/s in others (B1).
  [ ] Typical speeds in a liquid and in a solid both lie between 1000 and 10 000 m/s (B1), with the
      speed in the solid greater than in the liquid (B1).
  [ ] The speed of sound is greater in water than in air (B1), so the sound takes less time to
      travel the same distance through water (B1). The speed decreases on passing from a liquid into
      air.
  [ ] Sound passing from air into glass: the frequency does not change and the speed increases.
  [ ] Time for sound to travel a distance: t = distance ÷ speed (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Sound is slowest in gases, faster in liquids and fastest in solids: about 330 m/s in air,
      1500 m/s in water and 6000 m/s in steel.
  [ ] Sound arrives through a solid (a metal fence) in less time than through the air.
  [ ] When sound passes into another medium its frequency stays constant, so a larger wavelength
      means a larger speed (v = fλ).

MEASURING THE SPEED OF SOUND IN AIR

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Apparatus for measuring the speed of sound in air over a long distance: a tape measure /
      trundle wheel (B1); a stopwatch / timer (B1).
  [ ] Method: start the timer on seeing the smoke or flash from the pistol (B1); stop it on hearing
      the sound (B1); measure the large distance between the two people and use speed = distance ÷
      time (B1).
  [ ] Five-mark description: how the sound is made – starting pistol, clap, hammer on metal (B1);
      the time measured correctly – from seeing the flash to hearing the sound, or between clap and
      echo (B1); the distance measured correctly – from gun to observer, or observer to wall (B1);
      the calculation – speed = distance ÷ time, or 2 × distance ÷ time for an echo (B1); a
      precaution – use a large distance (over 200 m), repeat and average, clap in time with the echo
      and time many claps, or repeat in the opposite direction / when there is no wind (B1).
  [ ] Lightning: thunder is heard after the lightning is seen because the speed of sound is much
      less than the speed of light (B1). The speed of sound is found by measuring the delay between
      seeing the flash and hearing the thunder (B1) and dividing the known distance by it (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The light arrives almost instantly, so distance = speed of sound × time delay, and speed of
      sound = distance ÷ time delay.

ECHOES; REFLECTION AND REFRACTION OF SOUND

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Echo: a reflection of sound – sound that returns after a reflection.
  [ ] Distance to a reflector = speed × time ÷ 2, because the sound travels there and back: d = vt
      in any form earns the first mark (even the unhalved value), the halved answer the second.
  [ ] Speed from an echo = total distance there and back ÷ time (C1, A1).
  [ ] Time for an echo: speed = distance ÷ time (C1); the distance is doubled (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Clapping in time with echoes: number of claps in one minute = 60 ÷ time between claps (C1,
      A1).
  [ ] An echo drawn on a trace appears later on the time axis with waves of the same period (B1) and
      a smaller amplitude (B1).
  [ ] Reflection of sound from a smooth surface: the sound waves are reflected (B1) with angle of
      incidence = angle of reflection (B1); the listening tube is placed on the other side of the
      barrier, pointing at the point of reflection at the same angle to the surface as the first
      tube (B1).
  [ ] Experiment showing that sound obeys the law of reflection: tubes to give a narrow beam of
      sound from the source and/or to the detector (B1); a named detector (microphone, ear) or named
      reflector (hard flat surface) (B1); move the detector to find the position of maximum
      loudness, then measure the angles of incidence and reflection – they are equal (B1).
  [ ] Experiment to show the refraction of sound: a source of sound such as a loudspeaker and signal
      generator (B1); something in which sound travels at a different speed, shaped so that
      refraction can occur, such as a balloon filled with carbon dioxide (B1); a detector such as a
      microphone, moved to show that the sound has changed direction / is concentrated at one place
      (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The time between a sound and its echo is the time for the journey there and back: distance =
      speed × time ÷ 2; depth of the sea = speed × echo time ÷ 2.
  [ ] Clapping in time with the echo: the time between claps equals the time for sound to travel to
      the wall and back; N claps mark N − 1 intervals; average repeated times before calculating.
  [ ] An echo that arrives a time t after the direct sound has travelled an extra distance of twice
      the distance to the wall; for echoes from two walls, speed = difference in the there-and-back
      distances ÷ time difference.
  [ ] The interval between sonar pulses = echo time + the waiting time before the next pulse is
      sent.
  [ ] If each pulse is sent as the echo of the one before returns, the there-and-back time = total
      time ÷ number of pulses.

LOUDNESS, PITCH AND QUALITY; THE RANGE OF AUDIBLE FREQUENCIES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Louder means a larger amplitude (B1); higher pitch means a higher frequency – more waves on
      the screen for the same settings, because the time for one wave is less (B1).
  [ ] A quieter sound: the amplitude of the sound wave (or of the trace) has decreased. A smaller
      maximum voltage gives a quieter sound (B1) because the amplitude of the vibration decreases
      (B1). Larger vibrations of the cone give a louder note of the same frequency.
  [ ] Pitch depends on frequency (M1); low-pitched means low frequency (A1). The pitch stays the
      same when the frequency / period is unchanged.
  [ ] A sound of constant pitch becoming louder: the amplitude increases (B1); the frequency remains
      constant (B1).
  [ ] Comparing two notes from their traces (three points): the same pitch – same frequency /
      period; the one with the larger amplitude is louder; different quality (timbre) – a different
      wave shape, more overtones.
  [ ] Half the frequency on the same oscilloscope settings: a trace with half as many waves (twice
      the period), with at least one complete oscillation shown.
  [ ] Frequency from a trace: count the waves, or find the time for one wave and use f = 1/T, or
      number of waves ÷ time (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Range of audible frequencies for a healthy human ear: about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz).
      Tolerances allowed: lowest 15–25 Hz; highest 15–25 kHz in most schemes (18–22 kHz or up to
      30 kHz in single schemes).
  [ ] A sound is audible if its frequency is within this range; a 3.8 Hz sound is not heard because
      its frequency is too low – below the audible range.
  [ ] Smallest audible wavelength = v ÷ highest audible frequency; longest audible wavelength = v ÷
      lowest audible frequency (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Loudness goes with amplitude, pitch with frequency, and quality (timbre) with the shape of the
      waveform: the same note played on two instruments has the same frequency but a differently
      shaped waveform.
  [ ] A loud, low-pitched note is the trace with the tallest waves and the fewest cycles; a sound
      getting louder with falling pitch has an increasing amplitude and more widely spaced waves.
  [ ] The ratio of the longest to the shortest audible wavelength equals the ratio of the highest to
      the lowest audible frequency (1000 : 1).

ULTRASOUND AND ITS USES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Ultrasound: a sound (longitudinal pressure) wave – compressions and rarefactions (B1 or C1) –
      with a frequency above the limit of human hearing, above 20 kHz, so it cannot be heard (B1 or
      A1). Limits from 15 kHz to 25 kHz have been accepted.
  [ ] Audible sound has a smaller frequency than ultrasound (below 20 000 Hz), and so a larger
      wavelength in the same medium, where the speed is the same.
  [ ] Uses of ultrasound: cleaning; pre-natal and other medical scanning; sonar – calculating depth
      or distance, detecting shoals of fish; quality control / detecting cracks; breaking kidney
      stones.
  [ ] A use with its basic idea: pre-natal scanning (B1) – the ultrasound reflects off the fetus
      (B1).
  [ ] Pre-natal scanning: ultrasound is transmitted into the body (B1); it is reflected (an echo)
      from the baby (B1); the reflections are used to produce an image (B1).
  [ ] Ultrasound meeting a boundary between two materials: some is reflected (B1); some passes into
      the new material (is transmitted) or is absorbed (B1).
  [ ] A reflected ultrasound pulse has a smaller amplitude than the pulse sent out (any two): not
      all the sound is reflected – some passes through the far surface; some energy is absorbed by
      the material; the sound spreads out / is scattered as it travels.
  [ ] Detecting a crack in metal: a crack halfway through gives an extra pulse halfway in time
      between the transmitted pulse and the reflection from the back surface (B1); it is smaller
      than the transmitted pulse and is repeated after each transmitted pulse (B1).
  [ ] Ultrasonic cleaning: the object is immersed in a liquid / solvent (B1); the liquid and object
      are made to vibrate by the ultrasound (B1); the dirt is shaken off / dislodged (B1).
  [ ] To find the distance of an obstacle from the echo time, the speed of ultrasound in the water
      must be known (B1); distance = speed × time ÷ 2 (B1).
  [ ] Transmission of ultrasound through water: the water molecules vibrate (B1); they collide with
      neighbouring molecules (B1), passing on the vibration (energy) as a longitudinal wave of
      compressions and rarefactions (B1).
  [ ] Gas bubbles expand and contract as ultrasound passes because the pressure alternately
      decreases and increases (rarefactions and compressions).
  [ ] Frequency of ultrasound from its wavelength: f = v/λ (C1, A1).
  [ ] Statements to judge correctly: a higher pitch means a larger frequency, not a larger
      amplitude; ultrasound, not X-rays, is used for pre-natal scanning; gamma rays, not microwaves,
      have the highest frequency in the electromagnetic spectrum.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A sound is ultrasound only if f = v ÷ λ (wavelength in metres) comes to more than 20 000 Hz.
  [ ] An ultrasound scanner forms its image from ultrasound reflected at boundaries inside the body.
      Ultrasound is not used for sterilising.
  [ ] Pulse-echo: the extra time for a deeper reflection covers the extra distance there and back,
      so extra depth = speed × extra time ÷ 2.

4.2.1 ELECTRICAL CHARGE
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

POSITIVE AND NEGATIVE CHARGE; FORCES BETWEEN CHARGES

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Like charges repel; unlike (opposite) charges attract.
  [ ] An uncharged object has equal numbers of positive and negative charges.
  [ ] Two spheres repel when both have charge of the same sign.
  [ ] Positively charged particles are attracted to a negative plate and repelled by a positive
      plate.
  [ ] Electrons move on to the object that becomes negative and off the object that becomes
      positive.
  [ ] A positively charged sphere touched by an uncharged metal sphere: the positive sphere becomes
      less positive (B1); the other sphere becomes positively charged (B1); because electrons flow
      from the uncharged sphere to the positive one (B1). The two then repel.
  [ ] Experiment to show that a rod is charged – apparatus: small pieces of paper, a thin stream of
      water, a (gold-leaf) electroscope, or a suspended / pivoted charged rod (B1); observation: the
      paper is attracted / the water stream bends / the leaf rises / the suspended rod is repelled
      or attracted (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A conductor joining a charged and an uncharged metal sphere lets the charge be shared, so both
      end with charge of the same sign.
  [ ] Only repulsion proves the sign of a charge: a rod that attracts a positive strip and repels a
      negative strip is negative; a rod that repels neither strip is uncharged.
  [ ] On two charged conducting spheres the charges move to the sides furthest from each other when
      the charges are alike, and gather on the facing sides when they are opposite.

CHARGING BY FRICTION: TRANSFER OF ELECTRONS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Charging by friction (rubbing): electrons – negative charge – move (B1, C1 or M1), from one
      object to the other in the stated direction (B1 or A1). The first mark is for naming electrons
      / negative charge as what moves; positive charges do not move.
  [ ] The object that gains electrons becomes negative; the object that loses electrons is left
      positively charged (B1).
  [ ] A rod rubbed with a cloth that becomes negative: electrons move from the cloth on to the rod
      (B1); the cloth is left positive (B1). A rod that becomes positive: electrons are transferred
      from the rod on to the cloth, and the cloth becomes negative.
  [ ] Hair rubbed on a balloon: the balloon becomes negative and the hair is left positively charged
      (B1); opposite charges attract (B1).
  [ ] A liquid rubbing against a pipe becomes negatively charged: electrons move (M1) from the pipe
      to the liquid (A1).
  [ ] A person becomes positively charged by friction when he loses negative charge (C1): electrons
      are transferred from him to the surface (A1), which gains the electrons and becomes negatively
      charged (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Rubbing transfers electrons only: the two objects gain equal and opposite charges, which then
      attract. Protons do not move and there are no positive electrons.
  [ ] An aircraft becomes charged by friction with the air.

CONDUCTORS AND INSULATORS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] In a conductor the electrons are free to move; in an insulator they are not (B1). Two-mark
      version: free / mobile / delocalised electrons mentioned (B1); they are present in a metal and
      not present in a plastic (B1).
  [ ] Plastic is an insulator because it has no free electrons – all its electrons are bound.
  [ ] Insulators credited: plastic, perspex, polythene, nylon, glass, rubber, polystyrene.
      Conductors credited: any metal, carbon (graphite).
  [ ] An earthing cable is made of metal because metal is a conductor: it allows charges / electrons
      to flow through it.
  [ ] A charged object must be an insulator (or be held by one) so that the charges do not flow away
      to earth through the person – they stay on it.
  [ ] Charge stays on a rubbed balloon because the balloon / rubber / air is an insulator (C1):
      electrons cannot move through the rubber or the air (A1).
  [ ] Experiment to distinguish a conductor from an insulator: a circuit with a cell, a lamp (or
      ammeter) and the material under test connected in a gap – or a charged electroscope touched
      with the material (M1); the lamp lights / the ammeter reads with a conductor but not with an
      insulator (A1); a named conductor (B1); a named insulator (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Metals are good conductors because they have free electrons that can move.
  [ ] An insulator such as polythene held in the hand gains a large charge when rubbed with wool;
      the charge on a hand-held metal rod flows away through the hand.

INDUCED CHARGES ON A CONDUCTOR; ATTRACTION OF UNCHARGED OBJECTS (EARLIER SYLLABUSES)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A charged rod brought near an uncharged conductor: like charges repel (B1); the electrons in
      the conductor move away from a negative rod to the far side (B1); the positive charges do not
      move, so the side nearer the rod is left positively charged (B1).
  [ ] Drawing the induced charges: signs of the opposite kind to the rod on the side nearest the rod
      and of the same kind on the far side (M1 or B1); equal numbers of positive and negative signs,
      no more than the number shown on the rod (A1 or B1).
  [ ] Two touching conductors near a positive rod: negative charge on the side nearest the rod and
      positive on the far side of the further sphere (M1); equal numbers of each, with none on the
      touching sides (A1).
  [ ] The uncharged sphere is attracted to the rod: the unlike charges on its near side are
      attracted (B1); this attraction is greater than the repulsion of the like charges on its far
      side, because the unlike charges are closer to the rod (B1).
  [ ] A negatively charged rod near a stream of water attracts the positive particles (B1) and
      repels the negative particles (B1), so the side of the stream nearest the rod carries a net
      positive charge (B1).
  [ ] The ground beneath a negatively charged cloud becomes positive: electrons in the ground are
      repelled by the cloud, leaving the surface positive (B1), because like charges repel (B1).
  [ ] An object on the ground below a positively charged cloud becomes negatively charged: electrons
      are attracted by the cloud (B1) and come up from the ground (B1).
  [ ] Conducting grains between charged plates: electrons move towards the positive plate, leaving
      the layer nearest the negative plate positively charged.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] A positive rod attracts electrons to the near side of an isolated metal sphere and leaves the
      far side equally positive; there is no overall charge. Only electrons move.
  [ ] With two touching spheres the rod only separates charge: after the spheres are moved apart
      they carry charges equal in size and opposite in sign.
  [ ] A negatively charged object brought near a wall repels electrons in the wall, leaving the near
      surface positive, so the object is attracted.

EARTHING; CHARGING BY INDUCTION (EARLIER SYLLABUSES)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Earthing: connecting the object to the earth / ground (0 V).
  [ ] Sphere earthed while a negative rod is near: electrons flow from the sphere to earth (B1),
      because they are repelled by the negative charge on the rod (B1); the sphere is left
      positively charged (B1), with the charge only on the side nearest the rod and the far side
      neutral.
  [ ] Metal earthed while a positive rod is near: it gains a net negative charge, on the side near
      the rod.
  [ ] Earth connection removed first and then the rod: like charges repel each other (B1), so the
      charge spreads uniformly over the whole surface of the sphere (B1).
  [ ] Rod removed while the earth wire is still connected: the charge flows to or from earth and the
      metal becomes neutral (B1).
  [ ] Earthing a charged sphere: its charge disappears – it becomes neutral, with equal numbers of
      positive and negative charges.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Charging by induction, in order: bring the charged rod close; earth the sphere (touch it);
      remove the earth connection; then remove the rod. The sphere is left with a charge opposite to
      that of the rod.
  [ ] A positive rod attracts electrons from earth on to an earthed sphere; a negative object repels
      electrons to earth. Protons do not move.
  [ ] Bringing an earthed plate closer to a positively charged plate attracts more electrons on to
      it from earth.
  [ ] A charged rod attracts a stream of water from an earthed tap: the rod induces the opposite
      charge on the water, charge flowing to or from earth through the tap.

ELECTRIC FIELDS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Electric field: a region (of space) in which an electric charge experiences a force.
  [ ] Field between two oppositely charged parallel plates: at least three (four in one scheme)
      straight lines perpendicular to the plates (B1); equally spaced, curving outwards only at the
      edges (B1); arrows from the positive plate to the negative plate (B1).
  [ ] Field between a positive and a negative sphere: at least three lines running from one sphere
      to the other, spreading out as they leave one sphere and coming together at the other (C1 or
      B1); arrows from the positive sphere to the negative sphere (A1 or B1).
  [ ] Field around a negatively charged sphere: at least five straight radial lines outside the
      sphere, perpendicular to its surface, none curved (B1); arrowheads pointing inwards towards
      the sphere, none wrong (B1); lines uniformly spaced at the surface, never crossing or touching
      (B1).
  [ ] Force on an electron between plates: connect a battery / p.d. across the metal plates (B1);
      the electrons are deflected towards the positive plate, away from the negative plate (B1).
  [ ] Positively charged particles are repelled by the positive plate and attracted by the negative
      plate (B1), so they are deflected towards the negative plate (B1).
  [ ] A charged droplet accelerating upwards towards the negative plate: the droplet is positively
      charged (B1); there is a force on it in the direction of the field (B1); this force is greater
      than its weight, so there is a resultant upward force (B1). On touching the negative plate the
      droplet becomes negatively charged (C1) because it gains electrons from the plate (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] The direction of an electric field is the direction of the force on a positive charge; the
      force on a negative charge is opposite to the field.
  [ ] Field lines point away from a positive charge (radial for a point charge) and run from a
      positive charge to a negative charge; the field is strongest on the line joining two opposite
      charges.
  [ ] In a conductor placed in an electric field the free electrons move against the field
      direction.
  [ ] A stationary charge produces, and feels a force in, an electric field and a gravitational
      field; a magnetic field needs a moving charge.

STATIC ELECTRICITY IN PRACTICE: SPARKS, AIRCRAFT, SPRAYING, PRECIPITATORS (EARLIER SYLLABUSES)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Charged spray drops spread out because the drops repel each other (B1): they all have the same
      charge (B1). The paint reaches the back of the object because the drops are attracted by the
      oppositely charged object (B1).
  [ ] Useful static electricity: photocopier, electrostatic precipitator (removing fly ash), spray
      painting, inkjet printing, crop spraying.
  [ ] Describing a use: a sensible example (M1); a simple diagram showing the effect (A1); a
      correctly charged object identified (A1); what the charge does, for example charged paint
      droplets repel each other and are attracted to the earthed / oppositely charged object (A1).
  [ ] Danger of a charged aircraft during refuelling: a spark may jump from it (B1), which can
      ignite the fuel / cause an explosion (B1). Alternative: a current to or from the ground
      through a person (B1) gives a shock (B1).
  [ ] An aircraft is earthed during refuelling so that charge is conducted away along the cable to
      the ground and cannot build up (B1), avoiding sparks that could ignite the fuel (B1).
  [ ] When a positively charged aircraft lands on conducting tyres its charge is neutralised (B1),
      because electrons move up to it from the ground through the tyres (B1).
  [ ] A shock after walking on a carpet: friction between shoes and carpet causes charging –
      electrons move between foot and carpet (B1); the charge then flows between the person and the
      metal handle, discharging the person (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] In an electrostatic precipitator the dust particles gain electrons at a negatively charged
      grid, become negative and are attracted to the positive plates.

4.2.2 ELECTRICAL CURRENT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ELECTRIC CURRENT: RATE OF FLOW OF CHARGE; Q = It

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Electric current: the rate of flow of charge – the charge flowing per unit time (B1).
  [ ] A flow of electrons is an electric current because electrons are charged.
  [ ] Q = It in any form (C1 or B1); answer (A1). Convert first: mA to A; minutes or hours to
      seconds (a separate C1 in three-mark questions). The unit C or coulomb is sometimes a separate
      mark (B1) and is sometimes required for the answer mark.
  [ ] I = Q/t with the time in seconds (C1, A1); t = Q/I (C1, A1).
  [ ] With very small values: Q = It with both quantities in SI units (C1); the correct figures with
      a power of ten (C1); the answer (A1).
  [ ] Number of electrons = total charge ÷ charge on one electron (C1, A1). Number of electrons per
      second = current ÷ charge on one electron; current = number of electrons per second × charge
      on each (C1, A1).
  [ ] In a wire the electrons move towards the positive terminal, opposite to the conventional
      current.
  [ ] The same charge passes when the current is doubled for half the time – the product I × t is
      the same.
  [ ] A smaller current (for example because the resistance of an LDR has increased in dimmer light)
      (B1) means that the time for the same charge to pass increases, t = Q/I (B1).
  [ ] Two e.m.f.s acting in opposite directions: resultant e.m.f. = the difference (B1); I = V/R
      using the resultant e.m.f. (C1, A1); then Q = It (C1) with the time in seconds (C1); answer
      (A1).
  [ ] Battery discharge: current read from the graph (B1); Q = It with the time in seconds (C1, A1);
      energy E = QV (C1, A1); the result is only an estimate because the current / voltage falls as
      the battery runs down, some energy remains in the cells and some is wasted as heat inside the
      cells (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Current = charge ÷ time, so the ampere is a coulomb per second and one coulomb is one ampere
      second.
  [ ] Find the current first where necessary (I = V ÷ R); for the charge delivered by a battery use
      the total current from the battery; change ms, minutes or hours to seconds.
  [ ] The most appropriate ammeter range is the smallest one that includes the calculated current.

CONVENTIONAL CURRENT AND ELECTRON FLOW

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The particles that flow in a metal wire are electrons (M1); they flow away from the negative
      terminal and towards the positive terminal (A1).
  [ ] The free electrons are attracted to the positive terminal / repelled by the negative terminal
      (B1); conventional current is from positive to negative, opposite to the electron flow (B1).
  [ ] Conventional current is in the opposite direction to the movement of negatively charged
      particles such as electrons.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Conventional current flows from the positive terminal of the cell (the longer line of the
      symbol) round the circuit to the negative terminal; the electrons, being negatively charged,
      flow the opposite way.

MEASURING CURRENT: AMMETERS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] An ammeter is connected in series with the component (B1); a voltmeter is connected across –
      in parallel with – the component or the supply (B1).
  [ ] A meter connected in series measures current; a meter connected across a component measures
      voltage (p.d.).
  [ ] Circuit to measure the current in a heater or motor and the p.d. across it: power supply,
      ammeter and the device in series (B1); voltmeter in parallel with the device (B1).
  [ ] A suitable ammeter range runs from 0 to a maximum a little above the reading (for 0.50 A: a
      maximum between 0.50 A and 5.0 A, such as 0–1 A).
  [ ] Multi-range ammeter: start on the highest range and use it for the larger readings (B1);
      switch to a lower, more sensitive range for the smaller readings, keeping the reading on scale
      – use the smallest range that is not exceeded (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] An ammeter (symbol: A in a circle) measures the current in the component it is in series with;
      a voltmeter goes across that same component only.

DIRECT CURRENT AND ALTERNATING CURRENT

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Direct current flows in one direction only – its polarity does not change; alternating current
      repeatedly reverses direction (B1).
  [ ] Alternating current also varies in size (sinusoidally); direct current is steady (B1).
  [ ] Graphs: a sinusoidal voltage–time graph labelled a.c. (B1); the d.c. graph is a horizontal,
      non-zero line (B1); a.c. changes direction (polarity) at regular intervals whereas d.c. is
      constant / never negative (B1).
  [ ] The e.m.f. of a battery compared with an a.c. supply: always positive – its direction does not
      change (B1); constant in size (B1).

4.2.3 ELECTROMOTIVE FORCE AND POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ELECTROMOTIVE FORCE (e.m.f.)

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Electromotive force (e.m.f.): the energy transferred / work done per unit charge (B1 or M1) by
      the source in driving charge round the complete circuit (B1 or A1).
  [ ] The second mark is also given for saying that it is a property of the source (cell, battery,
      power supply) – the energy transferred from other forms to electrical energy per unit charge.
  [ ] e.m.f. and p.d. together: e.m.f. is the work done per unit charge round the complete circuit /
      by the source (B1); p.d. is the work done per unit charge across a component / between two
      points (B1).
  [ ] Similarity: both are energy per unit charge, measured in volts (J/C) with a voltmeter (B1).
      Difference: e.m.f. is energy changed from other forms (for example chemical) to electrical in
      the source (B1); p.d. is energy changed from electrical to other forms (heat, light) in part
      of the circuit (B1).
  [ ] An e.m.f. of 18 V means energy supplied per unit charge (C1): 18 J for each coulomb (A1).
  [ ] Charge delivered = energy ÷ e.m.f. (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] e.m.f. = work done ÷ charge; it is measured in volts (joules per coulomb), not joules.

POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE (p.d.); THE VOLT; W = QV

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Potential difference across a component: the work done / energy transferred (B1, M1 or C1) per
      unit charge passing through the component (B1 or A1) – work done ÷ charge.
  [ ] Energy transferred when a charge moves through a p.d.: E = QV (C1); answer (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] One volt is one joule per coulomb: V = W ÷ Q, so energy = charge × potential difference and
      charge = energy ÷ p.d.
  [ ] Find the charge (Q = It) or the energy (E = Pt) first where necessary; then power = energy ÷
      time and current = charge ÷ time.
  [ ] A bird standing on a high-voltage line is not connected to earth, so there is no potential
      difference across it and no current through it.

MEASURING POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE: VOLTMETERS

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A voltmeter is drawn in parallel with (across) the component whose p.d. is measured (B1).
  [ ] A voltmeter across the only component connected to cells in series reads the sum of their
      e.m.f.s.
  [ ] Choosing a voltmeter range: a range whose maximum is below the p.d. is unsuitable – the
      reading would be off the scale and the meter may be damaged (B1); a range far larger than the
      p.d. is unsuitable – the deflection is too small to read precisely, so the reading is less
      accurate (B1).
  [ ] A suitable range starts from 0 (B1) and runs up to a little more than the expected reading –
      for about 7 V, up to about 8–10 V and no more than 20 V (B1).
  [ ] A voltmeter reads much less than expected when one cell is connected the wrong way round and
      acts against the others (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Potential difference is measured in volts with a voltmeter.

CELLS IN SERIES AND IN PARALLEL

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Cells in series: the total e.m.f. is the sum of the e.m.f.s – number of cells × e.m.f. of one
      cell (C1, A1). The cells must all face the same direction.
  [ ] Number of cells needed in series = battery e.m.f. ÷ e.m.f. of one cell; e.m.f. of each
      identical cell = total e.m.f. ÷ number of cells.
  [ ] Identical cells (sources) in parallel: the e.m.f. equals that of one cell (B1).
  [ ] Adding an identical cell in parallel has no effect on the p.d.s in the circuit (B1), because
      the e.m.f. of identical cells in parallel equals the e.m.f. of a single cell (B1).
  [ ] Advantage of cells in parallel rather than a single cell (one mark): the battery lasts longer
      – runs down more slowly, twice as long for two cells; more energy is available; the supply
      still works if one cell fails; a cell can be replaced without switching off; smaller internal
      resistance. "A greater e.m.f." is not accepted.
  [ ] Two-mark version: they last longer (M1) because more energy is stored / there is a smaller
      current in each cell (A1); or the circuit keeps working if one cell fails (M1) because the
      other cell takes over (A1).
  [ ] Drawing cells in parallel: all the positive terminals are joined together and all the negative
      terminals are joined together.
  [ ] Battery diagrams: the correct cell symbol with the positive terminal (the long line) marked
      (B1); four 1.5 V cells in series, all the same way round, give 6.0 V (B1); eight cells giving
      6.0 V are four in series connected in parallel with another four in series (B1).
  [ ] Four 1.5 V cells giving 3.0 V: two parallel pairs connected in series (B1); the cells must not
      be all in series or all in parallel.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Cells in series add, but a cell connected the opposite way round subtracts: three cells one
      way and one reversed give 3 × 1.5 − 1.5 = 3.0 V.
  [ ] Identical cells in parallel give the e.m.f. of one cell – a lamp keeps its normal brightness –
      but each cell supplies only part of the current and so takes longer to run down.
  [ ] Two cells in series are drawn with the long plate of one next to the short plate of the other.
  [ ] The current leaving a battery equals the current returning to it.

4.2.4 RESISTANCE

RESISTANCE: R = V/I

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Resistance = potential difference (voltage) ÷ current: R = V/I (B1 where the equation is asked
      for).
  [ ] R = V/I, I = V/R or V = IR in any form (C1); answer (A1). In three-mark questions the stated
      formula (B1), the substitution (C1) and the answer (A1) are separate.
  [ ] Use the p.d. across the component itself and the current in that same component: resistance of
      a component = p.d. across it ÷ current in it (C1 or A1); current in a resistor = p.d. across
      it ÷ its resistance (C1 or B1).
  [ ] Put the current in amperes (mA to A) and the resistance in ohms (kΩ to Ω) before substituting.
  [ ] Correct units on the current and resistance answers (A and Ω) can carry a separate mark (B1).
  [ ] Where the current has to be found first from other data, that step carries its own mark (C1);
      then R = V/I (C1); answer (A1). The current must be the one in the component concerned: using
      the current in one lamp where the battery current was needed earned the first mark only.
  [ ] p.d. across one resistor, or across one part of a circuit = the current in it × its resistance
      (C1, A1; B1 where there is one mark); a voltmeter across a fixed resistor reads V = IR.
  [ ] For the whole circuit: current from the supply = e.m.f. (supply p.d.) ÷ total resistance of
      the circuit, I = V/R (C1); answer (A1). In reverse: total resistance of the circuit = e.m.f. ÷
      current from the supply (C1, A1); e.m.f. = current × total resistance of the circuit (C1, A1).
      Identical cells in parallel count as the e.m.f. of one cell.
  [ ] A resistor or lamp connected directly across the supply has the supply p.d. across it: its
      current = supply p.d. ÷ its own resistance (B1 for each current, or C1).
  [ ] In a question with several steps each use of the equation carries a mark: for example the
      current in a fixed resistor from its p.d. and resistance (C1), then the resistance of another
      component from the p.d. across it and the current in it (C1 or A1). Where two routes are
      possible, either the p.d. across one component or the total resistance of the circuit earns
      the method mark.
  [ ] "Show that" a current: write each step with its numbers – p.d. across a resistor = current in
      it × its resistance (B1); current in the resistor with that p.d. across it = p.d. ÷ its
      resistance (B1).
  [ ] A two-step question: V = IR with the current in amperes (C1, A1), then Q = It (C1, A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] For a fixed resistor V ÷ I is constant: for 100 Ω, 1 V gives 10 mA, 2 V gives 20 mA and 3 V
      gives 30 mA.
  [ ] Read the meter, change mA to A, then use V = IR; change kΩ to Ω before using I = V ÷ R.
  [ ] A lightning conductor is made of copper so that it has a low resistance.
  [ ] Whole circuit: current = e.m.f. ÷ total resistance of the circuit; then the p.d. across any
      one resistor = that current × its resistance (10 V with a total of 50 Ω gives 0.20 A, and 0.20
      × 40 = 8.0 V across a 40 Ω resistor). In reverse, total resistance of the circuit = V ÷ I.
  [ ] A resistor connected directly across the battery carries I = battery p.d. ÷ its resistance
      (12 V across 6 Ω gives 2.0 A; across 3 Ω, 4.0 A).
  [ ] p.d. across a resistor = IR (4 A in 6 Ω gives 24 V); another resistor with that same p.d.
      across it carries p.d. ÷ its resistance (24 ÷ 12 = 2 A).
  [ ] A current of a few milliamperes is best read on the range that puts it near mid-scale (4.8 mA
      on 0–10 mA).

OHM'S LAW

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Ohm's law: the current in a conductor is (directly) proportional to the p.d. across it –
      voltage ÷ current is constant (B1 or M1) – provided the temperature / physical conditions
      remain constant (B1 or A1).
  [ ] Ohm's law is obeyed when the current–voltage graph is a straight line through the origin.
  [ ] A lamp does not obey Ohm's law: its graph is curved / not a straight line through the origin /
      the current is not proportional to the voltage.
  [ ] Showing Ohm's law from a table of results: calculate at least two values of V ÷ I (or I ÷ V)
      and show that they are the same.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Ohm's law applies only if the temperature is constant, and only over the straight-line part of
      the I–V graph through the origin.

EXPERIMENT TO DETERMINE RESISTANCE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Circuit: correct symbols for the battery, the resistor (wire or lamp), the variable resistor,
      the ammeter and the voltmeter (B1); the ammeter in series with the component so that it
      measures the current through it (B1); the voltmeter connected across – in parallel with – the
      component (B1).
  [ ] For a lamp alone: cell, lamp and ammeter in series and no other components (B1); voltmeter in
      parallel with the lamp (B1).
  [ ] To obtain a range of readings: a variable resistor (rheostat) in series, a variable power
      supply, a different number of cells, or a correctly connected potential divider (B1).
  [ ] Voltmeter range: 0 to a value just above the largest p.d. needed (12–20 V for readings up to
      12 V) (B1).
  [ ] Readings: read the ammeter and the voltmeter (B1); change the current and repeat (B1);
      calculate R = V/I.
  [ ] One complete set of results for a wire (any two): measure the length of the wire, the p.d. and
      the current; calculate the resistance R = V/I; change the length and measure V and I again.
  [ ] The temperature is kept constant because resistance changes with temperature – and a hot wire
      may melt or burn (B1) – by using small currents, switching off between readings, or putting
      the wire in a water bath (B1).
  [ ] Corrections to a faulty circuit: swap the voltmeter and the ammeter so that the ammeter is in
      series and the voltmeter is across the wire (B1); all the cells must face the same way (B1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] To find a resistance the ammeter is in series with the resistor, carrying only its current,
      and the voltmeter is in parallel across that resistor only; then R = V ÷ I.
  [ ] Choose meter ranges just large enough for the readings: with R ≈ 5 Ω and a p.d. up to 2 V the
      current is up to 0.4 A, so use a 0–2 V voltmeter and a 0–0.5 A ammeter.

CURRENT–VOLTAGE GRAPHS FOR A FIXED RESISTOR AND A FILAMENT LAMP

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Fixed resistor: the current–voltage graph is a straight line through the origin (B1) – the
      current is directly proportional to the p.d. – because its resistance (temperature) is
      constant (B1).
  [ ] Filament lamp: a curved line starting at the origin (C1, M1 or B1), with decreasing gradient –
      curving towards the voltage (p.d.) axis – and a gradient that is always greater than zero,
      never flat or falling (A1 or B1). Where an operating point is given it must be plotted (B1).
  [ ] A graph curving towards the p.d. axis shows that the resistance increases at higher p.d.: the
      gradient decreases, so the current is lower than a proportional increase would give and
      R = V/I is larger (B1).
  [ ] Current not proportional to p.d. is shown by the graph not being a straight line (B1), because
      the filament gets hotter and its resistance increases (B1).
  [ ] A constant temperature is shown by the graph being a straight line / having a constant
      gradient.
  [ ] The resistance of a filament lamp increases over the range where the graph is no longer a
      straight line through the origin.
  [ ] A component with a current–p.d. graph that curves towards the p.d. axis is a filament lamp.
  [ ] If the p.d. is directly proportional to the current the resistance is constant: the
      resistance–current graph is a horizontal line above the axis.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Where the I–V graphs of two lamps coincide the lamps have the same resistance; the lamp that
      carries the larger current at the same voltage has the lower resistance.
  [ ] On a voltage–current graph (voltage on the vertical axis) the gradient is the resistance, so
      the line for a filament lamp gets steeper as the voltage increases.
  [ ] The current in a filament lamp never falls as the voltage rises.

THE DIODE: CURRENT IN ONE DIRECTION ONLY

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] A diode passes current (charge) in one direction only (B1). Accepted instead: it has a very
      high resistance – it acts as an insulator – for current in the other direction (when reverse
      biased); with an alternating supply there is current for only half of each cycle, so the
      current is direct.
  [ ] A diode connected the other way round (reversed): there is no current – no ammeter reading –
      because the reversed diode does not conduct / has a very high resistance / blocks the current
      (B1). A reference to the current–voltage graph of the diode is accepted as the reason.

USING CURRENT–VOLTAGE GRAPHS TO FIND CURRENT, p.d. AND RESISTANCE

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] R = V/I with both values read from the same point of the line (C1); answer (A1), quoted to 2
      or 3 significant figures. On a straight-line graph any pair of values may be used.
  [ ] For a lamp the resistance is found separately at each voltage: for example at a low voltage
      (C1, A1) and again at the working voltage (B1).
  [ ] The current in a lamp connected directly across the supply is read from its graph at the
      supply voltage (B1).
  [ ] The current in a lamp is read from its graph at the p.d. across it (B1); the p.d. across a
      resistor that carries this current = IR (B1).
  [ ] At a stated current, read each p.d. from the graph of that component (B1 each); resistance of
      the lamp at this current R = V/I (C1, A1).
  [ ] At a stated p.d., read each current from the graph of that component (B1 each); resistance of
      each = that p.d. ÷ its current (B1 or C1).
  [ ] Current–temperature graph for a thermistor circuit: read the current at the given temperature
      (C1); V = IR (C1, A1); with the p.d. constant, R = V/I using the current read at the new
      temperature (C1, A1).
  [ ] LDR: the resistance decreases in brighter light (B1), supported by values from the graphs –
      the resistances calculated, a larger current at the same voltage, or less voltage needed for
      the same current (B1).
  [ ] Resistance–length graph: find the resistance of the given length by proportion (B1); then
      I = V/R (C1, A1).

RESISTANCE AND TEMPERATURE: THE FILAMENT LAMP

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The resistance of a metal conductor increases as its temperature increases (M1), approximately
      linearly (A1).
  [ ] The resistance of a filament lamp changes because the temperature of the filament is not
      constant (C1): a higher current / voltage produces a higher temperature (B1) and so a higher
      resistance (B1 or A1). Also accepted as the reason: the particles / ions vibrate more
      vigorously and impede the electron flow.
  [ ] After a lamp is switched on: the temperature of the filament rises / the lamp heats up (1) –
      "the lamp releases heat" is accepted, "heat lost" and "the wires get hot" are ignored; the
      resistance of the filament increases, so the current falls (1) to a constant value. It must be
      an increase: "the resistance is high" is ignored.
  [ ] At a lower voltage the resistance of a filament lamp is (very much) smaller (B1), because the
      filament is cooler (B1); halving the voltage gives more than half the current.
  [ ] As a filament lamp gets brighter its resistance increases (B1), because its temperature
      increases (B1).
  [ ] A filament lamp often fails just after switch-on because of the current surge: the resistance
      is lower when the filament is cold.
  [ ] A series variable resistor turned down slowly protects a filament lamp: when cold the filament
      has a low resistance, so switching on suddenly would give a very high current (B1), which
      could melt the filament (B1).
  [ ] A metal wire (coil) heated or getting warmer: its resistance increases (B1 or M1), so the
      ammeter reading decreases (B1 or A1); the current is larger when the coil is cold.
  [ ] A metal conductor connected in a circuit with a fixed resistor is heated: the voltmeter
      reading across the fixed resistor falls (B1), because the current supplied by the battery
      falls (B1).
  [ ] Graph of the resistance of a metal conductor against temperature: a line of positive slope
      throughout (B1); a straight line with a positive intercept on the resistance axis (B1).
  [ ] Experiment on the resistance and temperature of a wire: a circuit with cell and ammeter in
      series with the wire and a voltmeter across the wire (B1); resistance calculated from R = V/I
      (B1); temperature measured with a thermometer (B1); temperature changed with a water bath /
      oven and the readings repeated (B1).
  [ ] Showing that a resistance does not change linearly with temperature: compare the changes in
      resistance for two equal changes in temperature (C1); they are not the same (A1).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] After switch-on the resistance rises from an initial non-zero value to a steady value while
      the current falls from a high value to a steady value.
  [ ] On a straight-line resistance–temperature graph equal temperature rises give equal increases
      in resistance.

RESISTANCE OF A WIRE: LENGTH AND CROSS-SECTIONAL AREA

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] The resistance of a wire is directly proportional to its length, for a constant
      cross-sectional area (B1), and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area, for a
      constant length (B1).
  [ ] A resistance–length graph that is a straight line through the origin shows the direct
      proportion.
  [ ] A wire of the same material and length with half the cross-sectional area has twice the
      resistance (B1); its current–p.d. graph is a straight line through the origin below the
      original line (M1), with half the gradient (A1).
  [ ] A wire of the same material, length and temperature with a greater resistance has a smaller
      cross-sectional area / diameter.
  [ ] The steeper current–voltage line belongs to the shorter wire, with a reason: its resistance is
      smaller (resistance is proportional to length) / a larger current for the same voltage / a
      smaller voltage for the same current (B1).
  [ ] At constant p.d., as the length increases the current decreases (C1): the current is inversely
      proportional to the length (A1).
  [ ] A thinner wire of the same length and material has a larger resistance (B1), so the power is
      less because the current is smaller at the same voltage (B1).
  [ ] Wires carrying a large current are made thick so that they have a low resistance (B1) and so
      do not get as hot or melt – less energy is lost as heat in the cable (B1).
  [ ] A filament has a higher resistance if it is (two of): longer; thinner; hotter; made of a
      poorer conductor.
  [ ] The same volume of metal with the length halved has double the cross-sectional area (C1); with
      R ∝ length and R ∝ 1/area the new resistance is a quarter of the old (A1).
  [ ] Scaling a resistance: R = old R × (new length ÷ old length) × (old area ÷ new area) – the
      correct figures with any power of ten (C1); answer (A1).
  [ ] Length by proportion: length = resistance ÷ resistance per unit length (B1, B1); for a lamp
      filament use the resistance of the cold filament (the low-voltage value).

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] R ∝ length ÷ cross-sectional area: twice the length and half the area give four times the
      resistance; half the length and twice the area give a quarter; twice the length and four times
      the area give half.
  [ ] Covering a wire with an insulating sleeve does not change its resistance; a larger
      cross-sectional area, a shorter length or a lower temperature all reduce it.
  [ ] Thick, short wires have a low resistance and are used where the current is large; long thin
      wires are adequate where the current is small.
  [ ] On a voltage–current graph the steeper line is the greater resistance.
  [ ] If the length of wire in a circuit is changed, find the new resistance by proportion and then
      the new current from I = V ÷ R with the same p.d.

CHANGING THE RESISTANCE IN A CIRCUIT: THE EFFECT ON CURRENT AND p.d.

  Paper 2 marking points
  [ ] Increasing a resistance in a circuit increases the total resistance of the circuit (B1), so
      the current decreases and the p.d. across a fixed resistor that carries this current (V = IR)
      decreases (B1).
  [ ] When the total resistance of a circuit decreases, the current from the supply increases (B1);
      the p.d. across a fixed resistor that carries this current increases (M1), because p.d. is
      proportional to current for a fixed resistance, V = IR (A1).
  [ ] A larger p.d. across a fixed resistor means that the current in it has increased, I = V/R
      (B1), so the resistance of the variable resistor has decreased – the current is larger for the
      same supply p.d. (B1).
  [ ] To increase the current in a coil (to make an electromagnet stronger): decrease the resistance
      of the variable resistor (B1), which increases the current in the coil (B1).
  [ ] The current in a lamp is smaller (B1) because the total resistance of the circuit is higher,
      or because the lamp has less than the full supply p.d. across it (B1).
  [ ] For the same p.d., the resistor with the smaller current has the larger resistance, R = V/I
      (B1).
  [ ] With the switch open there is no current, so the p.d. across a resistor is 0 V.

  Paper 1 multiple-choice points (explanations, not official marking points)
  [ ] Decreasing the resistance of a variable resistor decreases the total resistance of the
      circuit, so the current increases and a lamp is brighter; the e.m.f. is unchanged. Increasing
      its resistance reduces the current, so lamps are dimmer and a motor turns more slowly.
  [ ] A lamp is brightest when the current in it is largest, that is when the total resistance of
      the circuit is smallest.
  [ ] For the same p.d., the smaller resistance carries the larger current and the larger resistance
      the smaller current (I = V ÷ R).
  [ ] The current leaving a cell equals the current returning to it.
