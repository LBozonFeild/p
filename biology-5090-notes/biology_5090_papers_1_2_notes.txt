1.1 CELL STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION

STRUCTURES FOUND IN BOTH ANIMAL AND PLANT CELLS
- Cell membrane (cell surface membrane) - found in ALL cells (animal, plant, bacterial, fungal).
- Cytoplasm.
- Nucleus.
- Mitochondria.
- Ribosomes.
- Vacuole / vacuoles (small) may be present in animal cells. Reject: "cell sap" or "large vacuole" as a feature common to animal and plant cells.
- A human cheek cell and a leaf (spongy mesophyll) cell both show: cell membrane, nucleus and cytoplasm.
- Cytoplasm is present in a liver cell, a leaf palisade cell AND a red blood cell (a red blood cell has no nucleus, so the nucleus is not common to all three).
- Cell membrane is the only structure common to a bacterial cell, a red blood cell, a liver cell and an onion epidermal cell.

STRUCTURES FOUND IN PLANT CELLS BUT NOT IN ANIMAL CELLS
- (Cellulose) cell wall.
- Chloroplasts. Ignore: "chlorophyll" when a structure is asked for.
- (Large / central / permanent) sap vacuole containing cell sap.
- Starch grains (plant cells store starch).
- Animal cell (e.g. fresh liver) compared with a plant cell (e.g. onion epidermis) under the microscope: a difference in shape (with both cells named); the plant cell has a cell wall, a vacuole, a nucleus pushed to the outside / not towards the centre, and (in green cells) chloroplasts and starch grains - ORA for the animal cell.
- Vacuolar membrane (tonoplast) is credited as a labelled plant-cell feature in drawings.
- A single large sap vacuole identifies a cell seen under the microscope as a plant cell.
- A cell wall (or a large / central / sap vacuole) shows that a root hair cell / root cell is a plant cell. Root cells have NO chloroplasts.
- ALL plant epidermal cells have a cell wall and a nucleus; they do not all have chloroplasts or starch grains.
- In the leaf epidermis only the guard cells contain chloroplasts; the other epidermal cells have a nucleus but no chloroplasts.
- An animal cell is recognised by: no (cellulose) cell wall / only a cell membrane as the outer layer / no (large) vacuole / no chloroplasts.
- Newly formed (dividing) plant cells at a shoot tip differ from mature cells: no chloroplasts, no (sap) vacuole, less cytoplasm.

FUNCTIONS OF CELL STRUCTURES
- Nucleus - contains the cell's genetic material / chromosomes / DNA.
- DNA is the chemical in the nucleus that controls the production of protein. Chromosomes carry DNA; chromosomes are in the nucleus (not free in the cytoplasm) of animal and plant cells.
- Cell membrane - controls the entry and exit (passage) of substances into and out of the cell. It is partially permeable.
- The cell membrane of a living cell prevents a stain from entering (only dead cells take up such a stain).
- Mitochondria - site of aerobic respiration.
- Ribosomes - protein synthesis.
- Chloroplast - photosynthesis / converts light energy to chemical energy / makes glucose / makes starch.
- Chloroplast is a structure / organelle found in plant (leaf) cells; it contains chlorophyll and contains the enzymes for photosynthesis.
- Chlorophyll is a green chemical / pigment; it contains magnesium; it traps / absorbs / converts light.
- During the daytime a chloroplast contains a HIGH concentration of oxygen and a HIGH concentration of magnesium compared with the rest of the cell.
- After a plant has been in bright light, starch grains in the chloroplasts stain blue-black with iodine solution.
- (Sap) vacuole - storage / turgor / support; it contains varying amounts of water, ions and sugars.
- Cell wall - gives the cell its rigid shape / shape / support / turgidity.
- Cell wall - prevents the cell bursting when water enters; it resists turgor pressure. A plant cell with its cell wall removed bursts in distilled water, as animal cells do.
- Cell wall is made of cellulose (plants use cellulose for making cell walls). The cell wall does not contain starch.
- Cell wall is fully permeable (a solution passes through it); the cell membrane is partially permeable.
- Cytoplasm - the region of the cell that contains the nucleus and the other organelles.

IDENTIFYING STRUCTURES ON DIAGRAMS, PHOTOMICROGRAPHS AND ELECTRON MICROGRAPHS
- Cell wall - the outermost boundary line / layer of a plant cell.
- Cell membrane - the line immediately inside the cell wall (in an animal cell it is the outer layer).
- Cytoplasm - the (dotted / granular) region between the cell membrane and the vacuole.
- Sap vacuole - the large clear central space.
- Nucleus - single dark, rounded body lying in the cytoplasm (never inside the vacuole or the wall).
- Chloroplasts - several oval bodies lying in the cytoplasm around the edge of the cell (in an electron micrograph they show internal stacks of membranes).
- Mitochondrion - small oval / sausage-shaped body with a folded inner membrane.
- Ribosomes and mitochondria are NOT visible with a light microscope but are visible in the cytoplasm in an electron micrograph. Accept other correct structures not on the syllabus, e.g. rough ER, Golgi apparatus.
- A cell seen with the electron microscope is an animal cell if its outer layer is the cell membrane (no cell wall).
- In a plasmolysed plant cell the membrane has pulled away from the wall; the solution fills the space between the cell wall and the cell membrane.
- Order of structures crossed by carbon dioxide entering a leaf cell: intercellular (air) space -> cell wall -> cell membrane -> cytoplasm -> chloroplast membrane.

DRAWING AND LABELLING CELL DIAGRAMS (credited features)
- Cell wall drawn OUTSIDE the cell membrane.
- Nucleus drawn in the cytoplasm (must be in the cytoplasm) and labelled; nucleus drawn larger than a chloroplast.
- Vacuole drawn in the middle and larger than the nucleus; cell wall on the outside and nucleus between them.
- Chloroplast drawn in the cytoplasm, in the correct position, and labelled; chloroplasts are drawn only in leaf (photosynthetic) cells.
- Other labelled components credited: cell membrane; cytoplasm / protoplasm; sap / vacuole; tonoplast (vacuolar membrane); nucleolus / nuclear membrane.
- Guard cell (surface view) labels credited: nucleus; vacuole / cell sap; cytoplasm; chloroplast; cell wall; cell membrane; vacuolar membrane / tonoplast. Ignore: mitochondrion / ribosome.
- A label is credited only if the structure is drawn in a plausible position; a label line must end on the structure named (a line for the cell wall must not end on the inner line).

MICROSCOPY AND TEMPORARY STAINING
- Iodine solution stains starch grains blue-black; the cell wall (and the rest of the cell) stays the orange-brown colour of the iodine.
- A stained nucleus becomes visible in cells under the light microscope.
- Mitochondria occupy a share of the cell volume: mean volume of one mitochondrion = (fraction of cell volume occupied x cell volume) / number of mitochondria (e.g. 20% of 11 305 um3 shared by 1500 mitochondria = 1.5 um3 each).

BACTERIAL CELLS
- Bacterial cell structures: ribosomes; circular DNA (one large loop of DNA in the cytoplasm); plasmids; cytoplasm; cell membrane; cell wall.
- Bacteria are single-celled / unicellular.
- No nucleus / no true nucleus / no nuclear membrane (nucleus not membrane-bound); DNA lies in the cytoplasm.
- DNA is circular / a loop / a single chromosome / a strand of DNA; plasmids are also present.
- Plasmids - small rings of DNA that carry genes; a plasmid is another structure (besides the main loop) that contains DNA; the plasmid is the structure used in genetic modification: genes / DNA can be inserted into it and it can pass into a (bacterial) cell - a vector that carries a gene into the cell. The presence of plasmids (with rapid, asexual reproduction by binary fission giving genetically identical cells) is a reason why bacteria are suitable for genetic modification.
- Ribosomes - protein synthesis.
- Cell membrane - controls the passage of substances into and out of the cell.
- Cell wall - shape / support / turgidity; the bacterial cell wall is NOT made of cellulose.
- No (membrane-bound) organelles / no mitochondria; ribosomes are smaller.
- May have flagella / a flagellum (ignore "tail"); may have a (slime) capsule.
- Size: smaller than animal and plant cells / about 1-2 um (0.5-5.0 um) / microscopic.
- Reproduce by binary fission / asexual reproduction; capable of independent existence.
- A cell labelled with cell membrane, cell wall, ribosome, DNA and plasmid (or with a strand of DNA, cell wall and cytoplasm, about 1 um long) is a bacterium.
- Components of a bacterial cell that are also found in animal and plant cells: cytoplasm; cell membrane; ribosomes; DNA / RNA / genetic material / chromosome.
- Bacterial cell compared with animal cell: has a cell wall; no (true) nucleus; different arrangement of DNA, e.g. plasmid; (may) have flagella (ignore "tail") - the flagellum is the external structure used for movement by some bacteria (cilia also accepted); smaller; no mitochondria; smaller ribosomes; slime capsule; capable of independent existence; (an older scheme also credited: bacteria produce spores).
- Features always present in a plant cell but absent from a bacterial cell: nucleus; cellulose cell wall; mitochondria; large vacuole / cell sap.
- To enter a bacterium, a plasmid must pass through the (cell) wall and the (cell) membrane.

LOCATION AND FORM OF THE GENETIC MATERIAL
- Animal (human) cell: DNA is in the nucleus / within a nuclear membrane; it is linear / straight / a thread / strand; arranged as chromosomes; genes / chromosomes are paired.
- Bacterial cell: DNA is in the cytoplasm; it is circular; a thread (loop) plus plasmid(s).
- DNA is found in the nucleus / plasmids / chloroplasts / mitochondria.

COMPARING BACTERIA, VIRUSES AND FUNGI WITH ANIMAL AND PLANT CELLS
Context: classification of organisms is outside this syllabus section; the points below are the cell-structure distinctions credited in mark schemes.
- Virus: not a cell / acellular / not truly living; no cell wall, no cell membrane, no cytoplasm, no nucleus, no ribosomes; has a protein coat (capsid) (reject "protein wall"); contains genetic material that is DNA or RNA; reproduces only inside a living host cell / all viruses are parasites; much smaller than bacteria (below about 300-400 nm; cannot be seen with a light microscope); not affected by antibiotics.
- Bacterium: living cell with cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA (genetic material) but no nucleus; may have a plasmid; affected by antibiotics.
- Both viruses and bacteria contain genetic material (nucleic acid); neither has a nucleus; both can be pathogenic / parasitic.
- Animal cell compared with a virus: animal cell is larger; has a (cell surface) membrane; has a nucleus / nuclear membrane; contains cytoplasm / organelles; genetic material is always DNA.
- Fungal cell (e.g. yeast): has a nucleus (nuclei surrounded by a membrane), cell membrane, cell wall and cytoplasm; no chloroplasts; a bacterium differs from a fungal cell in having no true nucleus. Cell features credited as characteristics of fungi: cell wall (made of chitin); cytoplasm; cell nuclei (more than one nucleus / multinucleate; eukaryotic); hyphae / mycelium.
- Structural features that place an organism in the plant kingdom: chloroplasts; cell walls; multicellular.
- Yeast cells and plant (e.g. grape) cells both have: nucleus; cell membrane; cell wall; cytoplasm. Accept: mitochondria / ribosomes.
- Fungal hypha compared with a palisade cell: several nuclei / not divided into separate cells (palisade: one nucleus per cell, separate cells each with a wall); several small vacuoles (palisade: large central vacuole); stores glycogen (palisade: starch); no chloroplasts / chlorophyll; wall made of a different material (chitin).
- Spherical, unicellular organisms visible with the light microscope, with cytoplasm, a nucleus visible after staining and a vacuole are fungi (yeast) - not bacteria (no nucleus), not viruses.
- Features suggesting a microscopic one-celled organism is plant-like: chloroplast(s) and cell wall.
- A single-celled alga differs from a palisade cell: no vacuole / no cell sap; one (large) chloroplast only; round / spherical cell; the cell is the entire organism / not part of a tissue.

WHAT IS MEANT BY A CELL
- A unit of life.
- Made of nucleus + cytoplasm + membrane.
- Can undergo / is formed by division; a means of transferring genetic information.
- Can be modified (specialised) to do many different jobs.
- Often incapable of independent existence (in a multicellular organism).
- A reference to the significance of cells in any metabolic process is credited (reject a mere list of metabolic processes).

CELL WALL COMPARED WITH CELL MEMBRANE
- Structure: cell wall thick / cell membrane thin; cellulose in the cell wall; fat (lipid) / protein in the cell membrane; the cell wall surrounds / protects the cell membrane (ORA).
- Cell wall is (fully) permeable - water and dissolved salts pass through it by diffusion; it has no control over what enters. Reject: "permeable membrane" for the wall.
- Cell membrane is partially / selectively / differentially / semi-permeable; it controls what enters and leaves; only required substances enter; manufactured substances are exported; it holds / forms the boundary of the cytoplasm.
- Osmosis and active transport take place (only) through the cell membrane: water enters by osmosis (reject "water particles"); salts / ions / minerals or a named ion (reject "particles" / "substances") enter selectively by active transport; against a concentration gradient; using energy; the membrane therefore plays its part in turgor.
- Cell wall is tough / flexible / elastic; supports and protects the cell; maintains its shape; stops the cell bursting / the membrane tearing; allows turgor to develop; helps keep the plant upright.
- Advantage to a plant of having its cell membranes surrounded by cell walls: water enters the cell; by osmosis / diffusion; down a concentration gradient (the cell sap is more concentrated); pressure builds up within the cell (turgor); the cell wall is tough / flexible / made of cellulose; it prevents the cell bursting / the membrane tearing; gives shape / rigidity / support to the cell; and so support to the plant / stem / leaves.
- Root hair cells have both cytoplasm and cell walls (liver cells and red blood cells have no cell wall; mature xylem vessels have no cytoplasm); a root hair cell has a nucleus but no chloroplasts.
- In a bacterial cell two parts take part in making a protein: the DNA / genetic material - contains the gene for the protein / codes for the sequence of amino acids (mRNA); and the ribosome - joins amino acids together to build the protein.
- DNA in animal and bacterial cells: in the nucleus in animal cells but in the cytoplasm in bacterial cells; linear / straight strands in animal cells but circular in bacterial cells.

1.2 SPECIALISED CELLS, TISSUES AND ORGANS

LEVELS OF ORGANISATION
- Cell - the unit of life.
- Tissue - a group of cells that are specialised to perform a specific function; tissues are made up of cells; the cells in a tissue have a common / specific function.
- Organ - made up of (several) tissues working / combining together.
- Increasing order of complexity: (organelle, e.g. chloroplast) -> cell -> tissue -> organ -> organ system -> organism.
- Tissue examples credited: blood, muscle, nervous, epithelial, connective, xylem, phloem, palisade (mesophyll), epidermis. A layer of leaf cells such as the palisade or spongy layer is a tissue / mesophyll (ignore "spongy" / "palisade" / "cells" as the term). Onion epidermis is a tissue.
- Organ examples credited: heart, leaf, flower, root, stem (muscle also accepted as an organ example in one mark scheme).
- The kidney is an organ; an alveolus, a sperm and a neurone are not organs.
- The eye is an organ (the digestive system is an organ system, blood a tissue, a neurone a cell).
- Correct rows: neurone (cell), muscle (tissue), leaf (organ), urinary system (organ system); neurone (cell), vascular bundle (tissue), leaf (organ), excretory system (organ system). A chloroplast and a sap vacuole are not cells; a red blood cell is not a tissue; the liver is not an organ system.
- In a plant: whole leaf = organ; leaf mesophyll = tissue; leaf epidermis = tissue; one palisade cell / one guard cell = cell.
- In the circulatory system: cell = blood cell (or any correctly named one); tissue = muscle (accept blood / capillary); organ = heart / blood vessel or a named vessel (reject capillary). Examples must come from the system named in the question; mark the first in a list.
- In the female reproductive system: egg = cell; lining of the uterus = tissue; ovary = organ; the whole system = organ system.
- An explanation with examples needs one named cell identified as a cell AND one named organ identified as an organ.

SPECIALISED CELLS - STRUCTURE RELATED TO FUNCTION
- Root hair cell - in contact with the soil; takes in water (and ions); long, thin extension gives a large surface area.
- Root hair and villus share a feature that helps absorption: large surface area (to volume ratio) / long / thin.
- Xylem vessel - adapted to carry water (and mineral ions) and to give support; lacks cytoplasm / no cell contents; woody (lignified) cell walls.
- Changes as a young cell develops into a xylem vessel: elongation of the cell (reject "cylindrical"); loss of cell contents / of one named content (accept death of cell / hollow; reject loss of chloroplasts / "dissolving" of contents); loss of end walls; addition of thickening / strengthening material / lignin.
- Red blood cell - adapted to carry (transport) oxygen; lacks a nucleus; it is the blood component that cannot function effectively if the diet lacks iron.
- Ciliated cell (lining of trachea) - the cell bearing cilia; function = mucus removal (the cell without cilia secretes the mucus).
- Palisade cell and spongy mesophyll cell - leaf cells containing chloroplasts (identified among mixed cell diagrams).
- White blood cell and sperm cell are also identified from diagrams of mixed animal and plant cells.
- Sperm cell compared with a cheek (body) cell - how: has a tail (cheek cell has none); less cytoplasm (reject "no cytoplasm"); different shape / length / has a head or acrosome (reject "round"); different position / size / shape of nucleus. Why: tail for swimming / movement / reaching the ovum (accept motility / mobility); absorbs nutrition from seminal fluid; penetrates the ovum; nucleus at the front since only the nucleus enters the ovum; half the amount of (accept less) genetic material / haploid.
- Sperm compared with ovum: sperm smaller / ovum larger (ovum about 120-150 um; sperm about 60 um long with a head about 2.5 x 3 um); the sperm is only a nucleus + tail, whereas the ovum carries some nutrition / cytoplasm / yolk; the sperm is small enough to enter the egg; sperms have a tail / flagellum and swim / are motile (reject "move") to reach the ovum in the oviduct; ova are moved only passively.
- Sperm cell v pollen grain (any six): both are gametes; both haploid / have half the chromosome number of a body cell / are produced by meiosis; both fuse with an egg (fertilisation) to produce a zygote; the sperm cell has a flagellum / tail to move; and enzymes / an acrosome to penetrate the egg; the pollen grain is carried by wind or by an insect; is small / light / sticky / has a sculptured coat; and grows a pollen tube to reach the egg / ovule / ovary.
- A bacterial flagellum has the same function as the tail of a sperm cell: movement / swimming / locomotion.
- Root hair cells are plant cells; sperm and egg cells are animal cells.

- Root hair cell - structure related to function, as separately credited points: long / extension / projection / finger-like; increased / large surface area; uptake of water; by osmosis / diffusion; uptake of ions / mineral salts (or a named ion); by active transport.
- Identifying cells from their contents / outline: cytoplasm + cell membrane but no nucleus = red blood cell; long hollow tube with no contents = xylem vessel; cell with a long thin extension = root hair cell.

- Organs, tissues and cells explained with the eye as the example (max 4): a cell is the basic unit of life; cell example - light receptor / rod / cone; a tissue is a group of cells with a similar structure / function; tissue example - retina / cornea / muscle / sclera / iris / conjunctiva / suspensory ligament; an organ is a group of tissues with a specific function; organ example - the eye / optic nerve.
- Sequence from smallest to largest: gene -> chromosome -> nucleus -> cell -> tissue.

3.1 DIFFUSION AND OSMOSIS

DIFFUSION - DEFINITION AND FEATURES
- Movement of molecules / particles / ions.
- From a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration / down a concentration gradient. Reject: "along" a gradient.
- The movement is random (net random movement) - a result of the kinetic energy of the particles.
- Passive - no energy from the cell / from respiration is required.
- Processes that do not need energy from respiration: movement of oxygen from the alveoli (lungs) into the blood; diffusion of glucose; water entering a cell by osmosis. Processes that do: muscle contraction (e.g. of heart muscle), generation / conduction of nerve impulses, protein synthesis (converting amino acids into proteins), active uptake of ions (e.g. nitrate ions entering a root hair cell against the concentration gradient), production of antibodies by lymphocytes, digestion of food, mitosis, keeping the body temperature constant.
- No membrane is required (but molecules MAY diffuse through a partially permeable membrane).
- Diffusion does not always involve water, does not only happen in solutions / liquids (gases diffuse) and does not only occur in living systems.
- Continues until the concentrations are equal (equilibrium); a gradient is maintained when the substance is used up inside the cell.
- Diffusion and osmosis (not active transport) can continue in a root hair cell when no oxygen is available.

FACTORS AFFECTING THE RATE OF DIFFUSION
- Surface area - a larger surface area gives faster diffusion.
- Temperature - a higher temperature gives faster diffusion (the rate does NOT slow as temperature increases).
- Concentration gradient - a steeper gradient gives faster diffusion.
- Distance - a thinner membrane / shorter diffusion distance gives faster diffusion.
- Features of a gas exchange surface: thin / one cell thick / short distance; moist; large surface area; permeable; these give maximum / more / quick / efficient diffusion (absorption / exchange); gases dissolve / are in solution.
- A thinner blood-gas barrier gives faster / greater gas exchange (diffusion), so more oxygen is supplied / more carbon dioxide removed, allowing more (aerobic) respiration in muscles.
- Oxygen diffuses rapidly into the blood because: thin walls / one cell thick (alveoli / capillaries); relatively high concentration of oxygen in the lungs / more oxygen in alveolar air than in the blood / oxygen regularly replenished; continuous movement of blood; large surface area / rich supply of capillaries; haemoglobin absorbs the oxygen.

EXAMPLES OF DIFFUSION CREDITED
- Carbon dioxide moving into the palisade cells of a leaf; carbon dioxide entering a leaf.
- Oxygen diffusing out of a plant cell - always because the oxygen concentration is higher inside the cell than outside.
- Oxygen entering the blood from an alveolus (alveolar air has the higher oxygen concentration); uptake / loss of carbon dioxide by the blood (a process that occurs by diffusion alone).
- Water vapour passing out of a leaf (through the stomata).
- Water and ions moving through plant cell walls (without crossing a membrane) - both by diffusion.
- A dye entering roots: by diffusion, down a concentration gradient, through the permeable (cellulose) cell walls. Reject: osmosis for the dye.
- Respiration depends on diffusion: substances cross a (thin) membrane; oxygen into the cell; glucose into the cell; carbon dioxide out of the cell.
- Exchange between blood capillaries and surrounding cells: oxygen / glucose from capillary to cells and carbon dioxide / urea from cells to capillary, by diffusion across the cell membrane.
- Across the placenta: the substances the fetus needs (oxygen, glucose, amino acids, salts / ions, vitamins) diffuse from the mother's blood to the fetal blood, and carbon dioxide and urea diffuse the other way; because the necessary substances can diffuse across the placenta, the mother's blood need not (and must not) pass directly into the fetus. Drugs in the mother's blood reach the fetus the same way: by diffusion; across / through the placenta; into the blood of the fetus; along the umbilical cord / umbilical vein (reject umbilical artery).
- A seed germinating while still on the parent plant obtains its water and oxygen by osmosis / diffusion (one correct relevant reference is credited).
- Absorption in the small intestine: digested food molecules must be small / soluble / diffusible to be absorbed (large, insoluble, non-diffusible molecules are not); they pass by diffusion (and active transport) through the thin, one-cell-thick wall / epithelium of the villi / microvilli (reject "cell wall") - glucose and amino acids into the blood capillaries, fatty acids and glycerol into the lacteals / lymph (the lymph is later returned to the blood). Thin walls reduce the distance and so increase the speed of molecular movement; the villi give an increased surface area and have a good supply of capillaries.
- Vitamins need no digestive enzymes: their molecules are small / soluble / already in the simplest form; they are absorbed by diffusion; through the villi / capillary / cell membrane (a partially permeable membrane).
- When the absorbing surface of the small intestine is reduced (shorter / fewer villi; smaller area) there is less diffusion / active transport of glucose / amino acids / fatty acids / glycerol / vitamins / minerals. Conversely, the large surface area given by the villi gives a greater chance of food molecules diffusing into the blood.
- Gas exchange in a leaf (credited in photosynthesis / respiration answers): the gases move by diffusion (credited anywhere in the answer); through the stomata / intercellular air spaces / spongy mesophyll; then through the cell wall to the chloroplast; carbon dioxide in and oxygen out by day (a plant obtains its oxygen for respiration the same way, or from its own chloroplasts); a thin leaf gives a short distance for gases to move; a large surface area (of the leaf / of the cell surfaces) allows more gas exchange / CO2 entry; a reference to the concentration gradients of CO2 / O2 is credited; the air chambers of a floating leaf supply CO2 by diffusion; oxygen made in a green stem under water diffuses out and appears as bubbles.
- In the lungs: an inhaled substance (insulin taken by breathing it in; chemicals from cigarette smoke) reaches the alveoli / air sacs and enters the blood by diffusion into the capillaries; carbon dioxide leaves the blood by diffusion into the alveoli; when the walls of the alveoli are broken down (emphysema) there is less surface area, so less diffusion / gas exchange.
- Carbon dioxide enters a leaf: by diffusion; down a concentration gradient; through the stomata; into the air spaces (in the leaf).
- Processes in a leaf: water passes from the xylem vessel into a mesophyll cell by osmosis; it evaporates from the water layer on the surface of the mesophyll cell; water vapour passes out of the leaf (past the guard cells) by diffusion; carbon dioxide enters the leaf by diffusion.
- Movement of molecules between a blood capillary and the cytoplasm of the surrounding cells: the blood is under pressure; the capillary wall is thin / one cell thick; soluble materials / plasma are squeezed / leak out; oxygen / glucose pass from the capillary to the cells; carbon dioxide / urea pass from the cells to the capillary; by diffusion; across the (partially permeable) cell membrane; any other named soluble substance (e.g. amino acids) with its direction.
- A "disc diffusion" test is so called because the substance (e.g. an antibiotic) moves / diffuses out of the disc into the agar / bacteria.

OSMOSIS - DEFINITION
- Movement (diffusion) of WATER molecules.
- From a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential / down a water potential gradient. Accept: from a dilute to a concentrated solution; from high to low water concentration; from low to high solute concentration; hypotonic to hypertonic.
- Through / across a partially permeable membrane. Accept: selectively / differentially / semi-permeable; Visking tubing / dialysis tubing in a model.
- Passive - no energy required.
- Continues until the concentrations are equal on both sides / equilibrium / no net movement (or until pressure prevents further entry).
- References to "concentration" must say whether it is the water or the solute; "(water moves) down the concentration gradient" alone is ignored in newer mark schemes.
- Only water moves by osmosis - glucose and other solutes do not. Solute (e.g. sucrose) molecules are too large to pass through the membrane; water molecules are small enough.
- Osmosis is a special form of diffusion: it is diffusion of water only and it needs a partially permeable membrane, whereas diffusion needs no membrane; neither needs energy.
- In a diagram of two solutions separated by a membrane: large molecules = solute (sucrose); barrier = partially permeable membrane; small molecules = water; water moves towards the more concentrated solution.
- Osmosis compared with active transport: osmosis is simple diffusion needing no energy / active transport needs energy; water only / ions or larger molecules; down / against a concentration gradient. Both need a partially permeable (cell) membrane.

WATER POTENTIAL
- Distilled (pure) water has the highest water potential (at atmospheric pressure); dissolved solutes lower the water potential.
- A larger negative number (in kPa) is a lower water potential; net water movement between cells is always to the neighbouring cell with the lower water potential.
- Where arrows show water moving between cells, the cell that only loses water has the highest water potential and the cell that only gains water has the lowest.
- In a root, water potential falls from soil water -> root hair cell -> cortex cells -> xylem; a cell in the middle has a lower water potential than the cell outside it and a higher one than the cell inside it.
- Water enters root hair cells from high to low water potential by osmosis: the cytoplasm / cell sap has a lower water potential than the soil water; the root hair cell wall is permeable; the cell membrane is the partially permeable membrane.
- Starch is a better storage substance than glucose because it is insoluble and its molecules are large / compact, so it stays inside the cell / cannot cross the membrane, does not change (lower) the water potential inside the cell and so prevents the cell gaining water by osmosis.

PLANT CELLS IN SOLUTIONS OF HIGHER WATER POTENTIAL (e.g. WATER)
- Water enters the cell / vacuole.
- By osmosis (diffusion), because the cell sap is more concentrated / down the water potential gradient.
- Volume of the vacuole / sap / cytoplasm increases; cell swells.
- Pressure inside the cell rises - turgor (pressure); the cell becomes turgid.
- The cell wall withstands the pressure / prevents the cell from bursting; pressure from the cell wall prevents further expansion.
- A cell whose wall has been removed with an enzyme: water enters by osmosis, the cell swells and BURSTS (ruptures) - there is no cell wall to stop it.
- Cell enlargement during growth: water enters by osmosis from the soil / surrounding cells / xylem across the partially permeable membrane, increasing the volume of sap / cytoplasm.

PLANT CELLS IN SOLUTIONS OF LOWER WATER POTENTIAL (CONCENTRATED SALT / SUGAR SOLUTION)
- Water potential (water concentration) is lower outside the cell than inside (ORA).
- Water moves out of the cell by osmosis / diffusion through the partially permeable membrane.
- Vacuole shrinks / becomes smaller; cytoplasm shrinks; volume of the cells decreases.
- Cell membrane / cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall.
- The cell is plasmolysed (plasmolysis); it is flaccid / no longer turgid / lacks turgor pressure.
- The space between the cell wall and the cell membrane fills with the external solution (salt + water) - because the cell wall is permeable and the solution passes in, but the cell membrane is partially permeable: water can pass through it, other substances (e.g. salt) cannot. Ignore shading of the cell wall itself.
- The only partially permeable structures in a plasmolysed cell are the membranes (cell membrane / vacuolar membrane), not the wall.
- No sugar / salt enters the cell or leaves it.
- A model cell (tubing permeable only to water inside a permeable outer layer) behaves the same way: water leaves by osmosis, the model shrinks, the tubing pulls away from the outer layer. Reject: reference to the vacuole shrinking in the model.
- Pollen grains in a sucrose solution that is too concentrated: lower water concentration / potential outside; less / no water enters or water leaves; by osmosis - so fewer germinate.
- A dried-out plant cell: loss of water (osmosis / diffusion / evaporation); cytoplasm and sap vacuole shrink; membrane pulls away from the wall; no turgor pressure; plasmolysis.

EXPERIMENTS WITH PLANT TISSUE AND SOLUTIONS OF DIFFERENT CONCENTRATION
- Potato pieces gain length / mass in solutions of higher water potential (water enters by osmosis) and lose length / mass in solutions of lower water potential (water leaves).
- A piece that decreases in length was in a solution with a LOWER water potential than the potato cells; changes are due to water movement, not to sugar being taken up or lost.
- The solution in which there is no (or the least) change in length / mass has the same water potential as the potato cells; on a graph this is where the line crosses zero change (or where initial mass : final mass = 1.0).
- Graph of mass (or length) after immersion against sucrose concentration: the mass falls as the concentration increases.
- The strip in pure water shows the greatest increase in length.
- When potato gains mass in a dilute sucrose solution, the process is osmosis and the sucrose solution left behind becomes MORE concentrated.
- Boiled potato does not swell in water - only unboiled (living) tissue placed in distilled water becomes larger.
- Wilting / flaccid tissue: water lost from cells; loss of turgor.

EXPERIMENTS WITH PARTIALLY PERMEABLE (DIALYSIS / VISKING) TUBING
- A concentrated solution (sugar / salt / starch) separated from water by a partially permeable membrane: WATER molecules cross the membrane into the solution; the level of the solution rises; the solution becomes more dilute.
- A more concentrated solution inside the bag (e.g. 0.4 instead of 0.2 mol per dm3 sucrose) makes the liquid rise higher.
- Visking tubing is permeable to small molecules (e.g. glucose) but not to large molecules (e.g. starch).
- Starch + amylase inside Visking tubing standing in water: after an hour the water outside gives a red colour with Benedict's solution (sugar has diffused out) and stays yellow-brown with iodine solution (starch cannot pass through).
- Fat + lipase inside Visking tubing: fatty acids / smaller molecules pass through the tubing by diffusion down a concentration gradient and lower the pH of the water outside.
- Starch suspension inside a membrane bag standing in iodine solution: the starch turns blue-black because iodine diffuses IN through the membrane; starch does not diffuse out (the outside stays yellow-brown).
- Bags of partially permeable membrane in water, the water outside then boiled with Benedict's solution: starch solution alone - blue; reducing sugar solution - orange; starch solution + saliva - orange (sugar formed passes out); saliva alone - blue.
- Starch + amylase inside a membrane permeable to sugar and water but not to starch or protein: water outside gives red with Benedict's, stays blue with biuret (the enzyme, a protein, cannot pass out) and brown with iodine.

DIFFUSION ACROSS AN ARTIFICIAL (DIALYSIS) MEMBRANE - POINTS CREDITED IN KIDNEY-MACHINE QUESTIONS
Context: kidney dialysis itself is not in the listed syllabus sections; only the diffusion / osmosis / membrane points are kept here.
- The membrane / tubing is partially (selectively / semi-) permeable; the blood passes ALONG the tube (reject blood passing through the wall of the tube).
- Small molecules pass through: urea / nitrogenous waste / toxins, salts / ions, (excess) water; they move from the blood into the dialysis fluid.
- By diffusion (osmosis for water), not by active transport - any correct reference; from high to low concentration / down a concentration gradient / reference to the relative concentrations.
- Large molecules / proteins (e.g. insulin) / blood cells do not pass through - the membrane prevents protein molecules diffusing out, so they stay in the blood.
- The membrane is made as many tubes / sheets, coiled / folded / long, giving a large surface area.
- The dialysis fluid has no / low urea (so urea is removed along a concentration gradient) and the same glucose and ion concentrations as normal blood; it keeps the blood at a constant osmotic potential.
- The fluid is continuously replaced / flows counter-current: to maintain the gradient; to prevent a build-up of / ensure removal of waste products (urea, toxins); so that diffusion / exchange continues at its maximum.
- Blood returning from the machine has less / no urea (and a changed ion concentration; lower pressure).

ANIMAL CELLS AND TISSUES
- A red blood cell / animal cell placed in pure water: water enters (water uptake); by osmosis; because the water potential is higher outside / lower inside the cell; across the partially permeable membrane; the cell swells / its volume increases (ignore "turgid"); the membrane cannot withstand the pressure / there is no cell wall; the cell bursts (accept haemolysis).
- Animal cells in a liquid of lower water potential than their cytoplasm lose water and shrink (appear crinkled). A red blood cell in strong salt solution: water moves OUT of the cell and the cell decreases slightly in size (it does not burst and its volume does change).
- Animal cells burst in distilled water but plant cells do not, because the plant cell wall resists the turgor pressure.
- An animal with a partially permeable skin placed in sea water loses water by osmosis; a partially permeable object stored in distilled water loses water to a more concentrated fluid and shrinks.
- Absorption of water in the ileum: by osmosis / diffusion; down a water concentration / water potential gradient; through the villi / microvilli; into the blood / plasma / capillaries.
- Osmosis and diarrhoea (cholera): the toxin makes cells secrete chloride ions into the small intestine; the ions lower the water potential in the gut (accept: make the gut contents hypertonic); water moves out of the cells / into the gut (ignore "out of the blood"); by osmosis, down the water potential gradient / from high to low water potential; the faeces become watery.

WATER PRESSURE AND SUPPORT IN PLANTS
- Water inside the cells, taken up by osmosis, creates pressure / turgor.
- Turgid cells push against one another - this supports the plant (stem / leaves) and keeps it upright.
- The cell wall is tough / flexible and made of cellulose; it gives shape / rigidity / support to the cell and prevents the cell bursting.
- Additional support comes from strengthening material / lignin in the xylem.
- Turgor gives support in thin-walled cells (e.g. a root hair cell) but not in a xylem vessel.
- A plant watered with a concentrated salt solution (or with its roots standing in one): the soil solution has a lower water potential than the cell sap; water leaves the cells / is lost from the plant by osmosis; cells become flaccid / lose turgor / support; the plant wilts (accept drooping; reject withering). Reject: references to the quantity of water rather than its concentration.
- A plant that droops on a hot, dry or windy day is wilting / wilted / flaccid (reject plasmolysed): evaporation / transpiration; water loss faster than water uptake; loss of water from the cells (reject "from the plant"); loss of turgor / flaccidity / pressure (reject plasmolysed); loss of support (reject droop / wilt as the explanation).
- Model of a wilting stem (a net tube packed with air-filled balloons): air = cell sap; balloons = cells; rubber = cell walls; net = epidermis. With every balloon fully inflated the structure is firm; with some air let out it sags (cells that have lost water / turgor give less support).

WATER AS A SOLVENT AND ITS OTHER USES
- Solvent - substances dissolve in it (e.g. sucrose and amino acids carried in the phloem; mineral ions in the xylem; substances carried in blood; urea and other wastes excreted in solution in urine).
- Why water makes up so much of body mass (any three): solvent; a named dissolved substance; cytoplasm is mostly water; in blood / plasma for transport; in urine for excretion; in sweat - evaporation cools the body.
- Transport medium / transporter.
- Medium for chemical reactions / enzyme activity (reject "helps"); activates enzymes in a germinating seed. For germination water is credited as: solvent / for chemical reactions / for transport; activation of enzymes; softening / rupturing the testa.
- Reactant, e.g. in hydrolysis (digestion) and in photosynthesis.
- Much of the content of cells / cytoplasm / blood / the body is water; water lost in sweat / urine / breath must be replaced; prevents dehydration.
- Gives turgor / turgidity (support) and allows cell elongation in plants (reject "growth" unqualified); needed for transpiration, which cools the plant. Uses of water credited for a plant: photosynthesis / turgor / transport / solvent / hydrolysis / enzyme activity ("transpiration" was rejected as a use in one scheme).
- Also credited for water in the diet: temperature regulation / sweating; osmoregulation; lubrication; prevents constipation. (An older mark scheme rejected "simple references to temperature control".)

3.2 ACTIVE TRANSPORT

DEFINITION
- Movement of ions / molecules / particles / substances.
- Against a concentration gradient / from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration.
- Requires energy. Reject: energy "produced".
- The energy comes from respiration (released during respiration in the cell / mitochondria; ATP accepted in older mark schemes).
- Takes place across / through a living cell membrane; needs a living cell. Accept: carrier proteins in the membrane.
- A correct example with its context, e.g. nitrate / magnesium / mineral ions into root hair cells; glucose into the villi of the ileum (accept intestine).
- Active transport occurs in animals as well as plants.
- The cell membrane stops ions that have been taken up from moving back out of the cell.
- Active transport is credited as one named use of energy by an organism - alongside muscle contraction / movement, protein synthesis / making large molecules, temperature control / homeostasis, nerve impulses, growth and cell division - e.g. in a germinating seed, which takes up oxygen for respiration to release this energy, and when explaining energy losses along a food chain.
- Energy is also credited for other cell functions in the same questions: a sperm cell uses energy for the movement / action of its tail (flagellum) - swimming / motility; a palisade cell traps / absorbs light energy for photosynthesis (conversion into chemical energy). One older scheme credited a reference to phagocytosis / endocytosis / exocytosis in the active-transport part of an essay on movement into and out of cells.

ACTIVE TRANSPORT COMPARED WITH DIFFUSION AND OSMOSIS
- Both diffusion and active transport: movement of molecules / ions / particles.
- Diffusion: down a concentration gradient / high to low concentration; passive - uses only the kinetic energy of the particles; no energy from respiration; no membrane or living cell needed.
- Active transport: against a concentration gradient / low to high concentration; energy required from respiration; needs a (living) cell membrane / carrier proteins.
- Active transport is faster than diffusion (as a way of absorbing).
- Osmosis: water only, down a water potential gradient, no energy; active transport: ions or larger molecules, against the gradient, with energy.
- Processes that occur only through a membrane: active transport and osmosis (not diffusion).
- Processes that always occur down a concentration gradient: diffusion and osmosis (not active transport).
- Energy from respiration is used for active transport (and e.g. cell division, muscle contraction) but NOT for diffusion or osmosis.
- The greater the difference against which a substance is moved (e.g. inside 9, outside 3 units), the more energy is needed.
- Not active transport: water entering root hairs (osmosis); carbon dioxide entering a leaf through stomata (diffusion); ions carried in blood plasma; water moving in the transpiration stream.

ION UPTAKE BY ROOT HAIR CELLS
- Mineral ions / salts (or a named ion, e.g. nitrate, magnesium, potassium) are absorbed from the soil (water). Accept: minerals. Reject: nutrients.
- Through the root hairs (root hair cells).
- By active transport / active uptake, because the concentration of the ions inside the root hair cell is higher than in the soil water - uptake continues even when the ions are scarce / at very low concentration in the soil.
- Requires energy from respiration (mitochondria) in the root cells.
- When the concentration outside is higher than inside, ions also enter by diffusion; both processes can operate (e.g. solution 20 and root hair 15 units at the start; 18 and 35 after 48 hours - diffusion first, then active transport).
- In roots absorbing nitrate by active transport and water by osmosis, the nitrate concentration is higher in the roots than in the soil and the water concentration (water potential) is lower in the roots than in the soil.
- Importance of the ions: nitrate - to make amino acids / proteins / DNA, for growth; magnesium - to make chlorophyll (reject chloroplasts), for photosynthesis.
- Oxygen is also absorbed by root hairs, for root respiration.

EVIDENCE THAT ION UPTAKE IS BY ACTIVE TRANSPORT
- Root hairs take up ions more slowly in low oxygen concentrations / uptake of ions increases as oxygen concentration increases - respiration requires oxygen and supplies the energy.
- A chemical (poison) that stops or slows respiration reduces ion uptake: there is less energy for active transport of the ions.
- With respiration stopped, small amounts of ions still enter - by diffusion, not by active transport; uptake by the poisoned plant rises only as the outside concentration rises.
- On a graph of rate of ion uptake against oxygen concentration: at zero / very low oxygen, uptake is by diffusion (passive, not energy-requiring); where the rate rises with oxygen, uptake is by active transport (requires energy; energy from respiration; respiration requires oxygen); where the graph levels off, the rate depends on another (limiting) factor, e.g. temperature. Ignore "against a concentration gradient" as the explanation here.
- An ion whose rate of uptake does not change with oxygen concentration is entering by diffusion; one whose uptake rises with oxygen is entering by active transport.
- Aerating the solution around roots (bubbling air): more bubbles / larger surface area of air; more oxygen; more aerobic respiration; more energy released (reject "produced"); more active transport / absorption of ions (e.g. magnesium); more growth.
- A plant supplied with oxygen grows more: respiration in root cells; increased uptake of ions by active transport, which requires energy; more magnesium -> more chlorophyll -> more photosynthesis; more nitrate -> more amino acids / protein.
- A toxin that slows respiration reduces growth: less energy released; less active transport; fewer ions absorbed / absorbed more slowly; less nitrate -> less amino acid / protein / growth; less magnesium -> less chlorophyll; less photosynthesis; less carbohydrate made.
- Slower nitrate uptake -> proteins made more slowly / slower metabolism -> slower rate of growth.

ACTIVE TRANSPORT IN HUMANS
- Glucose (and amino acids) are absorbed from the gut into the cells of the villi (microvilli) by active transport - against the concentration gradient when the concentration in the small intestine is lower than in the blood.
- Importance in the alimentary canal: absorption / uptake; by the villi; of digested carbohydrate / glucose / amino acids / mineral ions (salts); faster than diffusion; against a concentration gradient; prevents wastage / loss by egestion.
- Evidence: glucose is absorbed faster by living intestine than by dead intestine (active transport), whereas a sugar absorbed at the same rate by living and dead intestine enters by diffusion.
- Evidence: amino acid and glucose absorption falls when oxygen is absent (active transport is involved); fatty acid and glycerol absorption is unchanged without oxygen (mainly diffusion).
- Processes absorbing substances in the small intestine: water by osmosis / diffusion; glucose / amino acids / fatty acids / glycerol by diffusion; glucose (also) by active transport; correct reference to the concentration gradient and to the energy requirement; through the membrane / epithelium; into tissue fluid / capillaries / plasma.
- Uses of the absorbed substances: amino acids for protein manufacture; glucose for respiration (energy release).
- Older mark schemes also credited reabsorption in the kidney (glucose, amino acids, salts) as an example of active transport.
- How energy from the Sun comes to be used for active transport in the alimentary canal (max 7): light energy; photosynthesis; starch / carbohydrate made; the plant / producer is eaten; digested; by enzymes; to glucose; respiration; energy released; used for absorption; movement against a concentration gradient.

SIMILARITIES AND DIFFERENCES: ION UPTAKE BY ROOTS AND GLUCOSE UPTAKE BY VILLI
- Similarities: large surface area / both are elongated structures; active transport / requires energy / against a concentration gradient; substances are in solution; both require a membrane; both also absorb water.
- Differences: root hairs (plants) / villi (humans); root hairs are single-celled / villi are multicellular; uptake is through cell walls in plants (ORA); ions are absorbed from the soil / glucose is absorbed from (in) the intestines / digestive system.

4.1 BIOLOGICAL MOLECULES

CHEMICAL ELEMENTS
- Carbohydrates: carbon + hydrogen + oxygen (accept C, H, O) - all three needed for the mark.
- Fats / lipids: carbon + hydrogen + oxygen only.
- Proteins: carbon + hydrogen + oxygen + nitrogen (ignore other possibilities such as sulfur; sulfur accepted where listed).
- Substances that contain ONLY carbon, hydrogen and oxygen: carbohydrates; fats / lipids.
- Elements present in both fats and proteins: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only; nitrogen is in protein (and urea) but not normally in a fat.
- An amino acid has nitrogen in its structure (fatty acids, glucose and glycerol do not); nitrogen is needed to form protein.
- All enzymes / antibodies are proteins, so always contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen.
- DNA contains carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and phosphorus (as well as oxygen) - cellulose, lipid and starch do not contain nitrogen or phosphorus.
- A tissue with a relatively high percentage of nitrogen: muscle - because it contains much protein.
- Chlorophyll contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and an atom of magnesium.
Context: in one data question the three most common elements by mass in the human body were oxygen, carbon and hydrogen (65 + 18.5 + 9.5 = 93.0 %; the three correct readings earned 1 mark, the total 2).

LARGE MOLECULES MADE FROM SMALLER MOLECULES
- Starch, glycogen and cellulose are made from glucose (a simple sugar); glycogen is a chain of glucose units only.
- Proteins are made from amino acids (an enzyme such as amylase is a large molecule built up from amino acids).
- Lipids (fats / oils) are made from fatty acids + glycerol - both needed.
- DNA is made from nucleotides.
- Cellulose is a long chain of glucose molecules: number of glucose units = length of cellulose molecule / length of one glucose molecule (same units), e.g. 10 000 (1 x 10^4).
- Examples of small molecules built up into larger ones in the body (assimilation): in the liver, glucose -> glycogen; in muscle, amino acids -> protein; under the skin, glycerol + fatty acids -> fat; amino acids -> antibodies.
- NOT building up: proteins -> amino acids and starch -> glucose (these are digestion / breakdown).
- Animal tissues with a high carbohydrate content: liver / muscle - their cells store glycogen.

BREAKDOWN OF LARGE MOLECULES (ENZYME - SUBSTRATE - PRODUCT)
- Amylase: starch -> maltose (accept glucose / reducing sugar / disaccharide where listed; one mark scheme rejected glucose and sucrose as the product drawn for starch).
- Maltose -> glucose.
- Protease: protein (polypeptide) -> amino acids (accept polypeptides / peptides / peptones where listed).
- Lipase: fat / oil / lipid (triglyceride) -> fatty acids + glycerol (accept glycerine / propantriol).
- The same breakdown is credited in germinating seeds (stored food digested) and in decay by bacteria and fungi: enzyme; digest / break down / hydrolysis.

CARBOHYDRATES IN PLANTS (NAMED CARBOHYDRATE LINKED TO ITS USE)
- Glucose - used in respiration; respiration of glucose releases energy.
- Starch - storage.
- Glucose - used to make amino acids / proteins / lipids.
- Cellulose - (to build) cell walls.
- Sucrose - transport (the sugar carried in the phloem); glucose is converted to sucrose for transport.
- A wrongly named additional carbohydrate loses the mark (e.g. "starch and glycogen are stored" in a plant = 0).
- Carbon-containing substances found in plants but not in animals: starch / cellulose / chlorophyll / sucrose.
- Starch is a better storage substance than glucose: insoluble; large / compact / complex molecule (polysaccharide); remains inside the cell / cannot cross the membrane (stored as grains); does not change (lower) the water potential inside the cell; prevents the cell gaining water by osmosis. Accept the reverse argument for glucose. Reject: "glucose is used up".

FOOD TESTS
- Starch: add iodine solution - positive result blue-black (accept black); negative result stays yellow-brown / brown / orange-brown.
- In a plant cell stained with iodine solution the starch grains / chloroplasts containing starch go blue-black; the cell wall is orange-brown.
- Reducing sugar (glucose, maltose): add Benedict's solution and HEAT - positive result an orange / red / brick-red (precipitate); negative result stays blue.
- Benedict's gives a graded colour with increasing glucose content: blue (none) -> green -> yellow -> orange -> red (most).
- Protein: add biuret reagent (cold, no heating) - positive result purple / violet / mauve / lilac; negative result stays (pale) blue.
- Fat / oil: ethanol emulsion test - add (dissolve the sample in) ethanol, then add to water - positive result a white / cloudy / milky emulsion; negative result stays clear / colourless.
- An enzyme solution (e.g. amylase, lipase, the enzyme in biological washing powder) gives a positive biuret test, because enzymes are proteins; amylase solution alone is blue with Benedict's and brown with iodine.
- A protein solution: iodine brown; Benedict's blue; biuret purple.
- Interpreting several tests on one sample: each food type is present only if its own test is positive (e.g. Benedict's red + biuret purple + iodine yellow-brown = reducing sugar and protein only; iodine blue-black + Benedict's blue + biuret violet = starch and protein).
- A food containing carbohydrate, fat and protein may show: iodine blue-black (starch is the carbohydrate), biuret violet, ethanol emulsion milky - even when Benedict's stays blue.
- Urine containing protein and glucose: Benedict's red; biuret mauve (purple); ethanol test colourless; iodine yellow-brown.
- A seed nutrient broken down by lipase (oil) is detected by the ethanol emulsion test.
- Lipase + oil inside a partially permeable bag in water: biuret is purple inside the bag (enzyme) but blue in the water outside; the pH of the water falls (e.g. to 5.5) as fatty acids pass out.
- Testing the contents of plant transport cells: the contents of a xylem vessel give negative results with both Benedict's solution and iodine solution; in the newer papers the contents of a phloem cell give a positive Benedict's result and a negative iodine result (sugars present, no starch). An older (2008) key expected negative results for the phloem cell as well - the later keys take priority.

5.1 ENZYME ACTION

CATALYST
- Speeds up / increases the rate of a (chemical) reaction.
- Is not changed by the reaction / is not used up / can be reused (only small amounts are needed).
- Accept (one mark scheme): lowers the activation energy of the reaction.

ENZYMES
- An enzyme is a protein.
- It is a (biological) catalyst - it speeds up (alters the rate of) chemical reactions.
- It is not changed itself / not used up / can be reused: after a reaction it is available to catalyse the same reaction again.
- Enzymes are made by / work in living cells; they are involved in ALL metabolic reactions.
- Structural features: made of protein / polypeptides / (chains of) amino acids; have an active site; have a specific shape / a shape complementary to the substrate.
- Enzymes change the rate of a reaction; they do not start reactions that would not otherwise occur and do not change the end products.
- Not true of ALL enzymes: secreted into the alimentary canal; work only inside cells; work only at body temperature.
- An enzyme supplement / washing powder ingredient is an enzyme / protein / catalyst; a named enzyme such as maltase is an example of a biological catalyst (bile, DNA and insulin are not).
- The enzymes used to cut and rejoin DNA in genetic engineering were credited simply as "enzyme" (or named) / catalyst.
- Enzymes are made from amino acids / are proteins - so a plant needs nitrate ions to produce the enzymes in its leaf cells.
- Enzymes inside microorganisms carry out the changes used in food production: enzymes (lactase) in bacteria convert lactose (milk sugar) to lactic acid in yoghurt-making and digest milk proteins; a reference to enzymes is credited for yeast in wine- and bread-making.

SUBSTRATE, ACTIVE SITE, ENZYME-SUBSTRATE COMPLEX, PRODUCT
- Substrate: the molecule(s) on which an enzyme acts.
- Product: the molecule(s) formed in the reaction.
- Active site: the part / region of the enzyme molecule where the substrate fits (accept: place where the reaction occurs). Reject: "lock" or "key" as the name of this region.
- The active site is on the ENZYME, not on the substrate.
- Enzyme-substrate complex (ES complex): formed when the substrate binds to / fits into the active site.
- Sequence: enzyme + substrate -> enzyme-substrate complex -> enzyme-product -> product released + enzyme free (unchanged).
- Named examples: amylase - starch -> maltose (accept sugar / glucose where listed; older names diastase / ptyalin accepted for amylase); maltase - maltose -> glucose; protease - protein -> amino acids; lipase - fat / lipid / oil -> fatty acids + glycerol; the enzyme that digests plant cell walls acts on cellulose (reject "cellulase" as the substrate).
- To remove stains: starch - amylase; fat - lipase.
- When asked to suggest an enzyme: name it, give a specific location correct for that enzyme, and give its function as substrate + product.

LOCK AND KEY HYPOTHESIS AND SPECIFICITY
- The enzyme has an active site.
- The active site has a particular / specific / exact shape that is COMPLEMENTARY to the shape of the substrate. Reject: the active site is the same shape as the substrate.
- The substrate fits into / binds to / combines with the active site.
- An enzyme-substrate complex is formed.
- The substrate is split (or substrates joined) - product(s) formed.
- The product(s) leave / are released from the active site.
- The enzyme is unchanged / ready to be used again.
- Enzymes are specific: each enzyme acts on only one substrate / catalyses only one reaction (e.g. maltase acts only on maltose); wrongly shaped substrates cannot fit the active site.
- The enzyme is the lock and the substrate is the key (e.g. lipase = lock, fat = key; amylase = lock, starch = key; protease = lock, protein = key).
- With a named example: enzyme + its name (e.g. amylase); substrate + its name (e.g. starch); named product formed.
- If nothing else is credited, a bare reference to "lock and key" can gain one mark.
- Also credited in older mark schemes: stress is put on the substrate molecule; the reaction can also work in reverse.
- The statement essential to the hypothesis is that each enzyme molecule has a distinct shape; the property it explains is that most enzymes catalyse only one reaction.
- In diagrams: the substrate is the molecule whose shape fits the active site; the molecule that appears unchanged before and after is the enzyme; the pieces released are the products.
- Cellulase breaks down / digests / hydrolyses cellulose (the material of plant cell walls); fibre (cellulose / lignin) passes undigested to the colon because humans have no suitable enzyme for it.

CONSEQUENCES OF THE SHAPE OF THE ACTIVE SITE
- If the active site is damaged / changed, the enzyme cannot carry out its specific reaction (the substrate no longer fits; fewer or no enzyme-substrate complexes; less or no product).
- A mutation (a change in the gene / allele / base sequence of DNA) changes the code for the enzyme (protein), so the active site no longer fits the substrate and the correct enzyme is not made.
- Rate against substrate concentration, with a fixed amount of enzyme: while the rate is still rising, the enzyme active sites are not all occupied.
- Evidence that a substance is an enzyme: it digests its substrate when untreated but hardly at all after boiling (e.g. pepsin + acid gives large amounts of amino acids from egg white; boiled pepsin + acid gives only traces).
- A solution of an enzyme gives a positive biuret test, because enzymes are proteins.
- Stored food in a germinating seed is made available by: an enzyme / named enzyme (correct for the storage product); digestion / hydrolysis - the enzymes are activated by / need water; large molecules are changed to small molecules / insoluble to soluble (accept a correctly named small molecule, including glucose). "Broken down" alone scores one mark only. The products are used as appropriate, e.g. protein for growth, carbohydrate / fat for energy (reject storage).
- Lipase is an enzyme; chlorophyll, fibrinogen and insulin are not enzymes.
- Changing an enzyme so that it acts on a different substrate (genetic modification): enzymes are proteins; a gene codes for the enzyme; a changed sequence of bases; gives a changed sequence of amino acids; and a changed shape of the enzyme molecule / active site; the active site; now has a shape complementary to / fits the new substrate.
- A gene mutation can leave a person unable to make the correct enzyme: the change in the gene / base sequence changes the protein, so the active site does not fit the substrate.
- An animal cannot digest a substance when it has no enzyme for it - it has no gene / allele coding for that enzyme.

5.2 EFFECTS OF TEMPERATURE AND pH

EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE - DESCRIPTION
- Increasing the temperature increases the rate of reaction (up to the optimum).
- There is a maximum / best / optimum temperature - the temperature that produces the highest rate of enzyme activity (the peak of the curve), not the highest temperature at which there is any activity.
- Above the optimum the rate slows rapidly.
- The reaction stops / the rate falls to zero.
- The rate is slower on both sides of the optimum; shape of the curve described (rises, peaks, falls steeply).
- Drawing the curve for a human enzyme between 20 and 80 C: graph rises; reaches a peak between 35 and 55 C; falls to zero between 50 and 80 C. Accept: a vertical drop. Reject: an incurving drop.
- Optimum values are read from the graph (a range is accepted, e.g. 37-39 C); most human enzymes work best at 35-40 C (body temperature); most product is released at the peak.
- Enzymes from different organisms have different optimum temperatures (e.g. a detergent protease with an optimum of 60 C, not 37 C; enzymes of bacteria from hot springs peak at about 75-85 C); each is active only within its own range.

EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE - EXPLANATION
- Heat increases the kinetic energy / rate of movement of the molecules.
- More (frequent, effective) collisions between enzyme and substrate (as temperature rises from 0 to 20 C the frequency of effective collisions increases; at low temperature there is less kinetic energy and fewer collisions).
- In lock-and-key terms: "the key turns in the lock more often".
- At high temperature the enzyme is denatured (accept: destroyed / damaged / loses its 3D structure). Reject: the enzyme "dies" / is "killed"; denaturing "of yeast".
- The shape of the active site changes.
- The substrate no longer fits / is no longer complementary to / cannot bind to the active site ("the key no longer fits the lock").
- Fewer / no enzyme-substrate complexes form.
- Reduced / no reaction - no product is made.
- Denaturation happens only at high temperatures (accept 60 C or above if a figure is given) and it is permanent: on cooling again the rate stays at zero / there is no change, because the enzymes are still denatured / the active site is still changed.
- Low temperatures do NOT denature enzymes - they only slow them (a frozen enzyme works again when warmed; a boiled enzyme does not work when cooled).
- The falling part of the curve means the enzyme is becoming denatured - not that substrate or enzyme is running out.
- Heat may also change the shape of the substrate molecule (credited once); in a protease reaction both enzyme and substrate are proteins, so both can be denatured.
- If body temperature falls, respiratory enzymes work more slowly.

EFFECT OF pH - DESCRIPTION AND EXPLANATION
- pH is a measure of acidity / alkalinity (accept: hydrogen ion concentration).
- Each enzyme has a best / optimum pH at which its rate is fastest; different enzymes have different optimum pH values.
- The rate is slower on both sides of the optimum (a roughly symmetrical curve); the enzyme is inactive outside its own range.
- At the optimum pH the active site and the substrate are complementary, enzyme-substrate complexes form and the frequency of effective collisions is greatest.
- Away from the optimum the active site changes shape and the substrate fits less well; at an extreme pH the enzyme is denatured and the substrate no longer fits the active site.
- The enzyme changes shape - the substrate does not.
- Raising the temperature towards the optimum raises activity at every pH but leaves the optimum pH unchanged (a second curve is drawn above the first, with its peak at the same pH).
- An enzyme whose optimum is about pH 2 works in the stomach: the stomach is acidic, due to hydrochloric acid. Gastric protease (pepsin) needs acid; a protease that digests protein in dilute alkali but not in acid comes from the pancreas.
- Starch digestion stops in the stomach because the pH there changes the shape of amylase: digestion / enzyme activity stops (amylase does not work); the pH is incorrect / too acidic; no suitable amylase is produced in the stomach.
- pH and the digestive enzymes: hydrochloric acid gives the optimum / best acidity / correct pH for the action of pepsin (protease) in the stomach; a drug that reduces acid production changes the stomach pH (less acid), so the protease has no / reduced activity (it works best in acid conditions) and less protein is digested (reject "the drug neutralises the acid"); bile and pancreatic juice neutralise the acid and provide the optimum (alkaline) pH for enzyme activity in the small intestine, avoiding denaturation of the enzymes; if the gall bladder is removed the pH in the duodenum is incorrect / more acidic / less alkaline and fat digestion / lipase action is slower / not optimum; salivary amylase acts at its (optimum) pH in the mouth.
- The enzyme that curdles milk comes from the stomach (of a young mammal): the stomach is acidic; the enzyme has a low optimum pH; young mammals take only milk; curdling increases the surface area.
- A swallowed enzyme tablet is given a coating that breaks down only in alkali: the stomach is acid (pH below 7); the coating resists the acid, so the tablet does not break down / the contents are not released in the stomach; this prevents the enzyme being denatured / destroyed / inactivated; the small intestine (duodenum) is alkaline (pH above 7); the tablet breaks down / the enzyme is released there, where it works at its optimum / fastest rate.
- An enzyme preparation that stays highly active across a wide pH range is not denatured and is effective in both the stomach (acid, about pH 2) and the small intestine (alkaline); a powder has a larger surface area, giving more activity / collisions.

EXPERIMENTS AND THEIR RESULTS
- Rate can be followed by measuring a reactant disappearing (e.g. starch remaining; time for the iodine colour to disappear) or a product appearing (e.g. maltose concentration; volume of gas; time to collect a fixed volume of oxygen; amino acids formed; fall in pH as fatty acids form).
- When TIME TAKEN (or amount of substrate left) is plotted, the curve is U-shaped: shortest time / least substrate at the optimum, more on both sides.
- Amylase + starch mixed at different temperatures and sampled with iodine: at 35 C the mixture goes yellow-brown immediately; at 20 C after about five minutes; at 10 C it is still blue-black after 30 minutes (too slow); at 90 C it is still blue-black after 30 minutes (denatured).
- Starch + amylase goes yellow-brown at 35 C and pH 6.9, but stays blue-black at 75 C or at pH 2.5.
- Amylase boiled and then cooled gives no digestion; amylase frozen and then warmed to 25 C does digest starch; kept at 1 C it does not within a few minutes.
- Starch-agar plate: the largest starch-free area forms round amylase solution alone - not round amylase + hydrochloric acid, boiled amylase, protease or water.
- Protein-agar plate / egg-white suspension: digestion (largest clear area; cloudy suspension goes clear; egg white digested first) only with unboiled pepsin (protease) + hydrochloric acid - not pepsin alone, boiled pepsin + acid, acid alone or water.
- Cooked egg white in stomach protease at pH 2, 5, 8, 11, 14: most digestion at pH 2, progressively less at higher pH.
- Seeds on starch agar: a starch-free zone forms round non-boiled (germinating) seeds because they contain amylase; none forms round boiled seeds, because the amylase is denatured by boiling.
- A tube with no enzyme is a control / comparison - it shows what happens without the enzyme.
- Explaining results at three temperatures: more product at the warm temperature with enzyme (enzyme working / effective collisions; more kinetic energy; near the optimum); less at the cool temperature (less kinetic energy / fewer collisions); little at the very hot temperature (enzyme denatured / active site changed shape / substrate does not fit; yeast or cells killed). A predicted result must follow the same pattern (e.g. rising dough: a larger volume at 40 °C than at 20 °C, a smaller one at 80 °C).
- Oil + bile without lipase: pH stays the same; no digestion of lipid. Oil + bile + lipase: bile emulsifies the oil / increases its surface area and provides the optimum pH for lipase; lipase digests the lipid into fatty acids + glycerol; the pH falls, caused by the fatty acids.
- An enzyme experiment is kept at about 40 C because that is the optimum / best temperature for the action of the enzyme. Ignore: a reference to body temperature alone.
- Describing data: quote the range over which the enzyme is active and the optimum values (e.g. "active from pH 6 to 8; optimum pH 7.5; active between 30 and 70 C; optimum 60 C"); readings need units.

APPLICATIONS CREDITED
- Industrial process / fermenter: pH and temperature are monitored to detect changes and keep the optimum / best conditions for the enzymes (and for growth / reproduction of the microorganism); this prevents denaturation and death of the microorganism; without it the rate of reaction / amount of product falls; the product is made rapidly / efficiently / cheaply with maximum yield.
- Heated milk is cooled before bacteria are added: prevents denaturation of enzymes; brings it to the optimum temperature; the bacteria are not killed; maximum yield of product.
- To speed up growth of microorganisms on agar: incubate at an optimum / suitable (raised) temperature and suitable pH - because growth and respiration are enzyme-controlled.
- A seed kept much hotter than its optimum: develops more slowly / does not germinate; enzymes denature; digestion (hydrolysis) of stored food slows / stops.
- Boiling plant tissue: kills cells / denatures enzymes / stops metabolic reactions (and breaks cell walls / membranes, releasing the contents).
- Boiling stops cut fruit browning: the enzyme's active site is denatured / changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, no enzyme-substrate complex forms, so no reaction.
- An enzyme used to tenderise meat is applied before cooking, not during: strong heat (60 C or above) denatures / destroys the enzyme, so it would not work; applying it earlier gives it time to act.
- In a compost heap different microorganisms thrive at different temperatures - linked to the effect of temperature on enzyme action.
- A protease used in laundry detergent is supplied with chemicals that give an alkaline solution: the enzyme then works at an increased rate / faster / at its optimum (pH); this prevents denaturation; the detergent is more effective / clothes are cleaner.
- Enzymes from different species have different structures (different amino acid sequences), so some enzymes / active sites denature at lower or higher temperatures than others.
- Seeds in soil warmed by released heat energy: the rate of enzyme action increases, so germination / growth of the radicle and plumule is faster (reject "for growth" / "helps growth" and any reference to a seedling or plant).
- Raising the temperature round stored fruit speeds up its changes: digestion / chemical breakdown increases; correct reference to enzymes; the rate of reaction / respiration increases (also faster reproduction of microorganisms).
- A suitable temperature is a condition for seed germination because of enzyme / named enzyme action; breaking down the food store (water is needed to activate the enzymes and as a solvent).
- Temperature and enzyme-controlled processes in plants and decomposers: photosynthesis is enzyme-controlled; its rate increases with temperature because of faster molecular movement / more kinetic energy / more collisions and because enzymes work faster; below the optimum the enzymes work more slowly (slower metabolic rate / slower photosynthesis); a glasshouse gives a higher / optimum temperature for enzymes; warm (moist) conditions increase enzyme action in decomposition. At high temperatures water loss / wilting, loss of turgidity of guard cells and closure of stomata slow photosynthesis.
- Microorganisms in fermenters and food production: temperature and pH are controlled because of the enzymes (25-45 degC and pH 5-8 quoted for an antibiotic fermenter; 32-50 degC for yoghurt; a suitable temperature / warmth for wine); the water jacket cools the fermenter contents, preventing high temperatures killing the fungus / denaturing the enzyme.

7.1 UPTAKE AND TRANSPORT OF WATER AND IONS

ROOT HAIR CELLS - STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION
- Identified as a root hair (cell) / root epidermis cell; these are the cells through which water (and mineral ions) are absorbed from the soil.
- The hair is a long, thin extension / projection / finger-like outgrowth of a single cell of the outer layer of the root; the vacuole extends into the hair.
- Long / very small (microscopic) - lies between the soil particles, in contact with the soil water.
- Gives an increased / large surface area.
- For maximum / rapid uptake (absorption).
- Of water - by osmosis (accept diffusion).
- Of ions / salts / minerals (or a named ion, e.g. nitrate, magnesium) - by active transport.
- Of oxygen - for root respiration. Reject: nitrogen as a substance absorbed.
- Cell contents: cytoplasm, cell wall, nucleus and large vacuole are present; chloroplasts are absent.
- Not a function of root hairs: sugar uptake, photosynthesis, anchorage, storage of starch, transport of sugars; roots do not "search" for water or ions.
- A feature of the cell that helps absorption, with its matching explanation:
    - cell membrane: living; partially permeable; only selected molecules enter; allows osmosis (reject diffusion here); active transport;
    - cellulose cell wall: permeable; tough / flexible; helps keep the shape of the cell; allows turgor / gives mechanical support;
    - cell sap / vacuole / cytoplasm: contains a concentrated solution (concentration gradient); for water entry; by osmosis / diffusion;
    - cytoplasm / mitochondria: respiration / energy release; for active transport;
    - nucleus (one mark only): controls the cell's activities.
- The cell membrane allows water and ions to move into and out of the cell - movement of both nitrate ions and water molecules is possible in either direction.
- A plant that takes its water from a host plant lacks roots / a root system (reject "root hairs" as the missing organ).

ROOT HAIRS COMPARED WITH VILLI
- Similarities: both long / elongated; both provide a large surface area; both absorb (uptake); by active transport / diffusion; both absorb ions (salts / minerals) and water; substances are in solution; a membrane is involved.
- Structural differences: root hair has a cell wall / villus cells have none; a root hair is part of one cell / a villus is made of many cells (multicellular); root hairs have no blood vessels / lacteals (ORA).
- Functional differences: root hairs absorb from the soil / villi absorb from the gut; villi absorb amino acids; glucose; lipids / fatty acids / glycerol.
- In diagrams: the animal structure is a villus (reject microvillus); the plant structure is a root hair (cell).

UPTAKE OF WATER
- By osmosis (accept diffusion). Reject: active transport.
- From a higher to a lower water potential / down a water potential gradient (accept: from a high to a lower water concentration; hypotonic to hypertonic) - the water potential (water concentration) inside the root hair is lower than in the soil water.
- Through the cell wall.
- Through / across the partially permeable cell membrane.
- Into the root hair (cell).
- In waterlogged soil with no oxygen, water uptake is unaffected (ion uptake slows); in soil flooded with salty sea water, water uptake by root hairs stops (ion uptake continues).

UPTAKE OF MINERAL IONS
- The ions are dissolved in (soil) water / in solution.
- Absorbed by root hairs.
- Pass through the cell wall.
- Pass through the (partially permeable) cell membrane - by carriers in the membrane.
- By active transport (accept diffusion where the gradient allows) with a correct reference to the concentration gradient - active transport is against the gradient: the ion concentration is higher inside the root hair than in the soil (e.g. magnesium 75 units inside, 15 outside).
- Active transport uses energy.
- The energy comes from (aerobic) respiration.
- Ion uptake by root hairs is the energy-consuming process controlled by the cell surface membrane - unlike absorption of carbon dioxide or oxygen by leaf cells, loss of water vapour into the leaf air spaces, or transport of water and ions up the xylem.
- Evidence: uptake of mineral ions / nitrate is reduced at lower oxygen concentrations. On a graph of uptake against oxygen: an initial increase in oxygen concentration increases the uptake; a further increase gives no further increase.
- Uptake against soil ion concentration: ions are absorbed even when the soil concentration is lower than that in the cell; the rate rises with soil concentration and then levels off.
- The large surface area of the root hair assists the uptake of ions.
- Two routes across the root: (through the living cells) active transport; requires energy; from respiration / mitochondria; across a living cell membrane; against the concentration gradient - works even when ions are very dilute in the soil. (Along the cell walls) suited to ions at high concentration in the soil: down a concentration gradient; by diffusion; the cellulose cell wall is fully permeable; a direct pathway to the xylem with no barrier.
- Water and ions moving along cell walls (not crossing a membrane) both move by diffusion.

WHY THE IONS AND WATER MATTER
- Nitrate ions are carried in the xylem and used to make amino acids / proteins (growth).
- Magnesium (ions) are carried, dissolved in water, in the xylem (in the transpiration stream) into the palisade mesophyll and into the chloroplasts, to make chlorophyll.
- Water is needed for cell elongation and for photosynthesis.
- Nitrogen from fertiliser to human food: ammonium ions / urea / nitrates in the fertiliser enter the soil; bacteria produce nitrates (nitrification); root hairs absorb them; by active transport; transported in the xylem; used to make amino acids / proteins; humans eat the proteins.
- A nitrate ion becoming part of a stored molecule in a seed: in solution; absorbed by a root hair; by active transport / diffusion with a correct description of the gradient; carried in the xylem; used to make an amino acid; the amino acid is carried in the phloem; to the fruit / ovary; made into protein; stored in the cotyledon / endosperm.
- Ions released into the soil from decomposed fallen leaves are recycled: ions / a named ion / mineral salts; pass into the plant / root; by diffusion or active transport (down / against the concentration gradient). Credited uses: nitrates and ammonium - amino acids / protein; magnesium and iron - chlorophyll; phosphate - DNA / membranes; sulfate - amino acids / proteins; calcium - cell walls; potassium - enzymes; growth.
- If the xylem is blocked (e.g. by bacteria), mineral ions such as magnesium (zinc also credited) do not reach the leaves, so the leaves cannot make chlorophyll and develop white / yellow patches.
- The water needed for photosynthesis comes from the soil (or irrigation) through the roots / root hair cells.

PATHWAY OF WATER (AND IONS) THROUGH THE PLANT
- Root hair cells -> root cortex cells -> xylem -> mesophyll cells (leaf).
- From the root cortex, water passes into the xylem vessels.
- The xylem carries the water up the stem to the leaf; xylem is the tissue bringing water into the leaf, and the tissue with the highest water concentration in the plant.
- Full pathway from soil to atmosphere, as separately credited points: root hair; cell wall; membrane; partially permeable; osmosis / diffusion; (root cortex); xylem; stem; leaf / mesophyll; water film on the mesophyll cells; air / intercellular spaces; out through the stomata / between the guard cells; as water vapour by evaporation / transpiration.
- Position of the xylem: in a root section it is the central star / cross-shaped tissue (phloem lies between its arms); in a stem section it is the inner part of each vascular bundle (phloem is the outer part), the bundles forming a ring; in a leaf it is the upper part of the vein (phloem below).

INVESTIGATING THE PATHWAY WITH A STAIN / DYE
- A cut shoot, or a whole plant with its roots, is stood in a solution of dye for some hours; sections of root, stem and leaf are then cut and examined.
- Only the xylem is stained (ignore "vessel"): the central star in the root; the inner part of each vascular bundle in the stem; the upper part of the vein in the leaf. Phloem, cortex, pith, guard cells and palisade cells are not stained.
- How the dye enters the root: by diffusion (reject osmosis - also when it is mentioned together with diffusion; ignore active transport); down a concentration gradient; through the (cellulose) cell walls of the root (hair) cells; the cell wall is permeable (reject semi- / partially / selectively permeable for the wall); the cell membrane lets the dye molecules pass. Ignore references to the cell membrane in the newer scheme.
- The dye then enters the xylem and travels upwards (accept: in the transpiration stream / by transpiration pull / capillarity / root pressure).
- Rate of movement = distance moved / time taken, converted to the units asked for (e.g. 12 mm in 5 minutes = 144 mm per hour).

WATER UPTAKE AND WILTING
- A mature plant wilts less than a newly transplanted seedling: its root system is undamaged (ORA); it has its root hairs; water uptake is sufficient for the plant (ORA); its cells / tissues stay turgid; older plants are also supported by xylem / lignin. Ignore: "more / less water uptake" alone.
- A plant pulled out of the ground and re-planted wilts for several days: root hairs removed / roots damaged; stem / xylem damaged; less water absorbed (reject: no water absorbed); the plant still loses water / transpires; cells lose water / become flaccid (max 3 for wilting). Recovery: root hairs regrow; more water is absorbed; cells gain water again / become turgid.
- Water leaving a cut shoot in a potometer: xylem; to the leaves / flowers; between the leaf cells; surface of the mesophyll cells; leaf air spaces; out through the stomata / guard cells.
- Water passing across the root: movement from cell to cell; through or between the cell walls; then enters the xylem.
- Dye rising in a stem: transpiration / evaporation; water (vapour) is lost from the leaf / stomata; this pulls / draws up the water / solution (reject sucks) / the water lost must be replaced; capillarity / root pressure / cohesion / adhesion also credited. When shading the stained tissue: xylem only (accept shading in one vascular bundle only).
- Coloured water reaches the leaves of a cut shoot fastest in warm, moving air (e.g. 30 C with a fan rather than 10 C or still air).
- A young plant dug up and re-planted may wilt because the surface area of its roots has been reduced (root hairs lost).
- When there is little or no water near the soil surface, shallow-rooted plants (roots too short to reach the water) decline first: less water for photosynthesis, and less water in which mineral salts can dissolve and be absorbed (less water for cell sap).

7.2 TRANSPIRATION AND TRANSLOCATION

DEFINITION OF TRANSPIRATION
- Loss of water.
- As water vapour / by evaporation.
- From the leaves / aerial parts of a plant / to the atmosphere.
- Through the stomata (accept: between or through the guard cells).
- Evaporation of water at the surfaces of the mesophyll cells and its loss (as vapour) through the stomata. Not: evaporation "from the stomata"; movement of water through the cuticle; movement of water in the xylem from roots to leaves; gas exchange.
- The term for loss of water vapour by a plant is transpiration (accept evapotranspiration). "Evaporation" alone was rejected in one scheme (2006) and accepted, with diffusion, in another (2019) - use transpiration.

HOW WATER LEAVES THE LEAF
- The xylem brings water into the leaf (reject phloem); it transports water from a named lower part to a named higher part.
- Water passes from the xylem into the mesophyll cells - by osmosis (diffusion also credited) - down a water potential gradient (the leaf cell contents are more concentrated).
- A film / layer of water lies on the surface / cell wall of the mesophyll cells.
- Water evaporates from the surfaces of the (spongy) mesophyll cells - forming water vapour.
- Into the air spaces / intercellular spaces (humidity inside the leaf is high).
- Water vapour then moves by diffusion - from high to low concentration / down a concentration gradient.
- Out of the leaf through the stomata / pores - between the guard cells. When asked for the cells between which the vapour passes: guard cells (ignore mesophyll; reject "stomatal cells").
- Stages in order: xylem vessel -> mesophyll cell = osmosis; water layer on the cell surface -> air space = evaporation; air space -> outside = diffusion. Most water evaporates from the spongy mesophyll cells; most transpiration takes place from the lower surface of the leaf.
- Arrows drawn on a leaf section: one arrow leaving the xylem (reject any passing through phloem); passing into a mesophyll cell; entering an air space in the spongy mesophyll; passing out of a stoma (the sequence must be plausible). Evaporation is marked where water enters the air space / at the wall of a mesophyll cell (reject on a guard cell).
- Pathway from a cut stem to the air: xylem; to the leaves / flowers; between the leaf cells; surface of mesophyll cells; (leaf) air spaces; through the stomata / guard cells.
- Whole pathway from the soil: osmosis; through the cell membrane; of a root hair; from cell to cell; through or between cell walls; enters the xylem; transpiration pull / stream (capillarity / molecular cohesion / root pressure also credited); evaporation / water vapour; from mesophyll cells / into air spaces; water potential gradient; diffusion; through stomata.
- Carbon dioxide enters a leaf by diffusion; water enters a leaf by the transpiration pull / transpiration.

LEAF STRUCTURES AND TRANSPIRATION (points are not transferable between structures)
- Cuticle: waterproof / waxy; prevents / reduces water loss / transpiration / evaporation.
- Xylem (reject phloem): transports water; from a named location to a named higher location.
- Spongy mesophyll: air / intercellular spaces; water film; evaporation / water vapour; diffusion; moist surface; large surface area.
- Stoma / stomata: guard cell; opens / closes; water vapour exits; by diffusion.
- Guard cells: control the size / opening and closing; of the stomata; so control water loss / transpiration; allow exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide; for respiration; and photosynthesis.
- Importance of stomata: allow carbon dioxide into the leaf (reject "into cells"); oxygen out (one mark only for "gas exchange"); for photosynthesis / to make carbohydrates; allow water vapour out / transpiration; which brings water / salts to the leaves / cools the leaf / plant.
- Stomata open in the day: carbon dioxide can enter / oxygen can be released (photosynthesis); water loss / transpiration gives cooling / brings water or ions to the leaf from the soil (reject: water loss "for temperature regulation / control"). Closed at night: prevents / reduces / stops transpiration / loss of water; avoids wilting; (oxygen entry for respiration also credited).
- Appearance of the pore: wide open in the day; partly open in the evening; closed at night.
- The upper surface of a leaf has fewer / no stomata / guard cells - this reduces / stops water loss / transpiration; it appears shiny / bright (cuticle).
- A gas that builds up in the leaf air spaces in darkness: water vapour; from transpiration (the transpiration stream); it cannot escape / the stomata (guard cells) are closed. (Carbon dioxide from respiration is the alternative answer.)

MOVEMENT OF WATER UP THE XYLEM - TRANSPIRATION PULL
- Water evaporates / transpires from the leaves - water vapour is lost from the leaf / stomata (accept: water vapour diffuses out).
- This reduces the water potential of the leaf cells; the water lost must be replaced.
- Transpiration pull (accept transpiration / transpiration stream) - draws / pulls water up (reject "sucks").
- Water molecules are held together by forces of attraction (accept cohesion / adhesion).
- Water moves in a continuous column.
- In the xylem (vessels), from the roots to the leaves (reject phloem). Reject: water moves by active transport.
- Older schemes also credited capillarity and root pressure.
- The main cause of water moving up to the leaves is evaporation from the walls of the mesophyll cells - water moves up the stem as it evaporates from the leaves (not active transport, evaporation from the epidermis, use in photosynthesis, osmosis or a push from the roots). The main source of the energy is heat from the Sun.
- The water-uptake curve follows the water-loss curve because: water evaporates / transpires from the leaves; this reduces the water potential of the leaves; water moves up / through the xylem vessels; transpiration pull / stream; forces of attraction between water molecules; this prevents loss of turgidity / wilting.
- A blockage / air bubbles in the xylem stops water moving up a cut shoot.
- Water molecules move up only in the xylem; in the phloem they move both up and down.

FACTORS AFFECTING THE RATE OF TRANSPIRATION
- A correct reference to diffusion is credited in any of the explanations.
- Naming factors: any of humidity, temperature / heat, light, wind / air movement, amount of water in the soil. Format often marked as factor; variation; explanation.
- TEMPERATURE higher -> rate increases: increased / faster evaporation (of water) / more water vapour; from the surface of the mesophyll cells; molecules have more kinetic energy / move faster; (steeper diffusion gradient). At a lower temperature the curve keeps the same shape but lies below the original.
- WIND / AIR MOVEMENT faster -> rate increases: wind blows away / removes water vapour (moisture) from around the leaf; humidity there decreases / fewer water molecules outside; increased concentration gradient; faster diffusion / evaporation. On a graph the line becomes steeper while air is blown; water loss plotted against wind speed rises and then levels off.
- HUMIDITY higher -> rate decreases: greater concentration of water molecules in the atmosphere; reduced / loss of gradient; slower / less evaporation; slower / less diffusion (ignore "water" alone). Lower humidity -> the lower the humidity the higher the rate; the concentration of water outside is lower than inside the leaf / the atmosphere is not saturated; water is lost by diffusion. A graph of rate against humidity falls as humidity rises.
- LIGHT INTENSITY higher -> rate increases: stomata open / more stomata open / open wider (one scheme: reject "guard cells open"); increased surface area for water loss / a greater volume of vapour can be lost; (greater light intensity + faster evaporation / more diffusion). A graph of rate against light intensity rises and then levels off.
- Low light / darkness (e.g. a black bag over the plant) -> rate decreases: photosynthesis decreases / stops; less water needed; guard cells lose their turgidity; stomata close / open less; humidity rises around the leaf; decreased concentration gradient; less diffusion; less evaporation / transpiration.
- SOIL WATER shortage: less water uptake; water lost cannot be replaced (accept: loses water faster than it gains it); the water content of the plant falls / loss of turgidity (accept wilting); stomata / pores close; water loss falls / stops.
- Combinations: fastest when hot, dry and windy (less humid and more windy; dry, bright and warm; lower humidity with higher temperature, e.g. 60 % and 25 C rather than 100 % or 15 C). Slower with decreased air currents or increased humidity. Increased humidity is the change least likely to increase the rate.
- Over a day: water loss rises during the light / day when the stomata are open and falls in the dark / at night when the stomata close; the rise through the morning is explained by increased light (intensity); increased temperature / heat (faster evaporation - ignore references to transpiration here); wind / increased air movement; decreased humidity; stomata / guard cells opening (wider). The greatest loss is around midday.
- A plant whose transpiration rate is lowest around midday closes its stomata during the brightest part of the day.
- Leaves are coolest when transpiration is fastest: highest / fastest rate; of (evapo)transpiration / evaporation; over the preceding hours; evaporation cools the leaves / removes heat. The temperature inside a leaf stays below the air temperature: evaporation (reject transpiration here); faster at high temperatures; cools / removes heat. A plant in dry soil is hotter at midday than one in damp soil: less evaporation of water / less loss of latent heat (reject "less transpiration"); so less cooling (reverse argument accepted).
- A branch enclosed in a transparent bag loses more water at first and then less: it warms up more quickly at first (like a glasshouse); humidity inside the bag increases; this becomes a limiting factor / less transpiration. The water that collects is safe to drink even from a poisonous plant: it comes from the soil (not made by the plant); it is carried in the xylem / xylem is just hollow tubes; it contains only dissolved salts / metabolites are carried in the phloem; the poison stays in the cells; only water evaporates in transpiration.
- Changing from humid to dry air: greatly increased upward movement in the xylem; little change in the phloem.

DIFFERENCES BETWEEN PLANTS AND BETWEEN LEAF SURFACES
- Reasons one plant loses more water than another in the same conditions (reverse argument accepted): different species; more / bigger leaves - greater surface area for evaporation; more stomata / greater density of stomata - more places for water to escape; bigger / wider stomata / degree of opening - larger area from which water is lost; thinner (waxy) cuticle - a thicker cuticle is a greater barrier to water molecules; better / bigger root system / more root hairs / faster water uptake; size of plant; no hairs / no sunken stomata. Mesophyll cells with a greater surface area also give greater loss. (Max 2 for structural features given without explanation.)

11.1 CIRCULATORY SYSTEM

THE CIRCULATORY SYSTEM
- A system of blood vessels with a pump (the heart) and valves that ensure one-way flow of blood.
- On a simplified diagram the heart is the structure through which both circuits pass; the lungs and the rest of the body are each on a separate loop.
- Examples from the circulatory system: cell - a blood cell (or a named one); tissue - muscle or a named tissue (accept blood / capillary); organ - heart / blood vessel or a named one (reject capillary). Examples from outside the circulatory system are rejected; only the first answer in a list is marked.

DOUBLE CIRCULATION
- Blood travels / passes through the heart (pump) twice.
- For each full circuit / during one complete circulation of the body (accept: heart - body - heart - lungs).
- One circuit from the heart to the lungs and back - the pulmonary circulation.
- One circuit from the heart to the (rest of the) body and back - the systemic circulation.
- Pulmonary circulation is at low pressure (accept the reverse statement for the systemic circulation).
- Systemic circulation is at high pressure - the pressure of blood going to the body tissues is higher than that going to the lungs.
- Correct reference to oxygenated / deoxygenated blood in the two circuits.
- Not credited as the meaning of double circulation: blood always enclosed in vessels; two types of blood vessel; two chambers / two valves on each side of the heart; two sets of arteries leaving the heart; two separate circulatory systems; blood going down one side of the body and up the other.
- Blood flows through the lungs at a lower pressure than through the rest of the body (it does not travel a greater distance, faster or in greater volume).

FUNCTIONS OF THE TWO CIRCUITS
- Circuit to the lungs: blood becomes oxygenated / picks up oxygen; blood loses carbon dioxide.
- Circuit to the body tissues: delivers oxygen / glucose to the tissues; collects carbon dioxide.
- Reference to a part of the heart together with a named vessel (e.g. right ventricle - pulmonary artery; left ventricle - aorta).
- Low pressure to the lungs / high pressure to the body.

READING CIRCULATION DIAGRAMS
- Oxygenated blood is in the vessel from the lungs to the heart and in the vessel from the heart to the rest of the body; the blood contains most oxygen between the lung capillaries and the left side of the heart, and between the left side of the heart and the capillaries of the body organs.
- The highest blood pressure is in the vessel leaving the (left side of the) heart for the body; inside the heart the pressure is greatest in the left ventricle.
- Shortest route from a leg to an arm: leg -> heart -> lungs -> heart -> arm.

HUMAN AND FISH CIRCULATION COMPARED
- Similarities (max 3): both have a heart acting as a pump; one-way flow of blood / valves in the heart; large surface area for gas exchange; respiratory surfaces have a rich blood supply.
- The pressure of blood in the aorta of a fish is lower than in the aorta of a human. The fish heart also has 2 valves and 2 vessels (human 4 of each) and chambers of similar size.
- Differences (max 3): the human heart has 4 chambers and the fish heart 2; humans have a double circulation and fish a single circulation; human blood passes through the heart twice during a single circulation but fish blood only once; lungs / alveoli in humans but gills in fish.

EFFICIENCY OF THE SYSTEM
- A hole between the left and right sides of the heart: mixing / non-separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood; more oxygen in the blood sent to the lungs; less oxygen in the blood sent to the body / organs / tissues / cells; reduced (aerobic) respiration / reduced energy / more anaerobic respiration / more lactic acid; the heart beats faster / harder to meet the oxygen demand; (blood) pressure reduced.
- During strenuous exercise the blood supply increases to the heart and to skeletal muscle: more oxygen / glucose supplied; increased (aerobic) respiration / anaerobic respiration prevented; lactic acid removed (skeletal muscle only); muscles contract harder / faster. Supply to the skin also increases - increased heat loss.
- Sitting still with the legs crossed makes the lower leg heavier: blood collects in the legs / feet; in the veins; the leg muscles are not being used; so blood is not pushed from one set of valves to the next; the mass / weight of the (lower) leg increases.
- Why the two circuits work at different pressures: the right ventricle wall is thinner / the left thicker; blood for the lungs has a shorter distance to travel and little work to do against gravity; low pressure avoids damage to lung capillaries; high pressure in the body circuit is needed for kidney filtration and to deliver oxygen / glucose to the brain.

TISSUES AND ORGANS WORKING TOGETHER IN THE CIRCULATORY SYSTEM
- Muscle tissue; in the artery walls; maintains blood pressure; continual contraction in the heart (pumping / beating); keeps blood moving.
- Nerve / nervous tissue; in the heart, controlling its rate.
- Blood as a tissue; a transport medium (any example of transport).
- Epithelial tissue (lining); reduces damage to the blood vessel walls.
- Any two organs identified (heart, artery, vein or a named vessel); blood vessels are tubes for the blood.
- The shortest route for blood travelling from a leg to an arm: leg -> heart -> lungs -> heart -> arm.

11.2 HEART

STRUCTURE OF THE HEART
- Four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle (accept auricle for atrium); the heart has two sides / is completely divided by the septum.
- The septum separates oxygenated blood (left side) from deoxygenated blood (right side).
- Most of the heart is muscle tissue; the muscular wall is supplied with oxygen by the coronary arteries (coronary + artery both needed) - the coronary arteries carry blood to the heart muscle.
- Vessels: vena cava brings blood from the body into the right atrium; pulmonary artery carries blood from the right ventricle to the lungs; pulmonary vein brings blood from the lungs into the left atrium (the vein that carries oxygenated blood); aorta carries blood from the left ventricle to the body.
- Oxygenated blood is in the left atrium and left ventricle (and aorta and pulmonary vein).
- Atrioventricular valves lie between each atrium and ventricle: tricuspid on the right, bicuspid (mitral) on the left. Semilunar valves lie at the base of the aorta and of the pulmonary artery (the aortic valve is a semilunar valve).
- Drawing valves on a heart section: two sets of semilunar (aortic / pulmonary) valves shown; two sets of atrioventricular valves in the correct places; free ends of the semilunar valves point upwards (into the arteries); free ends of the atrioventricular valves point downwards (into the ventricles); valves correctly labelled.
- Human heart compared with fish heart: human 4 chambers, fish 2; human 4 valves, fish 2; human 4 blood vessels, fish 2; human chambers of different sizes, fish chambers of similar size.
- Arrows for blood flow: correctly into, through and out of the right side; correctly into, through and out of the left side.

PATH OF BLOOD THROUGH THE HEART
- Route: right atrium -> right ventricle -> lungs -> left atrium -> left ventricle (-> body).
- Blood returning from the body passes through the right atrium and then the right ventricle; blood in an artery to a body organ (e.g. the renal artery) last passed through the left ventricle.
- Blood passes through four chambers in each complete circuit (eight in two circuits).
- Substances absorbed in the lungs (nicotine, an inhaled drug) reach the left atrium first; nutrients absorbed from the small intestine reach the right atrium first.
- Narrowing of the entrance to the vessel leaving the right ventricle first reduces blood flow to the lungs.

RELATIVE THICKNESS OF THE WALLS
- The left ventricle has the thickest (muscular) wall.
- Ventricles are thicker-walled / more muscular than atria (e.g. left ventricle 15 mm, right ventricle 9 mm, each atrium 3 mm) - a ventricle must exert a greater force than an atrium.
- The left ventricle is thicker-walled / more muscular than the right ventricle (ORA).
- Because it has further to pump the blood (round the body) / it generates a higher pressure; greater contraction / force applied to the blood.
- Pressure in the pulmonary artery is lower than in the aorta: right ventricle wall thinner / left ventricle wall thicker (less / more muscle; weaker / stronger contraction); the pulmonary route is a shorter distance (only to the lungs); little work to do against gravity; low pressure avoids damage to the lung capillaries; the body circulation needs high pressure for filtration in the kidneys and to send oxygen / glucose to the brain.
- Evidence in a diagram that blood leaves at different pressures for the lungs and the body: the left ventricle wall is more muscular than the right ventricle wall.

HOW THE HEART IS ADAPTED AS THE PUMP IN A DOUBLE CIRCULATION
- Four chambers / has a septum.
- Blood on the left and right sides is kept separate - deoxygenated and oxygenated blood kept separate.
- Right atrium receives blood from the body; right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs.
- Left atrium receives blood from the lungs; left ventricle pumps blood to the (rest of the) body.
- Larger / thicker wall of the left ventricle (ORA) - to pump blood all round the body.
- Valves give one-way flow / prevent backflow.
- It beats continually / rhythmically; it never tires or suffers from cramp.

CARDIAC MUSCLE CONTRACTION AND VALVE ACTION
- The walls are muscular; the muscle contracts (reject "pushing / forcing / pumping" where the question already says the heart pumps); this pumps / pushes / squeezes blood and creates pressure in the blood system.
- Correct reference to the atria and to the ventricles: the two atria contract together, then the two ventricles contract together, then there is a pause.
- Atrioventricular valves open to let blood pass from atrium to ventricle.
- When the ventricle contracts the atrioventricular valve closes - to prevent the return / backflow of blood into the atrium.
- Tendons / cords hold the atrioventricular valves (reject "heartstrings") - a statement of their action is needed.
- Semilunar valves open as blood is pumped out of the ventricles into the arteries, and close when the ventricle relaxes - preventing backflow into the ventricle.
- The cycle is repeated / the actions are co-ordinated.
- Right side in order: right atrium contracts; right ventricle contracts; tricuspid valve closes; semilunar valves open (as pressure builds up). Credit: 3 marks all correct; 2 for the correct sequence started in the wrong place; 1 for any two in sequence.
- Left ventricle: (muscle) pumps / contracts / pushes blood; at high pressure; to the body / into the aorta.
- Right atrioventricular (tricuspid) valve: opens to let blood into the right ventricle; closes when blood is pumped into the pulmonary artery / to the lungs; prevents backflow of blood; into the right atrium.
- States of the valves: ventricles contracting / emptying - atrioventricular valves closed, semilunar valves open; ventricles filling - atrioventricular valves open, semilunar valves closed.
- The heart ventricle does not have its muscles arranged antagonistically: heart muscle works on its own - it is not arranged in pairs (no flexor and extensor), no other muscle relaxes when it contracts, it is not attached to bones and it does not cause movement at a joint.

PRESSURE AND VOLUME GRAPHS OF ONE HEARTBEAT
- The atrium contracts in the short period just before the steep rise in ventricular pressure.
- The atrioventricular (bicuspid) valve closes where ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure (start of the steep rise).
- The semilunar valve opens where ventricular pressure rises above the pressure in the aorta.
- Between those two points, and again just after contraction (ventricular pressure below aortic pressure but still above atrial pressure), all the valves are closed.
- While ventricular pressure is high (plateau) the atrioventricular valve is closed and the semilunar valve is open; the rise in aortic pressure is caused by contraction of the left ventricle.
- The ventricle is contracting while its pressure is rising / high and while the volume of blood in it is falling.
- Pressure in an artery rises when the heart / ventricle contracts / pumps and falls when it relaxes (one mark for "heart beat / pulse" alone).
- A chamber in which the pressure rises sharply after the right atrium is the right ventricle (blood flows from right atrium to right ventricle / pressure increases).
- A graph for the circulation to the body differs from one for the lungs: wider variations in pressure; pressure higher; the left ventricle wall is larger / thicker / more muscular; blood has further to travel / takes longer; more fluctuations (ignore "increasing pressure").
- Heart rate from a graph or timing bar: beats per minute = 60 / time for one beat in seconds (0.6 s per beat = 100 per minute; two beats in 1.5 s = 80 per minute).

11.3 BLOOD VESSELS

NAMED VESSELS - WHERE THEY RUN
- Aorta: from the left ventricle to the rest of the body (all organs except the lungs); the first artery, with the highest pressure; oxygenated blood. Accept (dorsal / systemic) aorta, aortic arch.
- Vena cava: from the body / head to the right atrium; the last vein, with the lowest pressure; deoxygenated blood. Ignore anterior / superior / inferior.
- Pulmonary artery: from the right ventricle (through a semilunar valve) to the lungs; deoxygenated blood - an artery that carries deoxygenated blood.
- Pulmonary vein: from the lungs to the heart / left atrium; oxygenated blood - the vein that carries oxygenated blood; lowest carbon dioxide.
- Hepatic artery: from the aorta to the liver; carries oxygen (and hormones such as insulin / adrenaline).
- Hepatic portal vein (ignore the abbreviation HPV in the newest scheme): from the small intestine / gut / villi to the liver; carries absorbed (digested) food - glucose, amino acids - also ions, vitamins, water, alcohol; dissolved / in solution, in the blood / plasma.
- Advantage of blood from the ileum going first to the liver in the hepatic portal vein: excess glucose can be converted to glycogen for storage (and is not excreted).
- Hepatic vein: from the liver to the vena cava; carries away carbon dioxide, urea and other products of the liver.
- Renal artery: from the aorta to the kidney. Renal vein: from the kidney to the vena cava.
- Coronary arteries: on the surface of the heart, supplying the heart muscle.
- The liver receives blood from two major vessels (hepatic artery and hepatic portal vein) and all of it leaves in a single major vessel (hepatic vein); blood passing from the gut to the heart goes through the liver.
- The liver is the organ that receives blood from both an artery and a vein.
- Routes: vena cava -> right side of heart -> pulmonary artery -> lungs -> pulmonary vein -> left side of heart -> aorta. A heart-lung machine takes blood from the vena cava and returns it to the aorta. An assist device for the pulmonary circulation would run from the right ventricle to the pulmonary artery; one for the body circulation from the left ventricle to the aorta. An under-developed left ventricle immediately lowers the flow into the aorta.
- A join made between an artery and a vein is called an AV fistula (artery + vein).

COMPOSITION OF THE BLOOD IN DIFFERENT VESSELS
- Oxygenated: aorta, pulmonary vein, hepatic artery, renal artery, coronary artery (and the left atrium / ventricle). Deoxygenated: vena cava, pulmonary artery, hepatic vein, hepatic portal vein, renal vein (and the right atrium / ventricle).
- "All arteries carry oxygenated blood" is false; all arteries carry blood away from the heart, at high pressure.
- Carbon dioxide: lowest in the pulmonary vein; highest in the vena cava (after exercise); higher in the hepatic portal vein than in the artery supplying the gut.
- Glucose: highest in the hepatic portal vein about two hours after a meal, and most variable there; the hepatic vein also has a high concentration (blood leaving the liver after a meal has more glucose than blood arriving in the hepatic artery).
- Urea: highest in the hepatic vein (especially after a protein-rich meal); lowest in the renal vein; always lower in any blood vessel than in the urine in the ureter. The liver adds urea - it never removes it.
- Renal vein compared with renal artery: less oxygen, more carbon dioxide, less urea, less water.
- Blood entering and leaving the liver: hepatic artery - more / most oxygen, more hormones (insulin / glucagon / adrenaline are used / destroyed in the liver); hepatic portal vein - digested foods, ions, vitamins, water (direction of flow must be clear); hepatic vein - carbon dioxide / deoxygenated, urea; correct reference to glucose levels.
- Identifying veins from data: highest urea = hepatic vein; highest oxygen = pulmonary vein; lowest urea = renal vein.

STRUCTURE OF ARTERIES
- Thick wall.
- Thick layer of muscle and elastic tissue (more elastic tissue than a vein).
- Narrow / small (round) lumen.
- No valves along their length (the aorta and pulmonary artery have a semilunar valve at their base, which prevents blood returning to the heart).
- Carry blood away from the heart, under high pressure, with a pulse.
- Elastic walls stretch and recoil (the lumen can stretch) - this maintains the pressure / pulse beat; link on to capillaries and veins.
- Aorta: oxygenated blood; from the left ventricle to the rest of the body; thick walls; elastic + muscle; elastic recoil; high pressure; valve prevents blood returning to the heart (max 4 for one vessel).

STRUCTURE OF VEINS
- Thin wall.
- Less / thinner muscle and elastic tissue.
- Wide / large lumen - offers less resistance to blood flow.
- Valves - at intervals along their length - prevent backflow / keep blood flowing in one direction.
- Carry blood back to the heart, at low pressure, flowing smoothly (no pulse).
- Contraction of the surrounding (skeletal) muscles squeezes the thin-walled veins and pushes the blood along from one set of valves to the next (reject: muscular walls of the vein).
- Vena cava: deoxygenated blood; from the body / head to the right atrium; thin walls; wide lumen; low pressure; valves to prevent backflow / one-way flow.
- Pulmonary vein in a table of features: oxygenated blood, wide lumen, thin muscle layer. Pulmonary artery: thick muscle layer, narrow lumen, flow away from the heart, deoxygenated blood.

COMPARING VESSELS
- Vein v artery: thin wall v thick wall; thin muscle / elastic layer v thick; wide lumen v narrow lumen; valves v no valves (reverse argument accepted).
- In cross-section: the artery has the thick wall and small round lumen, the vein the thin wall and large lumen - refer to the size / shape of the lumen and to the size of the wall / muscle / elastic layer. The thin inner lining is the same thickness in both.
- In a data table the artery has the thicker wall and narrower lumen; the vein the wider lumen; the capillary a wall less than 1 micrometre thick and a lumen about 0.008 mm across. Cross-sectional area of the lumen = 3.14 x radius x radius.
- Pulmonary artery compared with vena cava: both carry deoxygenated blood; pulmonary artery from the heart / right ventricle, to the lungs; vena cava from the body, to the heart / right atrium; vena cava is a vein with valves; pulmonary artery has a thick / muscular / elastic wall, a narrow / small lumen and high pressure (reverse statements accepted).
- Fish and human: the pressure in the aorta of a fish is lower than in the aorta of a human.

VALVES IN VEINS
- Drawing a valve: two flaps drawn towards the lumen from a similar height on opposite walls; both flaps pointing in the direction of blood flow (towards the heart).
- A closed valve in longitudinal section: flaps touching; the vessel is widest on the side where the blood is held back, with walls shown before and after the valve.
- Function: the flaps open / close; prevent backflow of blood / keep blood flowing in one direction; in blood returning from the lower regions of the body; where the pressure is low.
- Valves are found in veins (e.g. the renal vein) and at the base of the aorta; not in capillaries or other arteries (e.g. the renal artery). A vein in the forearm: has valves; carries deoxygenated blood; blood is returning to the heart.
- Blood is kept flowing from the foot to the heart: (remaining) pressure; movement by contraction of the skeletal muscles; in the veins (or a named vein); valves in the veins; which prevent backflow.
- Sitting still for a long time lets the lower legs swell because fewer leg-muscle contractions push blood along the veins; blood collecting in the veins makes the lower leg heavier.
- Vein demonstration on the forearm: the vessel visible under the skin is a vein; the swelling where blood stops is a valve. A stretch of vein disappears when emptied: the vein is empty / no blood is flowing through; blood has been pushed out; blood cannot flow back past the valve; the finger at the other end stops blood entering; the vein walls are thin so are not visible beneath the skin. Gripping a rod repeatedly first: muscle contraction; increases blood pressure; increases blood flow / helps fill the vein.
- In a section of a vein the valve flaps point in the direction of flow, i.e. towards the heart; the organ on the other side is one the blood has come from (e.g. the brain).
- With a cloth tied round the arm, one finger pressed on a vein and another stroking the blood along it, the vein lies flat because the pressed finger prevents more blood entering the vein and a valve further along prevents backflow (the cloth / bandage does neither).
- A vein used as a graft in place of a coronary artery is less suitable: thinner / weaker (less muscular) walls; has valves; pressure difference (mark the first answer on each line).

CAPILLARIES
- Join / connect arteries (arterioles) to veins (venules); form a network; run close to / between the body cells.
- Very small / narrow / microscopic - small diameter of lumen; red blood cells pass in single file (the width of a red blood cell).
- Walls thin / one cell thick (about 1 micrometre; accept epithelium). ("They are one cell thick" was ignored in one scheme - the wall must be mentioned.)
- No muscle / no elastic tissue (compared with an artery or vein).
- Walls leaky / permeable - allow passage of tissue fluid / plasma / substances (accept phagocytes).
- Very numerous / large surface area.
- Short distance for diffusion; slow blood flow; pressure falls along the capillary.
- Exchange by diffusion, to / from the cells / tissues: oxygen and glucose (also amino acids, hormones, ions / salts) pass out of the capillary; carbon dioxide (also urea, water) passes into the capillary.
- Naming a vessel as a capillary from a diagram - any two reasons: blood cells in single file; running between body cells; walls thin / permeable / one cell thick; substances pass through; vessels form a network.
- In excretory organs: thin walls / one cell thick; allow diffusion; short distance / close to cells; of urea / salts into the kidney; of carbon dioxide into the alveoli; and bring oxygen to the excretory tissues.
- An insect feeds from a capillary rather than an artery: the capillary is close to the surface; thin / one cell thick; low blood pressure (reverse for the artery).
- In a capillary round an alveolus, carbon dioxide diffuses fastest at the end joined to the pulmonary artery.
- A capillary is adapted for the transport (delivery / collection) of hormone molecules: very small / narrow; thin / one-cell-thick / leaky walls; runs close to the cells; very numerous / large surface area; so maximum uptake / offloading of the hormone; by diffusion.

STRUCTURE RELATED TO BLOOD PRESSURE
- Pressure falls from arteries, through capillaries, to veins (e.g. 13.0, 4.0, 0.6 kPa; pulmonary artery 14, capillaries 8, pulmonary vein 5 kPa).
- The heart / ventricle is the pump / source of the pressure; pressure depends on distance from the heart (arteries take blood from / are close to the heart).
- Resistance / friction in the vessels, especially the capillary network; fluid is lost from the capillaries (formation of tissue fluid).
- Arteries have a narrow lumen and thick / muscular / elastic walls; veins have a wider lumen and a thinner layer of muscle / elastic tissue.
- Arteries show a pulse; blood flows smoothly, with no pulse, in veins / venules.
- Vessel diameter falls to a minimum at the capillaries and then rises again; veins are wider than the corresponding arteries. Rate of flow: capillary slowest, then small vein, small artery, main artery fastest.
- Typical pressures: aorta about 16 kPa (120 mm Hg); pulmonary artery about 4 kPa (12 mm Hg); vena cava about 2 kPa; pulmonary vein about 1 kPa. A pressure graph for the pulmonary circuit has the same shape as the systemic one but lies below it at every point.
- Pulmonary artery v aorta: right ventricle wall thinner / left thicker; shorter distance (only to the lungs); little work against gravity; low pressure avoids damage to lung capillaries; the body needs high pressure for kidney filtration and to supply the brain.
- A cut that has damaged an artery: the blood is bright red / oxygenated; it leaves in spurts / shows a pulse (ignore references to pressure).
- Drugs that lower blood pressure by acting on vessels: arteries / arterioles; muscular wall; relaxes / constriction prevented; larger lumen / dilation / widening; deposits / atheroma reduced; less resistance / friction.
- Prolonged raised blood pressure: cumulative effect; damage to capillaries (thin walls); damage to kidneys / brain / heart / blood vessels.
- Range in a table = highest value minus lowest value (e.g. muscle in the aorta wall: range 7 in young adults, 28 in old adults - four times greater).

FOLLOWING A SUBSTANCE ROUND THE BODY
- Oxygen from the air to a muscle: nose / nasal passages, larynx / trachea; bronchus / bronchioles; air sacs / alveoli; diffuses into the blood / capillaries; carried by red blood cells / haemoglobin (as oxyhaemoglobin); pulmonary vein; pumped by the heart (atrium / ventricle contracts); arteries / arterioles.
- Carbon dioxide from a body cell to the lungs: diffusion; cytoplasm; across the cell membrane; tissue fluid; capillary; plasma / red blood cell; vein / vena cava; right side of the heart; atrium then ventricle; pulmonary artery; capillary; alveolus.
- An amino acid from the ileum to the liver: absorbed / diffuses; villus; capillary; blood plasma; in solution; hepatic portal vein. Alcohol follows the same route from the stomach / small intestine to the liver, where it is broken down.
- A chemical absorbed through the skin of a finger and tasted on the tongue: diffusion; epidermis; dermis; tissue fluid / plasma; capillary; venule / vein / vena cava; right atrium and right ventricle; pulmonary artery / vein; lungs; left atrium and left ventricle; aorta / artery / arteriole.
- Oxygen and carbohydrate to the liver: oxygen - alveoli / capillaries, pulmonary vein, aorta, hepatic artery, used in respiration / oxidation of glucose; carbohydrate - villi, hepatic portal vein, converted to / from glycogen or respired.

11.4 BLOOD

COMPOSITION OF BLOOD
- Plasma (the liquid part; mostly water).
- Red blood cells (erythrocytes).
- White blood cells: lymphocytes and phagocytes.
- Platelets.
- Dissolved (named) food substances in the plasma; other plasma components, e.g. urea, carbon dioxide, hormones, antibodies, fibrinogen.
- Relative proportions, with a figure quoted: plasma about 55 % of the volume, red blood cells about 44 %, white blood cells about 1 %.
- In centrifuged blood the denser cells settle to the bottom and the plasma forms the upper layer. Red cells are denser than white cells: haemoglobin; densely packed; contains iron; less water / solvent in red cells; solutes increase density.
- Matching components to functions: red blood cell - transports oxygen; platelet - involved in clotting; plasma - transports (e.g. fibrinogen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, antibodies); phagocyte - engulfs pathogens; lymphocyte - makes antibodies.
- An essay that only names components without explaining functions earns at most 1 mark (for any three named).

IDENTIFYING BLOOD CELLS IN PHOTOMICROGRAPHS AND DIAGRAMS
- Red blood cells: the numerous small round cells with no nucleus and a paler centre. Accept red blood cell / erythrocyte / RBC.
- White blood cells: larger, fewer, with a stained nucleus. Lobed nucleus = phagocyte (accept polymorph / neutrophil / macrophage); large round nucleus filling most of the cell = lymphocyte.
- The clear liquid between the cells = plasma. Tiny fragments = platelets.
- The same red cells look different seen from the side and from above: both are the same shape; seen from different angles.
- A red cell looks paler at its centre: it is thinner in the middle / biconcave; contains haemoglobin; more haemoglobin at the edges than at the centre; light passes more easily through the centre; there is no nucleus.
- Red blood cells of birds compared with mammals (comparisons must be valid pairs): nucleus v no nucleus; larger v smaller; larger than the white cells v smaller than the white cells; oval / egg-shaped v round disc; biconvex / not biconcave v biconcave. Reject: spherical; references to haemoglobin or surface area; "oval v biconcave".
- Red blood cells are the sample examined to detect the malarial parasite (infected cells show the pathogen inside them); infection with the malarial pathogen can cause anaemia because red blood cells are destroyed / their number is reduced.

RED BLOOD CELLS
- Function: transport / carry / absorb oxygen. Accept transport of carbon dioxide (one scheme). Reject "carry substances"; ignore "contain haemoglobin" given as a function.
- Contain haemoglobin; which binds / combines with oxygen; forming oxyhaemoglobin; haemoglobin absorbs oxygen quickly and carries it in the red blood cells.
- No nucleus (anucleate); so more room / volume / space for haemoglobin. No cell wall. Have cytoplasm and a cell membrane.
- Biconcave shape ("doughnut" shape); gives a large / increased surface area; for faster / more uptake / diffusion of oxygen in and out.
- Small / flexible / elastic / able to change shape; so they can squeeze through capillaries.
- Live for about 90 - 120 days (3 - 4 months).
- Iron (Fe) is the mineral ion needed to make haemoglobin - red blood cells are the component that cannot function effectively if the diet lacks iron.
- Oxygen entering the blood in the lungs: dissolves; diffuses (through the alveolus wall / capillary wall / plasma); combines with haemoglobin / forms oxyhaemoglobin. Changes in blood passing through lung capillaries: carbon dioxide lost; oxygen gained / becomes oxygenated ("gas exchange" = 1 mark); haemoglobin becomes oxyhaemoglobin; pH changes / becomes less acidic; down diffusion / concentration gradients.
- Diet short of iron for years: iron is needed for haemoglobin; less haemoglobin is formed; less oxygen is absorbed by the red blood cells; so more oxygen remains in the air breathed out.
- Carbon monoxide combines with haemoglobin (forming carboxyhaemoglobin): less oxygen is carried by the blood / red cells / haemoglobin to the organs.
- Unusually low number of red cells - symptoms: tiredness / fatigue / weakness / dizziness / fainting; inability to exercise / inactive; breathing problems; because of reduced oxygen transport; reduced respiration; reduced energy release; irregular menstruation.
- Factors changing the red blood cell count: iron in the diet; anaemia / sickle cell anaemia; pregnancy; menstruation; accidental loss of blood; altitude.
- High altitude: less oxygen in the atmosphere; more red blood cells / more haemoglobin are made; more oxygen carried / supplied as oxyhaemoglobin; gives athletes a competitive advantage. Other adaptations of people living at altitude: rapid / deeper breathing; modified lung structure; more (efficient) haemoglobin; more efficient blood supply to organs / larger heart; faster heart rate / circulation.
- More haemoglobin per unit volume (e.g. in birds): more / sufficient oxygen carried as oxyhaemoglobin; for aerobic respiration (reject anaerobic); releasing more / enough energy; for muscle contraction.
- Red cells that lose their shape, become less flexible or stick together (as in uncontrolled high blood glucose, or sickle cell anaemia): reduced surface area / volume; so less haemoglobin; less oxygen absorbed / carried / supplied; loss of elasticity - harder to fit / move through capillaries / vessels; sticky - clump together / clot; blockage of a blood vessel; reduced blood flow to the tissue; less aerobic respiration; less energy.
- A fish with colourless blood has no red blood cells / erythrocytes / haemoglobin. It survives in very cold water: less aerobic respiration; enzyme reactions are slower; less oxygen is needed; more oxygen is absorbed; more oxygen is carried in the plasma; more blood / oxygen is pumped (larger heart, wider vessels, more blood); to the organs / tissues / cells.
- Calculations: 0.470 dm3 = 470 cm3, so at 5000 million cells per cm3 one donation holds 2.35 x 10^12 red cells; 5 million per mm3 = 5 x 10^6; a diameter of 0.007 mm = 7 x 10^-3 mm.

WHITE BLOOD CELLS
- Function: immunity / immune response / destroy pathogens - bacteria / viruses / microorganisms / dead cells. Ignore "germs" and "foreign bodies".
- Phagocytes: engulf / ingest pathogens - phagocytosis (or described). Reject "digesting" for the act of engulfing.
- Lymphocytes: produce antibodies (accept antitoxins). The blood component that makes antibodies is white blood cells / lymphocytes (the abbreviation "WBC" was ignored in one scheme).
- White blood cells are responsible for the rejection of transplanted tissue.
- White blood cells do not produce antigens, convert fibrinogen or transport toxins.
- Fewer white cells than normal. Problem: reduced immunity / immune response / less able to fight infection / kill microorganisms / more likely to suffer (infectious) disease (accept a named disease, bacteria / viruses / fungi; reject leukaemia). Explanation: less phagocytosis; less antibody production; microorganisms / pathogens remain in the blood / body / are not destroyed.
- HIV reproduces in white blood cells and destroys them: the immune system / immunity is weakened; lymphocyte numbers fall; reduced antibody production; reduced phagocytic action.

ANTIBODIES
- Pathogens carry antigens - specific chemicals / proteins on their surface; recognised as foreign.
- Lymphocytes produce antibodies.
- Antibodies are proteins; carried in the blood plasma.
- Each antibody is specific; its shape / structure is complementary to / fits the antigen (not "the same shape").
- The antibody binds to the antigen (antibody-antigen complex).
- This leads to direct destruction of the pathogen / virus / bacterium;
- or marks the pathogen (points it out / clumps pathogens together / immobilises them) for destruction / engulfing by phagocytes (macrophages).
- Antibodies destroy bacteria but not the body's own cells: the antigens / proteins are on the surface of the bacteria (not on human cells); they are recognised as foreign; the antibody is specific to that antigen.
- After infection or vaccination, memory cells are produced; antibody production on the next infection is rapid / increased. Testing blood for a specific antibody shows a past infection even when the pathogen is no longer in the blood / is only inside cells, because the lymphocytes will have made antibodies that remain in the blood.
- Antibodies compared with antibiotics: both cure disease / fight infection; antibodies are proteins, in the blood / plasma, produced by white blood cells / lymphocytes / internally by the body, in response to / against antigens / pathogens, and are more specific; antibodies affect viruses; antibiotics are drugs / medicines - artificial / manufactured (produced externally / in a laboratory / fermenter, often using fungi), taken / swallowed / injected, used only against bacteria and not viruses, and assist the immune system.
- Transplants and transfusions: white blood cells / lymphocytes make antibodies against foreign proteins / antigens, causing rejection; blood groups / types must match; relatives have similar proteins / tissues so rejection is less likely. Tissue treated before transplant has its antigens / proteins removed / blocked / coated (or made like human tissue): it is not recognised; by white blood cells / lymphocytes / phagocytes; the immune response is reduced; fewer antibodies / less phagocytosis.
- A person's blood group needs to be known for blood transfusion / tissue or organ transplant; to avoid clotting of the blood / prevent rejection; or to check paternity.
- A mother's antibodies do not usually meet the antigens of her fetus: the placenta; keeps the two blood streams separate; antibodies are too big / cannot pass across the placenta / through capillary walls. CAUTION - this ruling comes from one question about a mother's antibodies and the antigens of her fetus. Other mark schemes list antibodies among the substances that pass from the mother's blood to the fetus across the placenta, and credit "fewer antibodies (passed) across the placenta" as the reason why babies born prematurely are at higher risk of infectious disease.
- Variation in immune response between people: numbers of lymphocytes / ability to produce antibodies; HIV infection; age; sex; genetics; diet; previous exposure to the pathogen / immunity / vaccination.
- A blood test for antibodies is delayed until some time after possible infection: time is needed for the production of antibodies / for the immune response.

PLATELETS AND CLOTTING
- Platelets are involved in blood clotting; they plug damaged vessels (plug the hole).
- Platelets (release enzymes that) cause fibrinogen to change to fibrin. Reject "fibres" for fibrin.
- Soluble fibrinogen is converted to insoluble fibrin; which forms threads / a mesh.
- The mesh traps red blood cells; forming a clot; which dries to a scab.
- Importance: stops bleeding / prevents blood loss; prevents infection / entry of pathogens (bacteria).
- Order of involvement: platelets, fibrinogen, fibrin, red blood cells.
- Older schemes also credit thrombokinase / thromboplastin / prothrombin / thrombin / antithrombin / factor VIII as clotting proteins.
- Cuts that do not heal and blood that does not clot easily: a lower than normal level of platelets. A chemical that prevents clotting stops the animal making fibrinogen / fibrin.
- After a cut - complete sequence: capillaries / blood vessels damaged; bleeding; platelets; fibrinogen; fibrin; clotting; scab; new cell growth (reproduction / growth of skin cells); skin re-seals / barrier to bacteria restored; white blood cells; antibodies / antitoxins (clumping of bacteria); phagocytosis.
- "Anti-platelet" drugs are given before a stent is fitted: the natural response to damage is for blood to clot; platelets cause fibrinogen to change to fibrin; the drug prevents clotting.

PLASMA
- Mostly water; acting as a solvent - substances are carried dissolved / in solution.
- Transports nutrients: glucose; amino acids; mineral ions / salts (accept named); vitamins (accept named); fats / fatty acids and glycerol.
- Transports urea; carbon dioxide; hormones; plasma (blood) proteins such as fibrinogen and antibodies.
- Transports blood cells and platelets.
- Transports heat.
- Serves the body cells / carries hormones to target organs.
- One function of plasma: transport of a named substance (accept blood clotting).
- Most carbon dioxide is carried in the plasma (as hydrogencarbonate ions, HCO3-) and leaves the blood from the plasma into the moisture lining the alveolus and then the alveolar air.
- Mineral ions in plasma with functions: iron for haemoglobin / red blood cells; calcium for bones / teeth / blood clotting (accept others with a correct function, e.g. sodium / potassium for nerve impulses; reject nitrogen, iodine, sulfur given as elements).
- Blood compared with urine: blood has cells / platelets / plasma, protein / antibodies / amino acids / fats, glucose and more hormones / vitamins; urine has none of these, a higher urea concentration and more salts / water.
- Blood tests as a guide to health (at most 3 marks for factors with no explanation): glucose concentration - diabetes; urea concentration - kidney disease; number of red cells - ability to deliver oxygen / shortage of iron / anaemia; shape of red cells - sickle cell anaemia; number of white cells - cancer / leukaemia / infection; cholesterol - blocking of arteries; specific antibodies - immunity / previous exposure to a pathogen.

TISSUE FLUID AND EXCHANGE WITH CELLS
- Blood in the capillary is under pressure; the capillary wall is thin / one cell thick / leaky.
- Plasma / soluble materials are squeezed (leak) out of the capillaries to form tissue fluid - at the arterial, high-pressure end. Blood pressure, not osmosis or active transport, causes this transfer.
- Liquid in the capillary = plasma; liquid round the cells = tissue fluid (older scheme also accepts ECF / lymph / interstitial fluid / plasma for the fluid leaving; reject blood).
- Tissue fluid has fewer protein molecules than plasma; both contain dissolved glucose and oxygen (and white blood cells can pass through).
- From capillary to cells: oxygen; glucose; other soluble substances such as amino acids, hormones, ions, vitamins (direction must be given).
- From cells to capillary: carbon dioxide; urea / waste products; water.
- Movement is by diffusion; across the (partially permeable) cell membrane; water returns to the capillary at the low-pressure (venous) end by osmosis.
- Pressure in a capillary is highest at the end where blood enters from the artery.
- Carbon dioxide leaving a cell: diffusion; from the cytoplasm; through the cell membrane; into tissue fluid; into the capillary; carried in the plasma / red blood cells.
- Blood vessels supplying a tissue: bring glucose / amino acids / minerals / vitamins; for energy / repair of cells; bring oxygen; for (aerobic) respiration; take away urea / carbon dioxide; by diffusion; bring white blood cells; to fight infection.
- Substances passing from liver cells into the blood: urea (accept uric acid / creatinine); carbon dioxide; iron; glucose; vitamins; water.
- Hormones are transported in solution; in the blood; in the plasma.

13.1 EXCRETION

DEFINITION OF EXCRETION
- Removal (from the organism / body).
- Of toxic (poisonous) materials / toxins.
- And waste products.
- Of metabolism / made by the body (metabolism may be described, e.g. from respiration).
- Examples accepted in a definition: urea, carbon dioxide.
- Reject: egestion. Maximum of 3 marks for the definition where four points are listed.

CARBON DIOXIDE
- Carbon dioxide is a waste product of respiration.
- It is excreted through the lungs / alveoli; breathed out / exhaled (accept the action of one set of breathing muscles, e.g. the diaphragm relaxes / moves up / domes).
- The lungs are organs of excretion because: carbon dioxide (accept water); is removed from the body; it is a waste product / toxic material; of respiration; which is a metabolic process (max 3).
- The lung is the organ that excretes most carbon dioxide from the body.
- Carbon dioxide must be removed because it is a metabolic waste product and is poisonous.
- Diffusion and muscle action (not evaporation) help the excretion of carbon dioxide from the lungs.
- In expired air only the carbon dioxide (about 4 %) is excreted material. The excretory product absorbed from expired air by soda lime-type absorbers: carbon dioxide (allow water / water vapour).

UREA
- Urea is the excretory molecule made in the liver; from excess amino acids. Accept urea / uric acid for "a waste product formed from proteins in the liver"; ignore "nitrogenous waste".
- The part of the amino acid molecule removed and used to make urea is the nitrogen-containing (amino, NH2) group.
- Nitrogen is the element in urea that shows it is formed from amino acids and not from glucose.
- The liver is where urea is produced (identify the liver in a diagram of the abdominal organs). The liver also breaks down alcohol and converts glycogen to glucose; it does not secrete digestive enzymes.
- Urea and carbon dioxide (accept uric acid / creatinine) pass from the liver cells into the blood capillaries.
- Removal of urea by the kidneys is called excretion (not dialysis, diffusion or egestion).

SUBSTANCES EXCRETED BY HUMANS AND THE ORGANS INVOLVED
- Carbon dioxide; by the lungs / alveoli; in the breath.
- Urea; salts; water; drugs (or a named drug); toxins / broken-down hormones; by the kidneys; in urine.
- Also through the skin; in sweat (urea in sweat is an excretory product).
- Bile salts / bile pigments (older scheme).
- A fetus gets rid of its waste products across the placenta into the mother's blood: carbon dioxide and urea (accept nitrogenous waste / a named excretory product), by diffusion.

EXCRETION COMPARED WITH EGESTION
- Excretion: removal via lungs / bladder / skin (accept kidneys); of any two from toxins, nitrogenous waste (accept urea / uric acid), carbon dioxide; which are metabolic wastes.
- Egestion: removal from the alimentary canal / gut / rectum / anus; of undigested material - cellulose / lignin / fibre / roughage / faeces.
- Removal of faeces is not excretion because faeces are undigested food; e.g. cellulose / fibre / lignin / roughage; not produced by metabolism.
- Undigested food in faeces is not an excretory product.
- In multiple-choice items asking for an example of excretion the keyed answer is removal of carbon dioxide from the lungs; release of a hormone into the blood, removal of undigested food / faeces, and release of sweat / water from the sweat glands are the distractors (although urea in sweat is counted as an excretory product in another item).

EXCRETION IN PLANTS
- Release of oxygen from a plant (a leaf on a sunny day) is excretion.
- Waste products removed from a plant: carbon dioxide out (at night) and oxygen out (by day); out by diffusion; through the stomata / guard cells; also by leaf fall.

13.2 URINARY SYSTEM

STRUCTURES AND THEIR FUNCTIONS
- Kidney: makes urine; removes urea (nitrogenous waste / uric acid) - reject "removes urine"; removes excess salts / minerals / ions; removes toxins / poisons / hormones (reject "harmful" substances); removes water in excess (osmoregulation / varying amounts of water); by filtration of / removal from the blood / plasma; this is excretion.
- Ureter: carries urine from the kidney; to the bladder. Its wall shows peristalsis - wave-like / rhythmic; contraction of muscles (if named they must be circular); which pushes the urine along (reject "urea" alone); to the bladder.
- Bladder (urinary bladder; reject gall bladder): stores / holds / collects urine before it is released.
- Urethra: carries urine; from the bladder; to the outside of the body. In the male it also carries semen / sperm / seminal fluid (through the penis).
- Ureter and urethra compared: both carry urine (urea); ureter from kidney to bladder; urethra from bladder out of the body; urethra carries semen / sperm in the male. Similarity between the sexes: the urethra carries urine in both (reject just "urea"); difference: it carries semen / sperm / gametes only in the male.
- The spellings ureter and urethra must be correct to be credited.
- Renal artery: brings blood containing urea / waste products to the kidney - oxygenated. Renal vein: takes blood away from the kidney - deoxygenated, with the lowest urea concentration.
- Matching: renal artery - oxygenated blood; renal vein - deoxygenated blood; ureter - urine to the bladder; urethra - urine from the bladder.
- The route of urine: kidney -> ureter -> bladder -> urethra. The fluid is urine (reject urea).
- In a diagram: the renal artery comes from the aorta, the renal vein joins the vena cava, the ureter runs down from each kidney to the bladder, and the single urethra leaves the bladder.
- In the male: ureter carries urine only; urethra carries urine and sperm; sperm duct (vas deferens) carries sperm only; rectum carries neither. An enlarged prostate gland closes / restricts the urethra and so hinders urination (prevents the exit of urine from the bladder); ignore references to the bladder or to pain.
- The kidney is an organ; the organ that contains many nephrons.
- Volume of liquid passing per day: the renal vein carries slightly less than the renal artery but much more than the ureter.

WHY UREA MUST BE REMOVED
- Urea is toxic / a toxin.
- The liquid passing out of the urethra is urine and the waste product it contains is urea (accept ammonia / excess water / excess minerals).
- Most nitrogen compounds are excreted by the kidneys. People who need dialysis have kidneys that are not functioning correctly.

THE NEPHRON
- Glomerulus: the knot of capillaries that filters the blood (the capillary structures that filter urea from the blood).
- Bowman's capsule: the cup-shaped start of the nephron around the glomerulus.
- Tubule; the hairpin loop is the loop of Henle; surrounded by blood capillaries.
- Collecting duct: the final tube, which nephrons join and from which urine leaves.

FILTRATION
- Water, glucose, urea and ions (also amino acids) pass out of the blood into the Bowman's capsule / enter the nephron; by filtration (filtered from the blood).
- Protein does not enter the Bowman's capsule / the nephron / is not filtered / is not removed from the blood; because protein molecules are large. Blood cells and platelets also stay in the blood.
- Urea is lower in the renal vein than in the renal artery because: urea passes into / is removed by the kidney; by filtration; in the nephron / Bowman's capsule / glomerulus; and is excreted / passes out in the urine.

REABSORPTION
- All the glucose is reabsorbed / absorbed back into the blood (ignore "taken back into the blood"); selectively / by active transport ("selective reabsorption" earns both marks). Glucose concentration in the tubule falls to zero in its first part.
- Amino acids are also totally reabsorbed.
- Some ions are reabsorbed.
- Most of the water is reabsorbed - the volume of water in the tubule falls along the nephron (about 99 % of the water entering the kidneys is reabsorbed).
- Urea is not all reabsorbed - most stays in the tubule.
- Percentage reabsorbed = (amount entering in the blood - amount leaving in the urine) / amount entering x 100; e.g. water 150 dm3 to 1.5 dm3 = 99 %; glucose 150 g to 0 g = 100 %; urea 50 g to 30 g = 40 %.

URINE
- Urine contains: water; urea (accept nitrogenous waste or other named, e.g. uric acid, creatinine); salts / ions (accept named, e.g. sodium, chloride, ammonium, calcium). Also accepted: hormones, pigments, enzymes, toxins / drugs.
- Urine of a healthy person contains no glucose (reject sugar / glucose), no protein, no amino acids and no blood cells.
- Water in urine is important as a solvent - it dissolves urea / salts.
- Blood (renal artery) compared with urine (ureter): blood has cells / platelets / plasma, protein / antibodies / amino acids / fats, glucose, and more hormones / vitamins; urine has none of these; urine has a higher urea concentration, more toxins and more salts / ions / water. Ignore oxygen / carbon dioxide (older scheme credits them) and "minerals".
- Urine compared with blood plasma from a data table: less urea in plasma; more sodium in plasma; less chloride in plasma; quote comparative data with units; manipulate the data (e.g. urea 50 times higher in urine).
- Renal artery compared with renal vein and ureter from a data table: amino acids and protein the same in artery and vein; glucose the same or slightly higher in the artery; mineral ions and urea higher in the artery, by up to the amount that appears in the urine.
- Renal vein compared with renal artery: less urea, less oxygen, more carbon dioxide, less water (and fewer salts).

VOLUME AND CONCENTRATION OF URINE
- Hot day / more sweating: water is lost as sweat; a lower volume of / less urine; with less water / more concentrated; the kidneys reabsorb more water; to keep the blood concentration constant; fewer salts / nitrogenous compounds remain to be removed in urine because some leave in sweat. Reverse for a cold day (less water reabsorbed, more urine).
- After drinking a large volume of water: water is absorbed into the blood; the rise / excess in the water content of the blood is detected (accept hypothalamus / a correct reference to ADH); more water / a higher volume of water is excreted - more urine is produced; by the kidney (nephron); the ion concentration of the urine decreases; this is homeostasis - it keeps the concentration of water in the blood constant; urine passes via the ureter, bladder and urethra. The water potential of blood in the renal arteries and of urine in the ureters rises, with little change in the renal veins.
- Organ that makes absorbed water become water in urine: the kidney; it filters / removes water from the blood.
- Diet: more protein (reverse argument accepted); means more amino acids; broken down in / converted by the liver (deamination); so more urea in the urine / urine more concentrated; less water or more salts / ions in the diet also make the urine more concentrated. Glucose / sugar is credited only with reference to diabetes; ignore specific foods.
- A drink that produces the most urine should be avoided on a very hot day (marked independently of the drink named): it increases the volume of / water in urine; water is already being lost in sweat; more than usual; sweating is needed for temperature regulation / to keep cool; danger of dehydration / increased thirst. Ignore heat loss in urine.
- A meat-only diet on a hot day of energetic work: more urea in the urine; water lost in sweat; blood concentration has to be maintained; more water reabsorbed in the kidneys / less water in the urine / urine more concentrated.
- Investigation of urea in urine: mean = total / number of results, given to the stated significant figures (12.8 / 6 = 2.133 = 2.1). The group with the higher mean ate more protein; so more amino acids were deaminated to produce urea; so more urea was filtered / passed into the urine in the kidney. Both groups should drink the same volume of water: the volume drunk affects the volume of water used to form urine; and therefore the concentration of urea in the urine.
- A low mass of urea excreted could be due to low protein in the diet, kidneys functioning incorrectly or a liver functioning incorrectly - not to low salts in the diet.
- Kidney disease: urea in the sweat is higher; because filtration / removal of urea by the kidney is reduced; so more urea remains in the blood plasma.
- How the kidneys keep the blood at a constant concentration and why it matters: homeostasis / osmoregulation; salts / ions / urea; and water; are removed from the blood; with reabsorption into the blood; in varying quantities / of the excess; depending on food and water intake; and on loss in sweating; tissue fluid is therefore kept at a constant concentration; otherwise water would enter or leave cells; by osmosis; cells would swell / burst / shrink; enzyme action / metabolism would be affected.

THE LIVER: ASSIMILATION OF AMINO ACIDS AND DEAMINATION
- The liver is the organ that metabolises amino acids.
- Amino acids are made into proteins (e.g. plasma proteins / a named protein).
- An example of assimilation is the building of proteins from amino acids (absorption of glycerol, breakdown of glycogen or alcohol in the liver and release of a hormone are not assimilation).
- Alcohol, drugs and hormones such as progesterone are all broken down by the liver.
- Excess amino acids are broken down - deamination (or described: removal of the nitrogen-containing part).
- Forming urea; and a carbohydrate (glucose), which can be respired (lost as carbon dioxide) or stored as glycogen.
- Urea is formed from amino acids, in the liver; it is produced in the liver, filtered from the blood by the kidney, and stored (in urine) in the bladder.
- Other liver functions credited: converts glucose to glycogen and back; stores glycogen (insoluble, so no effect on the water potential of cells); breaks down / removes alcohol, drugs, toxins, lactic acid; breaks down hormones (preventing their continued effect); stores iron and vitamins; breaks down haemoglobin / red blood cells; makes bile (emulsifies lipids, increasing the surface area for lipase); high metabolic rate - heat production. The liver does not convert glucose to amino acids.
- Hormones, once they have acted, are destroyed / broken down; by the liver; and the products are removed (excreted by the kidneys).
- Blood that has passed through the liver: carbon dioxide increased, glucose increased, oxygen reduced, urea increased. In liver capillaries oxygen and amino acids pass out to the cells; carbon dioxide and urea pass into the blood.
- The liver is a major region for the assimilation of amino acids (to be identified on a diagram of the digestive system).

PATHWAYS
- Urea from the liver to the outside: transported in the blood / plasma; dissolved; hepatic vein to vena cava / heart; (lungs and heart again); renal artery; to the kidney; removed / filtered from the blood; becomes part of the urine; ureter; bladder; urethra.
- Protein eaten to urea excreted: digested / broken down / hydrolysed; into amino acids; by protease (or a named protease); absorbed in the small intestine (duodenum / ileum); into villi / capillaries / blood; hepatic portal vein; to the liver; deamination; urea carried in the renal artery; to the kidney; blood filtered / urea leaves the blood; forms urine; bladder / urethra.
- In a flow chart: protein / polypeptide -> (protease) -> amino acids -> liver -> urea (reject urine); carbohydrate stored in the liver = glycogen.
- An amino acid in the liver until its elements leave the body: made into protein; or deaminated; carbohydrate / glucose produced; respired - lost as carbon dioxide; or stored as glycogen; urea; hepatic vein; renal artery; kidney; ureter; urine (or sweat); bladder / urethra.
- Highest urea in the blood: hepatic vein; lowest: renal vein; urea is most concentrated in the ureter (urine).

REMOVAL OF EXCRETORY PRODUCTS - SUMMARY ANSWERS
- Toxic / poisonous materials (reject "harmful"); waste products of metabolism; urea; made in the liver, carried in the blood to the kidneys; leaves in urine; via the ureter, bladder and urethra (urination); water / salts / urea also leave in sweat; from sweat glands in the skin, via sweat ducts / pores, the materials coming from the blood capillaries; carbon dioxide (and water); from cells / blood to the alveoli by diffusion from the capillaries; exhaled / breathed out.
- Capillaries in excretory organs: thin walls / one cell thick; allow diffusion; short distance / close to the cells; of urea / salts into the kidney tubule; of carbon dioxide into the alveoli; and bring oxygen to the excretory tissues.
- Urine added to faeces makes a better fertiliser: a larger range of / more ions; more water; urea / nitrogenous compounds; which are easier / quicker to convert to nitrate.

14.1 MAMMALIAN NERVOUS SYSTEM

PARTS OF THE NERVOUS SYSTEM
- Central nervous system (CNS): brain; and spinal cord.
- Peripheral nervous system (PNS): the nerves / nervous tissue / neurones outside the CNS.
- Components of the nervous system other than the brain: spinal cord; nerves / neurones; a named receptor / sense organ.
- Receptors respond to changes in the environment (stimuli) and send impulses to the CNS; receptors are found at one end of sensory neurones.
- Effectors are muscles or glands; they carry out the response.
- All neurones transmit electrical impulses. Write "impulse" / "electrical impulse" - "signal" and "message" are ignored / rejected.
- Generating and conducting nerve impulses needs energy from respiration.
- Units: 1 mm = 1000 micrometres, so 0.00003 mm = 0.03 micrometres (the width of a synaptic gap in one question).

THE THREE TYPES OF NEURONE
- Sensory (afferent) neurone: carries impulses from the receptor / sense organ; to the CNS / spinal cord / brain. Impulses travel towards the CNS. Reject "nerve" where neurone is required.
- Relay (intermediate / inter- / connector) neurone: lies within the CNS (in the grey matter of the spinal cord, or in the brain); carries impulses from the sensory neurone; to the motor neurone. Impulses travel within the CNS.
- Motor (efferent / effector) neurone: carries impulses from the CNS / spinal cord / brain (from the relay neurone); to the effector - muscle or gland. Impulses travel away from the CNS.
- If no neurone is named, no marks are given for describing roles; when more than the required number are described the best are marked.
- Motor and sensory neurones compared. Similarities: both carry impulses; quickly; over long distances; during voluntary, involuntary or reflex actions. Differences: motor - from CNS to effector; sensory - from receptor to CNS.
- In a diagram through the spinal cord: the sensory neurone enters from the receptor (its cell body lies in a swelling on the root outside the cord); the relay neurone lies inside the cord; the motor neurone leaves the cord for the muscle.
- In temperature control, sensory neurones carry information from skin receptors to the brain and motor neurones carry it from the brain to the sweat glands.

REFLEX ACTIONS
- Definition: rapid (accept quick / fast / sudden / immediate / instant);
- automatic (accept involuntary / unconscious / instinctive / spontaneous / no decision or conscious thought involved / cannot be controlled);
- response / reaction;
- to a stimulus.
- Ignore "brain" in the definition; reject "no involvement of the brain".
- Value: automatic / innate / does not have to be learnt; protective / aids survival / limits damage (accept an example, e.g. prevents too much light entering the eye).
- Examples: withdrawing a hand from a hot or sharp object; blinking when an object approaches the eye; pupil change in bright light; jumping at a loud noise; eyes watering; sneezing. Not reflexes: steering a car, lifting a book, solving a problem.
- To describe one example: name the stimulus; name the correct receptor; describe the action; explain why that action is important.
- A simple limb reflex involves the spinal cord; the brain is informed of what has happened (it receives information) but does not initiate the response. Reject the brain if it is said to direct / decide the response (marking stops at that point in one scheme); reject "consciousness involved".

THE REFLEX ARC - SEQUENCE
- Stimulus (or a named example).
- Detected by a receptor (or correctly named; reject a named sense organ where the receptor is required) - the receptor converts the stimulus into an impulse.
- Impulse / electrical impulse;
- passes along the sensory neurone;
- to the CNS / spinal cord;
- across a synapse;
- to the relay neurone;
- (across a synapse) to the motor neurone;
- to the effector - a muscle (accept the correct named muscle, e.g. biceps / flexor for withdrawal) or gland;
- which contracts / acts to give the response (described, and correct for the stimulus).
- Sequence mark: receptor / sensory / relay / CNS points must come before motor / effector / muscle points.
- Flow diagram: receptor -> sensory neurone -> central nervous system (relay neurone) -> motor neurone -> effector -> response.
- Blink reflex: stimulus detected by receptors (in the retina - rods / cones - or in the cornea; ignore "eye"); impulse in the sensory (optic) neurone; relay neurone in the CNS; motor neurone; eyelid muscles (the effector); which contract to close the eye.
- Pupil reflex arc: retina -> sensory neurone -> relay neurone (in the brain) -> motor neurone -> iris muscle.
- Labelling a pin-prick reflex: the receptor is in the skin of the hand where the pin touches; the effector is the muscle of the arm.

REFLEX AND VOLUNTARY (DELIBERATE) ACTIONS
- Reflex: started by an (external) stimulus acting on a receptor; protective / always the same response; immediate / rapid; not learnt / automatic / instinctive / involuntary; the impulse takes the shortest pathway; effectors are muscles or glands.
- Voluntary: controlled / involves a decision / conscious; initiated from the brain; may be slower; always involves the brain.
- Named types of response: reflex / involuntary / unintentional (reject "spinal reflex"); voluntary / deliberate / intentional.
- Sequences: reflex with a gland as effector (eyes watering) - receptor, impulse, sensory neurone, brain, motor neurone, gland; voluntary action - brain, impulse, spinal cord, motor neurone, muscle, contraction; a loud noise followed by a faster heartbeat - receptor, impulse, sensory neurone, brain, motor neurone, gland, hormone, (heart) muscle.

BECOMING AWARE OF A STIMULUS
- Stimulus (stimulates - reject "detects" for the stimulus).
- Receptor (e.g. taste bud / sensory endings in the tongue; receptors in the skin; retina / rods / cones in the eye).
- Impulses (reject messages).
- Sensory neurone (reject nerve in the older schemes).
- Synapse; relay neurone.
- To the brain / CNS (ignore spinal cord for taste).

DAMAGE AND BLOCKS
- Damage to the spinal cord: impulses are not transmitted; so the effector (or a named effector) is not activated.
- Sensory neurone cut / blocked: no pain is felt and there is no reflex movement; the muscles can still be moved because the motor neurone is intact.
- Motor neurone blocked: the stimulus is felt but the limb cannot be moved.
- Block in the spinal cord between the reflex arc and the brain: the limb moves in response to the stimulus but nothing is felt.
- Relay neurone damaged: impulses cannot pass from the sensory neurone to the motor neurone.
- Pathway from the brain damaged: the reflex still happens but cannot be controlled consciously.
- Sensory root cut on one side of the spinal cord: loss of some sensation on that side of the body.

SYNAPSES
- A synapse is the junction between two neurones (accept synaptic cleft / synaptic gap as the name of the junction).
- The impulse arriving stimulates the release of neurotransmitter molecules;
- from vesicles;
- into the synaptic gap / cleft;
- the neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the gap;
- and bind with receptor proteins (receptor molecules / sites) on the membrane of the next neurone;
- this causes an impulse to be stimulated / initiated in the next (post-synaptic / relay) neurone.
- Role in a reflex arc: transmission of the impulse between neurones; in one direction.
- One-way transmission: vesicles contain / release the neurotransmitter; vesicles are only on one side of the gap; receptor proteins are in the cell membrane only on the other side of the gap.
- On a diagram the impulse arrow is drawn from the neurone containing vesicles to the neurone that does not. A diagram showing neurotransmitter molecules attached to the second membrane shows the "bind with receptor proteins" stage.
- Drugs acting at synapses. A pain-killing (analgesic) molecule has a similar shape to the neurotransmitter; fits / binds with the receptor proteins; blocks them / stops the neurotransmitter binding; so information from the pain receptor cannot reach the brain. Nicotine, with a shape similar to a neurotransmitter, binds to the receptor proteins on the post-synaptic membrane and impulses are generated / more impulses travel. Depressants (alcohol, heroin): impulses pass more slowly; between neurones; reaction times are longer / reflexes slower.

DATA
- Comparing reductions in impulse speed: convert each to the same form - e.g. 0.14 m/s per year for 20 years = 2.8 m/s; 10 % of 55 m/s = 5.5 m/s.

14.2 MAMMALIAN SENSE ORGANS

RECEPTORS AND STIMULI
- Receptor cells detect stimuli; in the eye the light receptors (photoreceptors - rods and cones) are in the retina.
- Receptors / nerve endings in the skin detect touch, pressure, pain and temperature (mark the first answer on each line; "detection of stimuli" alone earns 1 mark at most). They are unevenly distributed - e.g. a high density in fingers / lips; exposed areas need higher sensitivity.
- Taste buds / sensory endings in the tongue are the receptors for taste.
- The eye is an organ; its light receptor cells (rods / cones) are cells; the retina, cornea, iris, sclera, conjunctiva, muscle and suspensory ligament are tissues.

IDENTIFYING PARTS OF THE EYE IN A SECTION
- Cornea: the transparent, curved front of the eye.
- Conjunctiva and cornea cover the pupil at the front of the eye.
- Iris: the coloured ring in front of the lens; contains the circular and radial muscles.
- Pupil: the opening in the centre of the iris (the space in front of the lens was also credited as aqueous humour / anterior chamber).
- Lens: behind the pupil; held by the suspensory ligaments.
- Suspensory ligaments: between the lens and the ciliary body.
- Ciliary muscles (ciliary body): the ring round the lens to which the ligaments attach. In a front view the outer ring is the ciliary muscle and the radiating strands are the suspensory ligaments.
- Retina: the inner layer at the back of the eye; contains the receptor cells sensitive to light; where the image is detected.
- Fovea (accept yellow spot / macula): the small region of the retina directly behind the lens; has the highest concentration / greatest density of light receptors.
- Optic nerve: leaves the back of the eye; carries impulses to the brain.
- Blind spot: where the optic nerve leaves the retina.
- Choroid: the dark (pigmented) layer outside the retina; its blood vessels supply the retina - they bring glucose / amino acids / minerals / vitamins for energy / repair; bring oxygen for respiration; remove urea / carbon dioxide; by diffusion; bring white blood cells to fight infection.
- Order in which light passes: cornea -> aqueous humour -> (pupil) -> lens -> vitreous humour -> retina. Light does not pass through the sclera.

FUNCTIONS
- Cornea: refracts / bends light (most of the bending happens here).
- Lens: transparent / curved sides - allows light through and refracts / focuses it; flexible / elastic / able to change shape - allows accommodation (focusing on objects at different distances). One mark for the structural feature and one for its function.
- The cornea and the lens focus light rays onto the retina (with the ciliary muscle changing the lens shape).
- Suspensory ligaments (max 3): attached to the ciliary body / muscles; can be loose / slack or taut / tight; pull on the lens; control / change the shape of the lens; allow accommodation.
- Iris: controls the size of the pupil and so the amount of light entering.
- Retina: light is detected by the light receptors (rods / cones); nerve impulses are triggered; and pass along the optic nerve to the brain.
- Ray diagram: rays travel parallel from a distant object until they reach the cornea; converge at the cornea; converge further at the lens; and meet on the retina (not in front of it).

PUPIL REFLEX
- It is a reflex / involuntary action (older scheme: reflex / autonomic / automatic / involuntary; reject spinal / conditioned).
- Bright light: retina / rods / light-sensitive cells detect the increased light intensity; impulses pass along the optic nerve / sensory neurone; to the brain / CNS (the relay neurones are in the brain); impulses return along a motor neurone to the iris muscles (the effector).
- Bright light: circular muscles of the iris contract; radial muscles relax; the pupil constricts / narrows / becomes smaller (reject "contracts" for the pupil); less light enters.
- Dim light: radial muscles contract; circular muscles relax; the pupil dilates / widens / gets bigger; more light enters and reaches the retina / rods / cones.
- The circular and radial muscles are antagonistic - when one set contracts the other relaxes. (The iris, the wall of the oesophagus and the upper arm have antagonistic muscles; the heart ventricle does not.)
- Reject any reference to ciliary muscles or suspensory ligaments in the pupil reflex.
- Importance: protection / prevents damage; rapidly; of the retina / rods / cones / light receptors; from too much light / bright light / high light intensity. Drugs that prevent the reflex let too much light enter and damage the retina / light-sensitive cells.
- Receptors for the reflex are in the retina; the effectors are the muscles of the iris.
- Drawing the eye in bright light: the pupil is drawn with a smaller diameter (a shaded circle within the iris, smaller than before).
- Drops that keep the pupil wide open make the radial iris muscles contract. When a person is shocked (adrenaline) the pupils widen.
- Graphs: pupil diameter is larger at low light intensity and smaller at high intensity (inverse relationship; "proportional" alone is rejected); the diameter changes most rapidly at intermediate intensities and levels off at high intensity. A fall in diameter means the light is getting brighter - the steeper the fall the faster the increase in light intensity (taking off sunglasses on a sunny day gives a sudden fall). A slow rise in diameter means the light slowly became dimmer. In a darkened room the pupil is wide; it narrows when a light is turned on and widens again when it is turned off.
- Percentage decrease in diameter = (original - new) / original x 100 (e.g. 5.7 mm to 2.6 mm = 54.4 %).
- An albino has no pigment in the iris / choroid (reject "eye"): too much light enters / is reflected inside the eye / reaches the retina (no shading / shielding of the retina); damaging the retina / receptors / light-sensitive cells (reject "damage to eyes").

ACCOMMODATION - NEAR OBJECT
- Light is refracted / bent by the cornea.
- Ciliary muscles contract.
- Suspensory ligaments slacken / become loose / less tension.
- Lens becomes fatter / thicker / rounder / more spherical / more convex.
- Light is refracted / bent more by the lens.
- Light / image is focused on the retina (fovea).
- As an object approaches, the ciliary muscles contract more, the ligaments have less tension and the lens becomes more rounded; the faster the lens curvature rises on a graph, the faster the object is approaching.
- Reading glasses bring a near image into focus (max 3): the spectacle lens is convex; light rays are bent / refracted / converged; before entering the eye / eye lens; this compensates for the thinness / lack of convexity of the eye lens; so the rays focus on the retina / fovea (yellow spot).

ACCOMMODATION - DISTANT OBJECT
- Ciliary muscles relax.
- Suspensory ligaments tighten / become taut / are pulled tight / under tension.
- Increased pull / tension on the lens; the lens is pulled / stretched.
- Lens becomes thinner / flatter / longer / less convex.
- Light is refracted less (reject "bent more"); longer focal length.
- Light converges / the image forms on the retina / fovea.
- Lens thickness against distance: as distance from the eye increases the lens gets thinner (inverse relationship), up to about 150 cm, after which the thickness stays constant.
- Combined conditions: distant object in dim light - thin lens and wide pupil; reading in dim light - ciliary muscles contracted, circular iris muscles relaxed, radial iris muscles contracted; near object in bright light - ciliary muscle and circular iris muscle both contracted; looking at the sky at night - wide pupil and thin lens.
- These changes are controlled by the nervous system rather than by hormones because nervous control is quicker.

WHEN FOCUSING FAILS (question-specific applications)
- A lens that has lost elasticity (older person): light is refracted as it passes the cornea, pupil, aqueous / vitreous humour and lens; the lens is less able to change shape / accommodate; cannot bulge fully; cannot refract enough / shorten its focal length enough; so the image of a near object is not focused on the retina / fovea. Reading glasses: a convex lens; bends / converges the light rays; before they enter the eye; compensating for the thinness of the eye lens; so the light meets on the retina and the image is clear.
- A cornea that is too flat: light is not bent / refracted enough; so it is not focused on the retina.
- An eyeball that is too long: light rays are bent so that they meet (cross) before the retina, so distant objects are not seen clearly.
- A detached retina: the light receptors (rods / cones) are no longer in contact with the optic nerve; no impulses; reach the brain; no picture is formed / unable to see / blindness (reject blurred or reduced vision); less / no nutrition for the retina.
- Cloudy patches in the lens (cataract): failure to focus (all) the light rays; blurred image; passage of light impaired (reflected / absorbed / deflected); possible change in elasticity / ability to accommodate; faded colour vision.
- Parts with similar jobs: cornea / lens / humours let light through and focus it; retina / fovea / rods / cones detect it.
- Data on an eye condition: state the overall change, say whether it applies to both sexes / all age groups, identify the group that changed most and quote comparative figures.

14.3 MAMMALIAN HORMONES

DEFINITION OF A HORMONE
- A chemical (substance). Reject a named hormone given instead of "chemical"; ignore named examples.
- Produced / released by a gland (endocrine / ductless gland). Reject a specific named gland in the definition.
- Secreted into / carried by the blood.
- Alters the activity of / has an effect on a target organ (one or more specific target organs). Reject a specific named target organ in the definition.
- Destroyed by the liver.
- The organs affected by a hormone are called target organs.
- Three characteristics of all hormones: affect target organs; carried by the blood; produced by glands.

ENDOCRINE GLANDS AND THE HORMONES THEY SECRETE
- Adrenal glands (one on top of each kidney): adrenaline; target e.g. heart, liver.
- Pancreas (in the upper abdomen, below the stomach): insulin and glucagon; target liver. Accept islets of Langerhans; when labelling, the line must touch or enter the gland.
- Pituitary gland (in the head, at the base of the brain; the part of the brain region that produces / secretes hormones): follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH); target ovary. It coordinates the menstrual cycle.
- Testes (accept testicles / gonads): testosterone; responsible for sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
- Ovaries (in the lower abdomen): oestrogen and progesterone; target uterus. Oestrogen repairs / thickens / increases the thickness of the lining of the uterus (also development / release of an egg, secondary sexual characteristics, inhibits FSH). Progesterone maintains the lining of the uterus (also inhibits ovulation / FSH / LH); also from the corpus luteum / placenta.
- LH causes the release of an egg from the ovary (ovulation) and stimulates the ovary to produce progesterone; FSH and LH are the two hormones needed for ovulation.
- Sorting a list of hormones: made in the pituitary gland - luteinising hormone; made in the ovary - progesterone and oestrogen; released to increase blood sugar concentration - adrenaline and glucagon; released to build up the uterus lining after menstruation - oestrogen (ignore progesterone). Pancreas - insulin - glucose to glycogen conversion (in liver / muscles).
- Linking exercises: one mark per correct line; any additional line to or from a box cancels that mark. Adrenal - adrenaline - heart (or: conversion of glycogen to glucose); pancreas - glucagon - liver; pituitary - FSH - ovary; ovary - progesterone - uterus.

HOW A HORMONE REACHES ITS TARGET
- Named gland secretes the named hormone.
- In solution / dissolved.
- In the blood / plasma (bloodstream).
- Passes out into tissue fluid.
- Reaches / acts on the named target organ; capillaries allow maximum uptake / offloading by diffusion.
- After acting, hormones are destroyed / broken down; by the liver; the products are removed (excreted); this prevents a continued effect.

ADRENALINE
- Secreted by the adrenal glands; travels in the blood; to a named target organ (e.g. heart, lungs, liver).
- Released in a named situation - fright / fight / flight / shock / excitement / danger (or described); it prepares the body for action / increased activity (a defence mechanism).
- Increases heart rate - the heart beats faster ("increased heart rate" earns both the target organ and the effect); faster circulation / faster delivery of oxygen or glucose.
- Increases blood glucose concentration: stimulates the liver to convert glycogen to glucose; increases glucose uptake by muscle cells.
- More glucose / oxygen / blood to the muscles; blood diverted to muscles / away from the gut.
- Breathing rate changes (faster, deeper); air passages widen.
- Pupils dilate.
- Dry mouth; digestive activity decreases / peristalsis slows.
- Effect on liver cells: glycogen; is broken down to glucose; which is released into the blood / raises the blood glucose level (ignore references to glucagon / pancreas).
- When frightened: adrenaline is released, the heart beat increases and the pupils dilate; adrenaline (not insulin, carbon dioxide or lactic acid) raises the blood sugar level; a condition producing too much adrenaline raises blood glucose concentration.
- Not effects of adrenaline: lower heart rate, lower breathing rate, faster peristalsis, more saliva, smaller pupils, liver taking up glucose.
- A loud noise followed by a faster heartbeat: sound is the stimulus; detected by the ears; impulses in a (sensory) neurone; to the brain; (motor neurone to) the adrenal gland; adrenaline released; carried in the blood; to the heart muscle; fight-or-flight response.
- A raised adrenaline level explains a fast heartbeat and fast, deep breathing in a person at rest.
- During a race: adrenaline; converts glycogen to glucose; in the liver / muscles; boosting blood glucose; faster heart beat and circulation; more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles.
- Adrenaline occurs naturally in the body (unlike heroin, nicotine, penicillin). It is destroyed by the liver.

HORMONE AND DRUG
- Adrenaline is a hormone and caffeine a drug; a drug is taken into the body and affects chemical reactions / body functions; a hormone is made in the body / by glands.

NERVOUS AND HORMONAL CONTROL COMPARED (comparative statements are needed)
- Both trigger responses; both act on specific effectors / targets.
- Nervous: fast / faster. Hormonal: slow / slower.
- Nervous: short-term / temporary effect. Hormonal: longer-term / more lasting effect.
- Nervous: electrical impulses. Hormonal: chemical.
- Nervous: travel along neurones / nerve cells (reject "nerves" in the older scheme). Hormonal: carried in the blood / plasma.
- Nervous: impulses are all the same; generated by receptors. Hormonal: different chemicals; from glands.
- Nervous: effectors are muscles (or glands); specific; sometimes deliberate. Hormonal: act on glands / organs; more general effect; less controlled.
- Blood glucose concentration is controlled by hormones, not directly by the nervous system (heart rate, pupil diameter and dilation of blood vessels are under nervous control).
- Changes in the eye are under nervous control because it is quicker.

HORMONES IN HOMEOSTASIS
- To describe a hormone keeping conditions constant: deviation from the set point (for insulin the set point must refer to glucose in the blood); named hormone (reject adrenaline / thyroxine); the gland that makes it; its target organ; its homeostatic effect; return to the set point ("keeps constant" accepted; "regulates" / "controls" rejected); homeostasis.

14.4 HOMEOSTASIS

DEFINITION
- Maintenance of / keeping constant (accept regulating / control).
- The internal environment / conditions within the body. Ignore specific examples offered in place of the general statement.
- Homeostasis is the term for: maintaining constant conditions such as temperature in the body; keeping the internal environment within narrow / strict limits (a graph of body temperature fluctuating between narrow "limits of variation"); keeping temperature and glucose concentration constant; regulating temperature and the amount of water in cells; the kidneys removing waste and keeping the composition of the blood constant.
- With reference to sweating (any two): maintenance of a constant; internal environment / temperature; return to the norm / set point / negative feedback; evaporation of sweat removes heat / cools / lowers the temperature.

NEGATIVE FEEDBACK AND SET POINTS
- There is a condition / parameter with a set point (norm), e.g. body temperature.
- A change away from the set point (an increase or a decrease);
- is detected (by a sensor / receptor);
- information is communicated (nerve impulse / hormone);
- to a control centre / coordinator (hypothalamus / brain);
- which brings about a response / corrective mechanism;
- that reverses / corrects the initial change - the condition returns to the set point / norm / original level.
- Short form: a change away from a set point causes a change back towards the set point - it reverses any change. A specific example is accepted in place of a general account.
- The process by which a return to a set point is achieved is called negative feedback.
- Flow diagram: excess of a substance in the body -> sensor detects the concentration -> correction by a gland to reduce the concentration -> normal level of the substance.
- Water in the blood: rising -> more water in urine; falling -> less water in urine.
- Examples of negative feedback: keeping core body temperature near 37 degrees C; release of insulin after a meal; release of glucose from the liver after missing a meal; high blood pressure causing nerve impulses from the brain to dilate blood vessels so that pressure falls.
- Not negative feedback: release of adrenaline when frightened; activated platelets activating more platelets; LH and oestrogen stimulating more of each other; contractions in childbirth releasing a hormone that causes further contractions (these increase the change).

APPLYING NEGATIVE FEEDBACK
- A person drinks an excessive volume of water: water is absorbed into the blood (colon); the rise / excess in the water content of the blood is detected (accept hypothalamus / a correct reference to ADH); excess water is excreted / more urine is produced; by the kidney (nephron); via ureter, bladder and urethra.
- The kidneys keeping the blood at a constant concentration: homeostasis / osmoregulation; salts / ions / urea and water are removed from the blood; with reabsorption in varying quantities; depending on food and water intake and on sweating; so tissue fluid stays at a constant concentration; otherwise water would enter or leave cells by osmosis; cells would swell / burst / shrink; enzyme action / metabolism would be affected.
- A hormone in homeostasis (max 6): deviation from the set point (for insulin the set point must refer to glucose in the blood); named hormone (reject adrenaline / thyroxine); the gland that produces it; its target organ; its effect, which must be homeostatic; return to the set point ("keeps constant" accepted; "regulates" / "controls" rejected); the word homeostasis.
- Temperature control is homeostasis because a constant internal temperature is kept, and negative feedback because a change (increase or decrease) is corrected / reversed back to normal.
- The hypothalamus: homeostasis / maintenance of a constant internal environment; detects changes (increase / decrease) in blood temperature, blood (plasma) concentration, carbon dioxide concentration; triggers the appropriate response; controls pituitary secretions; acts through hormones / the nervous system ("controls / regulates" alone earns one mark).
- Human respiration rate stays relatively constant because of homeostasis - body temperature is kept constant (thermoregulation).

14.5 TEMPERATURE CONTROL

IDENTIFYING SKIN STRUCTURES
- Hair, in its hair follicle.
- Hair erector muscle: attached obliquely to the hair follicle.
- Sweat gland: a coiled tube in the dermis with a duct leading to a pore at the surface (when asked for the gland, "sweat duct" was rejected in one scheme; another accepted a label on gland or duct - the label line must touch the structure).
- Receptors / nerve endings: near the surface or as small end organs in the dermis (a temperature receptor lies under the epidermis); joined to sensory neurones.
- Blood vessels: capillary loops just under the epidermis, supplied by arterioles from deeper vessels. Accept blood vessel / capillary / artery / arteriole as labels. The vessel that constricts when body temperature falls is the arteriole (accept artery) - the vessel supplying the capillary loops.
- Fatty tissue (adipose tissue): the layer of rounded cells at the base of the skin.
- Skin of a hot person is recognised by: sweat on the surface; wider blood vessels / capillaries / more blood in them (reject veins; ignore "constrict / shrink" for the other condition); the condition is "warmer" (a comparative statement is required) / body temperature has increased.

RECEPTORS AND THE HYPOTHALAMUS
- Receptors / sensors in the skin detect changes in temperature (of the surroundings); also touch, pressure and pain.
- Sensory (afferent) neurones (reject "nerve") carry impulses to the CNS / brain / spinal cord.
- The hypothalamus (accept thermoregulatory centre) is the part of the brain that monitors / controls body temperature: it detects the temperature of the blood and triggers the appropriate response. It lies at the base of the brain just above the pituitary gland.
- Damage to the hypothalamus could cause inability to control body temperature.
- Motor neurones carry impulses from the brain to the effectors: sweat glands, hair erector muscles, the (smooth) muscle of arterioles / blood vessels (reject capillary as the structure receiving impulses), skeletal muscles (shivering).
- Full nervous sequence: receptors in the skin detect the temperature of the surroundings / hypothalamus detects blood temperature; impulse; sensory neurone; to the CNS / brain; motor neurone; to a named effector; response (shivering, vasoconstriction or vasodilation, more or less sweat); negative feedback.
- Order on entering a hot room: temperature receptors in the skin are stimulated; the brain coordinates the response; the skin secretes sweat; sweat evaporates. On entering a cold room: cold receptors in the skin are stimulated first. When the external temperature drops: body temperature falls; the brain detects cooler blood; shivering begins; body temperature rises.
- Structures involved in temperature control: blood vessels near the skin surface, hypothalamus, skeletal muscles; sweat glands, temperature receptors and blood vessels in the skin; brain and skin.

WHEN THE BODY IS TOO HOT
- Sweat glands produce / secrete / release more sweat on to the skin surface;
- the sweat evaporates;
- evaporation removes heat / thermal energy from the body - cools it (latent heat of vaporisation).
- Vasodilation: arterioles / blood vessels supplying the skin dilate / widen. Reject "capillaries dilate" in the newest scheme (older schemes accepted dilation with reference to capillaries).
- More blood flows to / through the capillaries near the surface of the skin. Reject "capillaries move closer to the surface".
- The capillaries are close to the surface of the skin (ignore "arterioles close to the surface").
- More heat is lost from the blood; by radiation (accept conduction / a named method). Ignore "keeps warm", "releases energy" unqualified, and heat loss due to sweating when vasodilation is being explained.
- Hair erector muscles relax; hairs lie flat; less air is trapped / loss of insulation.
- Metabolism / respiration is reduced.
- This allows regulation of temperature / prevents overheating.
- When the air is hotter than the body only the evaporation of sweat can lose heat (vasodilation and stopping shivering cannot).
- Wet skin feels cold because the water absorbs heat from the body and evaporates. Red cheeks on a hot day are caused by dilated arterioles in the skin.

WHEN THE BODY IS TOO COLD
- Low temperature is detected; by receptors / sensors in the skin; impulses to the brain / hypothalamus.
- Vasoconstriction: arterioles / arteries / blood vessels constrict / narrow (their muscle contracts). Reject constriction of capillaries or veins.
- Less blood flows to the capillaries / close to the skin surface; the blood carries heat, so less heat is carried to the surface.
- Less heat is lost by radiation.
- Sweat glands become less active / sweating is reduced or stops; less evaporation of sweat (reject "no evaporation"). This is the one response that does not involve muscle contraction.
- Shivering: rapid (sudden / uncontrollable / continuous) contractions of (skeletal) muscles; this releases / produces heat (from respiration).
- Hair erector muscles contract; hairs are raised / stand upright; trap a layer of air; air is a good insulator / insulation; heat loss is reduced.
- Higher metabolic rate / adrenaline release; behaviour such as moving about or putting on clothes (one behavioural reference credited).
- Body temperature rises again to normal.

INSULATION
- Fatty tissue in and under the skin insulates the body / is the best insulator in the skin / helps heat retention.
- Fat also stores energy and supplies energy / heat; the layer becomes thinner when less food is eaten or more exercise is taken, because fat is respired when the body uses more energy than it takes in. Thinning of the fat layer is the slowest way of losing more heat.
- A compact body with small ears and a short tail gives a small surface area (to volume ratio) from which heat can be lost - an advantage at low external temperatures.
- Sweat glands that hardly function in a furry animal of a cold habitat: very little sweat is lost; sweating is not needed / would be harmful; fur would prevent evaporation; less heat is lost.

HOMEOSTASIS AND NEGATIVE FEEDBACK IN TEMPERATURE CONTROL
- A constant internal (core) temperature is maintained - homeostasis.
- A change (rise or fall) is detected and corrected / reversed back to normal - negative feedback.
- Flow diagram: above-normal temperature -> coordinator (brain) -> corrective mechanisms (sweating; more blood flowing close to the skin surface) -> normal temperature; below-normal temperature -> coordinator -> corrective mechanisms (shivering; less blood near the surface) -> normal temperature.
- Growth of longer hair is not a homeostatic mechanism; a fall in surrounding temperature causing a fall in respiration rate is not negative feedback.
- Maintaining body temperature uses energy from respiration; an animal with a constant body temperature uses more oxygen as the external temperature falls (below about 28 degrees C); a small mammal diving in very cold water needs more oxygen because mammals regulate their body temperature, respiration releases the heat, and more is needed for muscle contraction.
- If body temperature does fall, respiratory enzymes work more slowly.

SWEAT AND WATER LOSS
- Sweat contains water, salts / ions and a little urea (urea is made in the liver).
- Percentage of daily water intake lost as sweat = volume of sweat / total intake x 100 (200 / 2500 = 8 %).
- The proportion lost as sweat varies: homeostasis; to keep body temperature constant; sweat evaporates to remove heat; more activity or a higher temperature means more sweat; if the volume lost in faeces / breath / urine changes, the percentage lost in sweat changes.
- Other routes of water loss: urination; breathing out; faeces; bleeding / crying / vomiting.
- Sweating rate is higher when walking than sitting (more energy / heat released); when carrying a load (more respiration / work by muscles); in the sun than in the shade (higher temperature / faster evaporation); lower when clothed than unclothed (greater humidity next to the skin / less skin exposed / clothes deflect or absorb heat).
- On a hot day less urine is made, and a drink that increases urine output should be avoided (water is already being lost in sweat; danger of dehydration).
- Causes of a rise in body temperature above normal: exercise; high surrounding temperature; high humidity; hot food / drink; increased metabolism / respiration; fever / illness; ovulation; wearing more clothes. It falls again through sweating and its evaporation, vasodilation, more blood to the skin, heat loss by radiation and reduced metabolism.
- Sweating and transpiration - similarities: loss of water; by evaporation / as vapour; to the surroundings; cooling effect; through pores; in the epidermis; both affected by temperature / humidity / wind. Differences: sweating occurs in animals, loses urea / salts, is under nervous control as part of homeostasis, uses sweat glands / ducts, the sweat coming from the blood.

APPLICATIONS
- An antiperspirant should be used only on small areas: sweating is inhibited; the ability to regulate temperature would be lost; the body would overheat; affecting metabolism / enzymes. An antibacterial soap is better for controlling odour: it kills the bacteria (reject germs); that decompose / break down; the nitrogenous compounds / wastes in sweat; it can be used all over the body / does not block sweat ducts; temperature regulation is not affected.
- Alcohol relaxes the muscle of artery walls: vasodilation; more blood to the capillaries; the blood carries heat; more heat is lost; body temperature falls further in the cold (hypothermia).
- A condition in which hands and feet react as if to extreme cold shows vasoconstriction - blood vessels at the skin surface narrow, reducing blood flow.
- Graph reading: a rise followed by a fall in body temperature fits vigorous exercise followed by increased sweating; for a daily temperature record read the values at the stated times, take the mean, and find the range as highest minus lowest.
- Farm animals kept with restricted movement at a surrounding temperature close to their body temperature: less energy is required; to raise body temperature / keep the body warm / for thermoregulation; less movement / muscle activity; less respiration (ignore "energy production"); more energy is available to increase biomass / grow.

14.6 BLOOD GLUCOSE CONTROL

WHY BLOOD GLUCOSE IS REGULATED / THE SET POINT
- Blood glucose concentration is kept close to a set point - the level about which it oscillates and to which it returns (on a regular oscillating graph, midway between peaks and troughs; on a graph of meals, the baseline level).
- Glucose is always present in the blood - a graph line does not start at the origin / starts part-way up the y-axis.
- Blood glucose is controlled by hormones, not directly by the nervous system.
- After a meal blood glucose rises and then falls because: glucose is converted to glycogen / insulin is released; and glucose is used in respiration / for energy.
- A diet of slowly digested starch is healthier (max 2): less variation in blood glucose concentration; the pancreas does not have to produce so much insulin; diabetes is less likely to develop.

LIVER AND PANCREAS
- Pancreas (accept islets of Langerhans): produces / releases insulin and glucagon - controls blood glucose concentration. It is an endocrine gland; it also makes amylase, protease, lipase and alkali (pancreatic juice).
- Liver: converts glucose to glycogen and glycogen to glucose (also amino acids to glucose - never glucose to amino acids); stores glycogen. The liver does not secrete insulin.
- Glycogen is used as a store of glucose / energy; it is insoluble, so it does not change the water potential of the cells.
- Blood from the ileum goes first to the liver in the hepatic portal vein so that excess glucose can be converted to glycogen for storage and not excreted.

INSULIN - WHEN BLOOD GLUCOSE IS TOO HIGH
- Insulin is secreted (by the pancreas) when blood glucose is too high - e.g. after a (carbohydrate) meal; more insulin is released as blood glucose rises.
- It is carried in the blood (plasma); to the liver / muscles.
- It increases the uptake of glucose by cells (increased permeability of cells to glucose).
- Glucose is converted to glycogen (increased synthesis of glycogen); in liver / muscle cells; for storage.
- Blood glucose concentration is lowered - back to normal.
- Insulin is broken down by the liver; its rate of secretion and its concentration in the blood both vary.
- Too much insulin makes the blood glucose concentration low. After an insulin injection the production of glycogen increases.
- Paragraph completion: glucose is transported in solution by the plasma; the hormone insulin is released into the blood by an endocrine gland called the pancreas; it promotes the conversion of glucose into glycogen.

GLUCAGON (AND ADRENALINE) - WHEN BLOOD GLUCOSE IS TOO LOW
- Blood glucose has decreased / is low / the body needs more sugar (e.g. exercise, missing a meal).
- Glucagon is a hormone that increases blood glucose concentration (accept "controls blood sugar level" / "converts glycogen to glucose"; reject "decreases blood sugar").
- Glycogen (in the liver) is broken down / hydrolysed to glucose - made soluble;
- the glucose passes out of the liver into the blood (hepatic vein); so the hepatic vein can contain more glucose than the blood arriving at the liver.
- The pancreas produces less insulin.
- Adrenaline also raises blood glucose (when frightened / anxious) by stimulating the breakdown of glycogen to glucose in liver cells; the hormones released to increase blood sugar concentration are adrenaline and glucagon.
- Liver glycogen graphs: the glycogen store rises while blood glucose is above average (glucose -> glycogen) and falls while glycogen is being converted to glucose.
- Flow diagram: eating a meal -> blood sugar rises -> pancreas produces more insulin -> liver turns glucose to glycogen -> normal; exercise -> blood sugar goes down -> pancreas produces less insulin -> liver turns glycogen to glucose -> normal.

TYPE 1 DIABETES
- Cause: the pancreas does not produce (sufficient) insulin - damaged pancreas cells.
- So glucose is not converted to glycogen / cells do not take up glucose.
- Signs: increased / high blood glucose concentration.
- Glucose in the urine (the urine gives a positive Benedict's test; accept sweet-smelling breath / urine) - the kidney cannot reabsorb all the glucose.
- Frequent urination.
- Tiredness / severe thirst / unexplained weight loss.
- Blood glucose fluctuates widely / is poorly controlled; a person without diabetes shows the smallest fluctuations, at the lowest level.
- Treatment: insulin (by injection - "injection" alone is rejected, insulin must be named).
- Dietary control: reduced / controlled carbohydrate or sugar intake (ignore fats), because carbohydrate is digested to glucose.
- Glucose is higher than normal in both the blood and the urine; the specific sign is glucose in the urine, since glucose is always present in blood.
- A blood test for glucose concentration detects diabetes.
- Insulin for treatment is made by genetically modified bacteria.
- Graph reading: in a person with diabetes the point at which blood glucose begins to fall shows when an insulin injection was given; an injected substance that lowers blood glucose is insulin; a rise in blood glucose starting at a marked time is explained by eating sugary food; a rapid rise after an external stimulus is due to adrenaline.

15.1 COORDINATION AND RESPONSE IN PLANTS

GRAVITROPISM AND PHOTOTROPISM
- Both are plant growth responses; towards or away from a stimulus.
- Phototropism: the stimulus is light. Gravitropism: the stimulus is gravity.
- A stem / shoot growing towards light is positive phototropism (the correct example of phototropism).
- A shoot (plumule) growing upwards, against gravity, shows negative gravitropism.
- A root (radicle) growing downwards, towards gravity, shows positive gravitropism - two marks: "gravitropism" and "positive".
- A seed germinating in darkness: the shoot grows up (negative gravitropism) and the root grows down (positive gravitropism). A plant laid on its side in the dark: roots show positive gravitropism, shoot negative gravitropism.
- A root growing down into soil is not positive phototropism and not negative gravitropism. A main root that emerges into the air and grows back into the soil is responding negatively to the stimulus of light.

AUXIN
- Auxin is the chemical (plant hormone) made in a plant shoot that coordinates the shoot's responses to light and gravity.
- Auxin is produced in the shoot tip (not in the stem).
- It diffuses / spreads down the stem from the shoot tip.
- It stimulates / promotes cell elongation in shoots (it does not stimulate cell division, germination, ion uptake or root branching, and it does not inhibit elongation).
- It is unequally distributed in response to light and gravity; in full (all-round) light it is equally distributed.

HOW A SHOOT GROWS TOWARDS LIGHT (POSITIVE PHOTOTROPISM)
- The response is named phototropism.
- Light is detected by the shoot (stem) tip.
- Auxin is produced in the shoot tip;
- diffuses down the stem / shoot;
- more auxin moves / diffuses to (accumulates on) the shaded side - the side away from the light / less on the side exposed to light. Reject "auxin is destroyed on the light side"; ignore "uneven distribution" unless qualified.
- Auxin promotes / stimulates cell elongation;
- cells on the shaded side elongate more than those on the light side / more growth on the shaded side;
- the shoot bends / grows towards the light. Ignore "moves towards the light".

HOW A SHOOT GROWS AWAY FROM GRAVITY (NEGATIVE GRAVITROPISM)
- Auxin diffuses down the stem;
- auxin collects on the lower side of the stem;
- cell elongation is stimulated on the lower side of the stem;
- the stem grows away from gravity.

INVESTIGATIONS
- Tip covered with a black paper cap / aluminium foil: no response - the shoot grows straight up (the tip detects the light).
- Tip covered with a transparent cap, or a black collar round the stem below the tip: the shoot still bends towards the light.
- Permeable layer between tip and stem: the shoot still responds. Impermeable layer between tip and stem: no response (auxin cannot pass down).
- A thin glass plate inserted across the shaded side just below the tip: the shoot will not grow towards the light (auxin cannot pass down the shaded side). A plate separating the two sides lower down, or a tip removed and then replaced, does not stop the response.
- Tip cut off and replaced on one side of the shoot (in the dark): auxin passes down that side only, that side elongates more, and the shoot curves away from the side carrying the tip.
- Seedlings in full light have auxin equally distributed and grow straight; seedlings lit from one side have auxin unequally distributed and bend towards the light.
- Shoot tips placed on jelly blocks with light from one side: more auxin collects in the block under the shaded side (e.g. 14 units) than under the lit side (6 units); with the tip itself divided by glass the total collected (20 units) is the same. Conclusions: auxin moves downwards out of the shoot tip; its distribution in the tip is affected by light; auxin moves from areas of more light to areas of less light; auxin is not destroyed by light.

PLANT AND ANIMAL RESPONSES TO LIGHT COMPARED
- Human eye: light detected by the retina / light receptors; involves neurones / the nervous system / electrical impulses; the response is muscular contraction in the iris; making the pupil constrict / smaller.
- Plant: light detected by the stem tip; involves hormones / auxin; more auxin moves to the side away from the light; causing more growth / elongation on the shaded side; positive phototropism - the stem grows / bends towards the light.

17.1 VARIATION

CONTINUOUS VARIATION
- A range of phenotypes between two extremes - many forms / intermediates; any value from a minimum to a maximum; gradual change, small differences from one to the next; the extremes may differ considerably.
- Caused by both genes and the environment (the interaction of the two); an example of an environmental factor may be credited (e.g. amount of food, sunlight).
- Examples: body length / height, body mass / weight, foot length, length of seedlings, mass of fruits, human skin colour (reject eye colour as a continuous example).
- Shown as a bell-shaped (normal distribution) curve or a histogram with most individuals in the middle classes; a labelled graph is accepted as part of an answer.

DISCONTINUOUS VARIATION
- A limited number of phenotypes - few, distinct forms / separate categories; no intermediates.
- Caused by genes only (the result of inheritance); unaffected by the environment / environmental effect small; inherited / passed on to the next generation.
- Examples: ABO blood groups, seed shape and seed colour in peas, tall and dwarf peas, sex, eye colour, red hair, albinism, sickle cell anaemia, free or attached ear lobes, horns or no horns in cattle, two colour forms of an animal, the number of flagella on a bacterium, ability / inability to show a particular inherited reaction.
- Shown as a bar chart with separate bars; a labelled bar chart is accepted.
- A blood-group bar chart shows discontinuous variation because there are no intermediate values / distinct, separate categories / a limited number of phenotypes (not because the percentages differ).
- In an answer giving one example of each type, a correct example paired with an incorrect one loses the mark.

- Explaining each type with an example: discontinuous - few phenotypes / categories or no intermediates; inherited / passed to the next generation; genes / genotype / alleles; correct example (blood group, eye colour, sex, ear lobes, tongue rolling); bar chart. Continuous - many phenotypes / intermediates / a range; genes + environment; a named environmental factor (diet, exposure to sunlight); correct example (skin colour, hair colour, height, weight); normal distribution.

BOTH TYPES TOGETHER
- A chart with two separate groups of bars, each group showing a spread (e.g. heights of tall and dwarf pea plants), shows both continuous and discontinuous variation.
- Four graph shapes: one bell curve (continuous); separate bars (discontinuous); two separate humps (both); a straight line through the origin is not variation data.
- A characteristic first thought to be either / or, later found to show a range of strengths because of environmental factors, is continuous.

GENETIC AND ENVIRONMENTAL CAUSES
- Identical twins are alike in characters caused by genes only - any two genetic similarities, e.g. sex, eye colour, blood group, shape of ears / nose (reject "look alike", same age, same chromosomes); because both inherited identical / the same genes; they arise from the same zygote (one sperm and one egg).
- They may differ in environmentally affected characters, e.g. size, weight, hair colour (reject "different traits" unqualified and intelligence); because genes are only partly responsible / the environment has an effect; with a named environmental factor (amount of sunlight, lack of food).
- A plant carrying the allele for chlorophyll production still forms no green leaves in the dark - the environment (light) is needed for the character to appear.
- A condition caused by genes and environment together (e.g. short sight becoming commoner): a change in activity such as less time outdoors / more time looking at near objects, screens or phones; the eyes get too little practice at focusing on distant objects.
- Plants propagated to be genetically identical (clones) show little variation / mutation; so all / very large numbers lack resistance to a disease (all are genetically susceptible); and, grown close together / connected to one another, a higher percentage is killed than among wild plants - the disease is hard to control.
- Limited genetic variation follows from isolation and self-pollination: a crop grown in isolation from other varieties of the species (reject "other species") can pollinate only with plants in the same building / cannot cross-pollinate with plants outside, so its seeds show only small variations; a plant that depends too much on self-pollination lacks (genetic) variation.

- The two main causes of variation: genes / mutation (or a named mutagen); and the environment / habitat (or a named environmental factor).
- Plants of a species growing at very high altitude are much smaller - factors (any two): temperature; oxygen; carbon dioxide; water; soil fertility / lack of nutrients; wind; different genetic make-up / mutation. Reject any of the first five described as "high"; ignore light.
- A species that is cross-pollinated (has two parents; mixing of genes / alleles) shows more variation in its phenotype than one that is not.

- How variation arises in a natural population (max 4): mutation / change in a gene or chromosome; which is spontaneous / random / sudden; mutagens (radiation / chemicals); meiosis; random mating / cross-fertilisation / recombination; the environment; one environmental factor identified.

INVESTIGATING VARIATION - DATA HANDLING
- Number of people with a blood group = percentage read from the chart / 100 x population (e.g. 10 % of 63 million = 6.3 million; the correct answer alone earns both marks).
- A missing percentage = 100 - the sum of the other groups.
- Measurements such as foot length are grouped into classes and the number in each class counted; a pattern with most individuals in the middle classes indicates continuous variation caused by genes and the environment.

17.2 DNA

STRUCTURE OF DNA
- The molecule is DNA / deoxyribonucleic acid.
- Two strands / double stranded (accept sugar-phosphate backbone);
- coiled to form a double helix (the term for its shape).
- Each strand is a chain of nucleotides - the nucleotide is the unit of DNA; a DNA molecule consists of many nucleotides.
- Each nucleotide contains a base; the letters A, T, G and C represent (nitrogenous / organic) bases.
- The strands are held together by bonds between pairs of bases.
- Complementary base pairing: A with T, and C with G - always the same way.
- DNA contains the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
- Incorrect statements: "four strands"; "a nucleotide contains a base, a sugar and an amino acid"; "bonds between sugar molecules hold the strands together"; pairs such as A-C, A-G, C-T, T-G.

USING THE BASE-PAIRING RULE
- Complementary strand: swap each base for its partner (e.g. CTCAGCA pairs with GAGTCGT).
- Number of A = number of T; number of C = number of G. Example: 400 bases with 80 A -> 80 T -> 240 remaining -> 120 C = 30 %. If 46 nucleotides contain A, 46 contain T.

DNA, GENES AND CHROMOSOMES
- A chromosome includes / carries a long molecule of DNA; the DNA is divided into sections called genes; a gene is a section of a molecule of DNA; genes are found in chromosomes.
- Size order, smallest to largest: gene, molecule of DNA, chromosome.
- The DNA strand is part of a chromosome (accept chromatid).
- Wrong statements: chromosomes are DNA molecules called genes; genes are divided into sections called DNA molecules; genes include DNA molecules called chromosomes.
- Where DNA is found and why it matters (max 3): in the nucleus (accept plasmids / chloroplasts / mitochondria); in chromosomes / genes / it contains genetic information (reject "contains genes" here); it can be copied / inherited / is responsible for characteristics; it controls the production of a protein.
- DNA is the chemical in the nucleus that controls the production of protein.
- Animal cell v bacterial cell: in the nucleus v in the cytoplasm; linear / straight strands v circular. In a bacterium the strand (loop) is the structure made mostly of DNA; plasmids also contain DNA.

- Gene: a section / piece of DNA (of a chromosome); controls the production of one protein / polypeptide (one characteristic); may be copied; the unit of inheritance - passed on to the next generation.
- The part of a cell that contains the genes: nucleus / chromosomes / DNA.
- DNA in inheritance: it forms the chromosomes / genes / alleles; is copied / replicated; and passed to the next generation from the parents; in the gametes (egg and sperm).

- Chromosomes in inheritance (max 5): made of DNA; carry genes; responsible for characteristics (accept traits / enzymes, or a named character such as eye colour); passed from one generation to the next; during reproduction / at fertilisation; correct reference to chromosomes during cell (nuclear) division; sexual reproduction gives variation / asexual gives none.
- Naming: structures in the nucleus responsible for inheritance - chromosomes / genes; the chemical in them that makes inheritance possible - DNA; a marked part of the DNA strand - gene / allele.

- Each chromosome contains one molecule of DNA; a chromosome is larger than a gene; a gene does not contain smaller regions called alleles; an allele does not "carry molecules of DNA"; a chromosome is not part of a DNA molecule and does not control just one feature.
- A gene is a unit of inheritance that controls the production of a protein; the primary function of DNA is to control the production of protein.
- Size order: gene, chromosome, nucleus, cell, tissue.

HOW DNA CONTROLS CELL FUNCTION
- DNA contains genes;
- DNA (a gene) codes for proteins / polypeptides (accept "contains instructions for proteins" / "controls protein production");
- by the order / sequence of its bases (nucleotides);
- the sequence of bases determines the sequence (order) of amino acids;
- the sequence of amino acids determines the shape / structure / function of the protein;
- example of a protein: enzyme, haemoglobin, antibody, a hormone such as insulin (a single protein controlled by a single gene); these proteins may act as enzymes.
- Reject: "DNA codes for the production of amino acids"; "amino acids code for proteins".
- Each person has a unique set of genes; genes make proteins; so proteins differ between people; relatives share genes and so have similar proteins / tissues.
- In a cell making a protein: the DNA / genetic material contains the gene for the protein / codes for its amino acid sequence (mRNA); the ribosome joins amino acids together to build the protein.
- Enzymes from different species differ in structure because their DNA base sequences, and so their amino acid sequences and shapes, differ; so some denature at different temperatures from others.

CHANGES TO DNA
- Gene mutation: a change / alteration; in the base / nucleotide sequence of DNA / gene structure.
- The changed base sequence changes the sequence of amino acids;
- so the protein has a different shape / structure (e.g. a different hair protein; an enzyme whose active site no longer fits its substrate).
- Genetic modification is a change in genetic material: a new / changed sequence of bases gives a new sequence of amino acids and a new shape of enzyme / active site, complementary to a new substrate.
- The piece of human DNA put into bacteria to make insulin is a gene / sequence of bases; for insulin (hormone) production.
- An animal that cannot digest a particular substance has no enzyme for it; because it has no gene / allele coding for that enzyme.
- Passage completion: the nucleus contains thread-like structures called chromosomes; made up of many units called genes; that instruct the cell to produce a particular protein.
- A gene is a section of DNA; an allele is a version (alternative form) of the gene.
- An animal lacking an enzyme that its diet makes unnecessary does not have a functional gene for that enzyme (mutation / evolution); no energy is wasted producing an unnecessary enzyme.

17.3 INHERITANCE

GENETIC INFORMATION - CHROMOSOMES, GENES AND ALLELES
- Chromosomes: long thread-like structures in the nucleus that carry the genetic information; made of DNA; carry genes.
- Genes are found in the nucleus / on the chromosomes; the chemical that makes inheritance possible is DNA.
- Gene: a section of DNA / of a chromosome; controls the production of one protein (polypeptide) / one characteristic (accept feature / phenotype); can be copied; is the unit of inheritance / passed on to the next generation (accept "contains hereditary information").
- Allele: an alternative form of a (the same) gene - accept version / variation / type / sort of a gene; reject "alternative form of genes". Alleles of a gene lie at the same position (same locus) on the two chromosomes of a (homologous) pair; they may be dominant, recessive or codominant / have different effects; an allele is copied during cell division and is part of a DNA molecule.
- Gene v allele: a gene is a section of DNA; an allele is a version of that gene.
- On a pair of chromosomes, the same position on each marks the site of a gene, which exists as two or more alleles that might be different; in a heterozygous individual the equivalent position on the other chromosome carries a different allele of the same gene.
- A genotype written Tt shows that there are at least two different alleles of the gene.
- Size: a gene is smaller than a chromosome, which is smaller than the nucleus (gene -> chromosome -> nucleus -> cell -> tissue).
- Body cells hold two copies of each gene; one copy of each (half the total) was inherited from the mother and one from the father.
- Evidence that genes are copied and passed on to the next generation: asexual reproduction produces genetically identical offspring.
- Chromosomes in inheritance (max 5): made of DNA; carry genes; responsible for characteristics (accept traits / enzymes / a named character); passed from one generation to the next; during reproduction / at fertilisation; correct reference to chromosomes during nuclear division; sexual reproduction gives variation, asexual none.
- Role of the nucleus and cell division in inheritance (any six): chromosomes; DNA / genetic material; genes / alleles; code for proteins; proteins determine characteristics; meiosis; reduction division / produces haploid cells / gametes; fertilisation; combination / mix of genetic material / genes / chromosomes from the two parents.
- DNA in inheritance: forms chromosomes / genes / alleles; is copied / replicated; passed to the next generation from the parents; in the gametes (egg, sperm).
- Paragraph completion: a chromosome (accept chromatid) includes a long molecule of DNA divided into genes; alternative forms of a gene are alleles; one may be dominant and the other recessive; a change in gene structure or chromosome number is a mutation; chemicals and radiation increase its rate.
- A gamete carries only one chromosome of each pair, with the alleles that were on that chromosome (drawn as one chromosome, genes in the same shapes and sequence).
- Egg nucleus v zygote nucleus: egg is haploid / 23 chromosomes and has only an X chromosome; zygote is diploid / 46 chromosomes / 23 pairs and has either XX or XY.
- Each parent has an equal share in the genotype of the offspring (credited when the chromosome numbers of gametes and body cells are compared).

KEY TERMS
- Dominant allele: a form of a gene that is always expressed / always has an effect when present - expressed even if only one copy is present / in the heterozygote / over another allele.
- Recessive allele: only affects the phenotype if two are present (it does not always affect the phenotype, is not always harmful and is not restricted to one sex).
- Homozygous: having two of the same form of a gene (both alleles the same). Heterozygous: having two different forms of a gene.
- Genotype: the combination of alleles an organism has. Phenotype: the feature that appears.
- Dominance explained (max 3): allele(s); responsible for a characteristic; only one needs to be present / reference to the heterozygote; for the character to be expressed in the phenotype; example such as IAIO = group A, IBIO = group B, IA and IB dominant to IO.
- Carrier: a heterozygous person who has one recessive (affected) allele but shows no symptoms.
- Dominant alleles give the same phenotype in heterozygotes and homozygotes. They can mutate, are not necessarily commoner or rarer, and do not necessarily improve survival.
- An organism with a dominant phenotype may be homozygous or heterozygous; a recessive phenotype is always homozygous (probability 1.00); one recessive allele alone does not give the recessive phenotype.
- A homozygous genotype may be dominant or recessive - "the phenotype of a homozygote is always dominant" is wrong; "dominant-phenotype individuals are always heterozygous" is wrong.
- A heterozygous person cannot show the recessive feature (e.g. no heterozygous blue-eyed person, max 3): the recessive phenotype needs two recessive alleles (bb); such a person is homozygous; heterozygous means the alleles differ (Bb); B is dominant, so Bb shows the dominant feature.
- Pure-breeding parents are homozygous.

GENETIC DIAGRAMS - HOW THE MARKS ARE AWARDED
- Parental genotypes correct (use the letters given; wrong symbols lose the mark).
- Gametes correctly shown - all four, separated / circled; needed once only for a homozygous parent. Gametes must not simply repeat the parental genotypes.
- Offspring genotypes correctly derived (Punnett square correctly filled or correct linking lines).
- Offspring phenotypes correctly linked to the genotypes; the individual asked about clearly identified.
- Ratio or probability stated.
- Labels: correct use of the words parents, gametes, genotypes / phenotypes / offspring (a mark may need three correct labels).
- Each step is credited if it follows correctly from the step before, even after an earlier error; if the gametes are wrong no mark may be available, but gametes wrongly placed in a Punnett square can still gain the cross and probability marks. Any order within a marking point; a heterozygote may be written either way round (Rr or rR).
- Answers accepted for probability / ratio: 50 % / 1/2 / 0.5 / 1 in 2 / 2 in 4 / 1:1; 25 % / 1/4 / 0.25 / 1 in 4 / 1:3.

HETEROZYGOUS x HETEROZYGOUS (3 : 1)
- Parents Tt x Tt; gametes T, t and T, t; offspring TT, Tt, Tt, tt.
- Genotype ratio 1 : 2 : 1 (a quarter TT, half Tt, a quarter tt); homozygous : heterozygous = 1 : 1; each offspring has a 50 % chance of being heterozygous.
- Phenotype ratio 3 dominant : 1 recessive (75 % : 25 %); the recessive offspring (tt) are identified as the ones with the recessive feature.
- Explains how two parents who both show the dominant feature (or two unaffected carriers) can have a child with the recessive feature: both parents must be heterozygous / carriers; both pass the recessive allele to the child; probability 25 %.
- The recessive phenotype is the rarer one (1 in 4) in the offspring of a hybrid cross.
- Heterozygotes mating always go on producing all three genotypes (TT, Tt, tt), because both T and t alleles are handed on in the gametes.

HETEROZYGOUS x HOMOZYGOUS RECESSIVE (1 : 1) - TEST CROSS
- Parents Tt x tt; gametes T, t and t, t; offspring Tt, Tt, tt, tt; 1 dominant : 1 recessive (50 % each).
- Explaining a 1 : 1 result (max 6): one feature is controlled by a dominant allele; the other by a recessive allele; one parent is heterozygous; the other is homozygous recessive; correct reference to gametes; gametes correctly identified for both parents; how the gametes pair to give 1 : 1.
- An organism of unknown genotype crossed with a homozygous recessive: about half the offspring recessive -> the unknown parent is heterozygous.
- About equal numbers of the two types (e.g. 26 : 24) show that one parent was heterozygous and the other had two recessive alleles.

HOMOZYGOUS x HOMOZYGOUS, F1 AND F2
- Homozygous dominant x homozygous recessive: all offspring alike - all heterozygous, all showing the dominant feature (red x white giving all red: offspring Rr only).
- The feature shown by all the F1 is the dominant one.
- F1 x F1 gives an expected F2 ratio of 3 : 1.
- Two-step cross: BB x Bb gives BB and Bb (all dominant); a Bb offspring x bb gives Bb and bb in equal numbers.
- If all of many offspring of a dominant-phenotype parent mated with recessive partners show the dominant feature, that parent is (most likely) homozygous dominant.
- A dominant-phenotype parent that produces any recessive offspring must be heterozygous.
- Dominant-phenotype individual x recessive individual has two possible outcomes: RR x rr -> all Rr, all dominant; Rr x rr -> Rr and rr, 1 : 1.
- To obtain the highest proportion of recessive offspring from the crosses on offer, choose Bb x bb (50 %) rather than Bb x Bb (25 %).

PROBABILITY
- Each fertilisation is independent: the chance for the next child is unchanged by earlier children.
- Combine with sex by multiplying by 1/2: e.g. affected child of two carriers 1/4, affected boy 1/8 (0.125, 12.5 %); child of a given blood group 1/2, boy with that group 1/4.
- Dominant condition: heterozygous affected parent x unaffected parent -> 0.5 chance of passing on the dominant allele; an affected girl 0.25.
- A person who does not show a dominant condition cannot pass it on (0.00).

FAMILY TREES
- Two unaffected parents with an affected child: the condition is recessive and both parents are heterozygous.
- Every unaffected child of an affected (homozygous recessive) parent must be heterozygous; an unaffected person with an affected child must be heterozygous.
- An unaffected brother or sister of an affected person (both parents carriers) has a 2 in 3 chance of being heterozygous.
- Two people who both show the recessive feature can only have children with that feature.
- Dominant condition: an affected person who has an unaffected parent or child is heterozygous; unaffected people are homozygous recessive.
- In a table of genotypes and phenotypes, an individual with one dominant allele must be given the dominant phenotype.
- Why marriage between closely related people is discouraged when a harmful (mutated) allele is recessive (max 3; reverse argument accepted): the parents have a normal phenotype / appear normal; but may both be heterozygotes / carriers of the recessive allele; there is a greater chance (correctly qualified) of children inheriting two recessive alleles / being homozygous; and so suffering from the condition.

EXPECTED AND OBSERVED RATIOS
- Observed ratios differ from expected ones because fertilisation is random (random fusion of gametes) and the number of offspring is small (small sample size).
- Genetic ratios are unreliable for small numbers - a few children all of one type do not prove a parent is homozygous.
- Example: expected 3 : 1, obtained 12 : 6 = 2 : 1.

MODELLING INHERITANCE WITH SPINNERS OR CUBES
- Two discs / cubes each marked half A and half a model a cross between two heterozygotes: expected results 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa (e.g. 20 : 40 : 20 in 80 trials).
- The two students / containers represent the parents (genotypes, gonads); spinning or shaking represents meiosis / gamete formation / fertilisation; the letters represent alleles / genes (accept chromosomes / gametes; reject genotype).
- Shaking the containers prevents choice / bias, so the results are random.
- A missing count = total trials minus the other counts.
- Results differ from expected because the expected results are theoretical / statistical and each spin is random / due to chance; the disc may be badly made (axis not through the centre); counting errors. Ignore wind / force of spin.
- To model heterozygous x homozygous recessive: one cube with three T and three t faces, the other with six t faces.
- To model blood groups: use the alleles A, B and O (with or without I); one disc with A and B and one with A and O, in equal numbers; representing father and mother; spin many times; record / count the results.

CODOMINANCE AND ABO BLOOD GROUPS
- Codominance (max 3): two alleles; neither is recessive / both (equally) dominant; both have an effect / are expressed in the phenotype (phenotype intermediate); in the heterozygote; example IAIB = group AB.
- The inheritance that gives group AB is codominance (reject "incomplete dominance"; one older scheme accepted it).
- Three alleles IA, IB and IO; only two are present in one person. IA and IB are codominant; IO is recessive to both.
- Genotypes: group A = IAIA or IAIO; group B = IBIB or IBIO; group AB = IAIB; group O = IOIO (four phenotypes, six genotypes).
- IBIB (or IAIA) is homozygous dominant; IOIO is homozygous recessive; IAIB shows codominance.
- A group A person has at least one dominant allele but may be homozygous or heterozygous; all group O people are homozygous.
- Omitting the "I" prefix is penalised once only; some schemes accept the letters A, B and O alone.
- IAIO x IBIO -> IAIB, IAIO, IBIO, IOIO: groups AB, A, B and O, each 25 % - the way parents of groups A and B can have a group O child (the IOIO box is identified as that child).
- IAIB x IOIO -> IAIO and IBIO: groups A and B, 1 : 1 - no child has the same group as either parent.
- IAIA x IBIB -> all IAIB.
- IAIA x IBIO -> IAIB and IAIO: groups AB and A only.
- IAIO x IOIO -> IAIO and IOIO: groups A and O, 1 : 1.
- IBIO x IBIO -> IBIB, IBIO, IBIO, IOIO: groups B and O.
- IAIB x IAIB -> IAIA, IAIB, IAIB, IBIB: 1 A : 2 AB : 1 B.
- IAIO x IAIO -> a group O child with probability 0.25.
- Group A x group AB parents: children may be A, B or AB.
- A group AB person cannot have a group O child: the child must be IOIO, receiving IO from each parent; the AB parent can only pass on IA or IB; IA and IB are dominant to IO. So a group O mother with a group O child cannot have an AB partner.
- A child needing an allele that neither parent has cannot be their genetic child (O x B parents cannot have an AB child).
- A group O parent with children of groups A and B has an IAIB partner; a family containing A, B, AB and O children has IAIO and IBIO parents; brothers of groups A and B show that at least one parent is heterozygous.
- Mother group O, fetus group B: the fetus is IBIO.
- Blood group percentages differ between countries (max 2): the groups are controlled by alleles; each person has two; allele frequencies differ between populations; separate groups breed among themselves (geographical isolation, culture).
- A gene with two codominant alleles and one recessive allele gives six genotypes and four phenotypes.
- Other codominance results: red x white -> all intermediate (pink / roan); intermediate x intermediate -> 1 : 2 : 1 (25 % red); intermediate x red -> 1 red : 1 intermediate; two parents each with both codominant alleles can have children of all three types.
- Unexpected intermediate offspring (max 2): mutation; a named mutagen; possible codominance; heterozygous plants being intermediate; availability of certain ions.

LETHAL COMBINATIONS
- Heterozygous x heterozygous gives 1 : 2 : 1; if one homozygous combination dies before birth, the 1 in 4 of that genotype is missing and the survivors show 2 : 1; the probability that a survivor is heterozygous is 2/3 (0.67).

SEX DETERMINATION
- Females are XX; males are XY. A karyotype with two similar (X) sex chromosomes and no Y is female; one with a Y chromosome is male (reject "Y gene").
- All egg cells carry an X chromosome; each sperm carries either an X or a Y - only one sex chromosome per gamete.
- The father determines the sex of a child: sex depends on which sperm (X or Y) fertilises the egg - the chromosome content of the sperm.
- Egg X + sperm X -> XX girl; egg X + sperm Y -> XY boy; boys receive the Y from the father and the X from the mother.
- Equal numbers of each sex (max 3): eggs carry X; sperms carry either X or Y (the mark is lost if "alleles" or "genes" is written); in equal numbers; so equal chances of XX and XY (1 : 1). A Punnett square alone does not score without explanation.
- Sex is determined at fertilisation by the sex chromosomes (not by temperature).
- Identical twins from one XY zygote are both male: the zygote is XY; it divides by mitosis; into genetically identical cells.
- The Y chromosome is found only in males, so cells containing a Y found in a female must have come from a male donor.
- A gene carried only on the X chromosome: males (XY) have only one allele, females (XX) have two; so males are more likely to lack the dominant allele and show the condition.

MUTATION
- Mutation: a change / alteration; in genetic material - DNA / a gene / a chromosome / the base (nucleotide) sequence.
- Gene mutation: a change in the structure of a gene (base sequence); it can code for a new / different / incorrect protein. Chromosome mutation: a change in chromosome number.
- Mutations are random / spontaneous / sudden.
- Factors that increase the rate of mutation (mutagens): ionising radiation - or a named type (alpha, gamma, X-rays, ultraviolet); chemicals - or a named mutagenic chemical (mustard gas, benzene, tar; accept carcinogens / smoking). Also credited: faulty cell division; viruses. Reject heat, infra-red and disease; ignore inheritance.
- Ionising radiation and inheritance (any four): it increases the chance of; mutation; change in chromosome number; change in gene structure / sequence; coding for a new or incorrect protein; may give new beneficial variation; may give a genetic disease (example).
- Describing mutation with examples: for two examples alone, or examples linked to the wrong type of mutation, only one mark.

GENE MUTATION - SICKLE CELL ANAEMIA
- Caused by a gene mutation - a change in the structure of a gene / DNA / allele; affecting haemoglobin (formation); it is genetic / inherited; shown by people with two recessive alleles (homozygous recessive).
- The red blood cells are abnormal, so the blood cannot carry the normal amount of oxygen; sickle-shaped cells have reduced surface area / volume, less haemoglobin, are less elastic and harder to move through blood vessels, may stick together and block a vessel, reducing blood flow to a tissue.

CHROMOSOME MUTATION - DOWN'S SYNDROME
- Caused by a chromosome mutation: one extra chromosome / three instead of two (trisomy) / 47 chromosomes instead of 46; the extra one is chromosome 21.
- It arises during the production of a gamete (meiosis): a gamete with one extra chromosome (24 instead of 23); the extra chromosome is passed on at fertilisation, giving a zygote with 47 chromosomes. (No mark for only stating that the child has 47 chromosomes.)
- Parents without the syndrome can therefore have an affected child.
- A karyotype with three copies of chromosome 21 and X + Y is a male with Down's syndrome: conclusions and reasons - male, because of X + Y / non-matching sex chromosomes; Down's syndrome, because of 47 not 46 chromosomes / an extra chromosome 21.
- Three sets of chromosomes in each cell (triploid, 3n) instead of two: a disadvantage is interference with sexual reproduction / infertility - meiosis (reduction division) is not possible, so gamete formation / seed production is impaired. It is commoner in plants than in animals because many plants can reproduce asexually / propagate vegetatively - mitosis is unaffected / meiosis is not involved (also credited: triploids are more hardy / survive better).

SOURCES OF GENETIC VARIATION
- Mutation (of a gene or a chromosome); meiosis; random mating / cross-fertilisation; random fertilisation / recombination of alleles.
- Genetic changes that may occur in a population (max 2): gene mutation; chromosome mutation; codominance.
- A feature unique to each individual: alleles; mutations; meiosis; different combinations of alleles; some homozygous and some heterozygous; codominance. A feature unaffected by another feature is controlled by a different gene.
- Cross-pollinated species (two parents; mixing of alleles) show more variation in phenotype.
- Asexual reproduction / binary fission (mitosis) gives identical offspring - a clone, no variation (not "similar"); no meiosis, fertilisation or gametes, only one parent. Reject "no mutation".
- Meiosis gives genetically different cells / gametes (all gametes are genetically different), which leads to genetic variation in the offspring / population; mitosis gives genetically identical cells / clones - no genetic variation in the offspring. In a comparison of the two divisions the point must be linked to the right division.
- Sperm nuclei differ from one another (and from the nuclei of body cells) because meiosis gives different allele combinations on the chromosomes.
- Plants propagated without seeds / without sexual reproduction are all genetically identical, because it is sexual reproduction / gametes that produce genetic variation (ORA).
- A seedling grown from a seed is not genetically identical to its parent plant: sexual reproduction (shown by the remains of a flower); self- or cross-pollination; with some genes / chromosomes from another flower / plant.
- A seed and the fruit wall round it are genetically different: the seed results from fertilisation / sexual reproduction; fusion of gametes (pollen nucleus + egg); gametes often from different plants / flowers; gametes made by meiosis / reduction division; giving a new combination of (different) genes / alleles (max 3 for the seed); the fruit wall results from mitosis; so is genetically identical to the parent / a clone.
- Sexual reproduction (fusion of gametes - including eggs and sperm released into water) gives genetically dissimilar offspring: the offspring show genetic variation (credited as an advantage of sexual reproduction).
- Genetically different from one another: brothers and sisters in one family; gametes from the same parent; seeds produced by the same tree (two seeds, or two fruits with their seeds, can have different genotypes - each seed comes from a separate fertilisation). Genetically identical: two leaves, two petals or two root cuttings of one plant; cuttings taken from the same plant. Inside a fruit the cotyledon (embryo) has a genotype different from the fruit wall (pericarp) and the flower stalk, which are tissues of the parent plant.

APPLYING GENETICS
- Marriage between close relatives is discouraged (max 3): the parents appear normal; but may both be heterozygous carriers of the same recessive allele; there is a greater chance; of children inheriting two recessive alleles / being homozygous; and having the condition.
- Offspring of two different species are usually infertile (max 2): their chromosomes / genes (reject alleles); do not match / pair / differ in number; so meiosis is not possible / gametes cannot be formed.
- Why a person's blood group needs to be known: transfusion / transplant; to prevent clotting / rejection; to check paternity.

17.4 SELECTION

NATURAL SELECTION - THE SEQUENCE OF MARKING POINTS
- Variation exists within a population / there are different varieties.
- The variation arises by mutation / genetic change (a change in a gene).
- There is competition (e.g. for food) / a change in the environment / predation.
- Some variations are advantageous - those individuals are better adapted / better suited to the environment (state the advantage in the context: better camouflaged, less easily seen, collect more food, more armour, resistant).
- They are more likely to survive / less likely to be eaten (differential survival). The converse is accepted for the less well adapted form.
- They are more likely to reproduce / breed / have offspring.
- They pass on the allele (gene) for the advantageous feature to the next generation / it is inherited. Ignore "pass on the gene" where the question is about a named allele.
- This is repeated over many generations / a long period of time (cumulative effect).
- The advantageous form becomes more common / the other form less common - a change in the phenotype of the population.
- The process is natural selection; it results in evolution.
- Natural selection is reproduction between those members of a species that are best fitted to their environment (the term is not "survival of the fittest").
- Both competition and variation are essential for natural selection.
- Correct statements: many organisms in a population die before they breed; organisms with more advantageous inherited features are more likely to survive and produce offspring; survivors may transfer an advantageous feature to their offspring; an organism with an advantageous inherited feature does not always pass it on.
- Natural selection can lead to better adapted species surviving, to the evolution of new species and to the extinction of a species. It does not cause mutations.
- Natural selection results in evolution - the two are not the same and evolution does not cause natural selection.

GENES, VARIATION AND EVOLUTION
- Part played by genes (max 6): genes are inherited / passed on in reproduction; mutation / change in a gene; produces variation - differences in appearance, behaviour or phenotype; some are advantageous / give better adaptation; competition; change in the environment; survival; natural selection; over a long period of time; change in phenotype.
- Changes in gene structure leading to a new species: change in appearance / phenotype; variation; change in environment; advantage; survival; natural selection; reproduction; inheritance; over a period of time; finally breeding only with the similar type / a separate species.
- Why variation matters for the long-term survival of a species (max 5): some variations are advantageous; competition; in the organism's habitat / environment; those organisms survive; breed; pass on the beneficial allele; over many generations; continuous adaptation to a changing environment; evolution / natural selection; variation caused only by the environment is less important.
- A new species forming after migration to a different environment is evolution (accept speciation): different environment; mutations; advantageous variations; survive; reproduce / passed on; over many generations; change in phenotype. The two species can no longer interbreed because of differences in genes / DNA or chromosomes, problems with fertilisation, no attraction / incompatibility, or geographical separation (max 2).
- Genetic changes help a population survive an environmental change by making individuals better adapted to the new condition, e.g. protected from a predator / less eaten.
- A feature that helps an animal see in cold water adapts it to survive (max 4): it can see / hunt prey; so obtains more food / energy; out-competes other animals; sees predators; avoids being eaten; sees mates; reproduces.
- Loss of something no longer needed (an enzyme whose substrate is absent from the diet; genes for detecting bitter tastes in plant-eaters): the diet makes it unnecessary; no energy is wasted producing it; mutation / natural selection / evolution; no functional gene remains.
- Asexually reproducing organisms stay unchanged for long periods: offspring identical / no variation; so no (or limited) natural selection and no (or limited) evolution. A disadvantage of asexual reproduction to a species: no variation / no evolution / all susceptible to the same disease / no resistance to environmental change.
- Cross-pollination (two parents) gives variation in the offspring, allowing adaptation / natural selection / evolution. Reject references to new species.
- Unrelated animals look alike when they have evolved in similar environmental conditions.
- In a changing climate some mutations may prove to be an advantage in the new conditions.
- Disadvantages of self-fertilisation to a plant (max 3): fewer or no new alleles / genes; limited variation; limited evolution / limited resistance to changes in the environment; inbred weaknesses likely to appear / no hybrid vigour / decreased fertility.
- Sexual reproduction (e.g. by flowers / cross-pollination) v asexual reproduction for the survival of a species: sexual reproduction gives genetically different offspring / variation (meiosis); asexual reproduction gives genetically identical offspring / clones (mitosis); a named condition affecting survival (disease, temperature, water availability); some individuals will survive a changing environmental condition, so the population is more likely to survive environmental change / disease; the species can live in a wider range of environments; increased capacity to respond to changes in the environment.

EXAMPLES AS THEY ARE MARKED
- Camouflage: the form whose markings match the background is less easily seen / not eaten, is found in the greatest numbers, breeds and hands on its alleles; forms that do not match are easily seen and eaten and decline. If the background changes, the form that now matches increases.
- Hunting pressure: where animals with tusks are killed, selection acts against them; tuskless animals are less likely to be killed, reproduce and pass on the allele, so they become commoner.
- Feeding: as low vegetation disappeared, animals with shorter necks died / did not breed; later animals have longer necks (and are fewer).
- Beak shape: individuals with more pointed beaks are better able to compete for food.
- Graph of two colour forms after predators arrive: the camouflaged form rises and the other falls - natural selection.
- Describing data on changing proportions: say which category falls, which rises, and which rises and then stays constant, with the dates.

RESISTANCE TO ANTIBIOTICS (INCLUDING MRSA), DRUGS AND PESTICIDES
- Variation within the bacterial population.
- A mutation (of a gene) produces resistant bacteria - "resistant", not "immune" (accept tolerant).
- The antibiotic kills many (non-resistant) bacteria; resistant ones survive.
- Survivors reproduce; and pass on the alleles (resistance) to the next generation.
- Repeated over many generations; so a higher proportion of the population is resistant / resistant forms become more common.
- Bacteria have a short generation time.
- Causes of the increase: antibiotics prescribed too readily / over-prescribed / too easily available; patients not finishing the course / treatment stopped too early; long exposure to the drug.
- If treatment stops before all the bacteria are killed: resistant bacteria remain; they multiply again; symptoms recur; and the same antibiotic can no longer kill them.
- MRSA is not destroyed by antibiotics such as penicillin because it is resistant; HIV is not, because it is a virus.
- The more antibiotics a country uses, the more people have MRSA: more antibiotics are used to try to kill the pathogen; they do not kill resistant bacteria; and their use increases the number of resistant bacteria.
- Reducing the risk: use antibiotics only when essential / only for harmful bacterial infections - not for viral infections, not to prevent infection, not without prescription, not shared.
- Antibiotic discs on a bacterial plate: the antibiotic diffuses from the disc into the agar; a clear zone shows where bacteria have been killed, so the bacteria are not resistant to that antibiotic; no clear zone means the bacteria are resistant (or the antibiotic is the wrong one / not concentrated enough). An antibiotic giving clear zones on every strain's plate is effective against all the strains; one giving a clear zone on only one plate is effective only against that strain.
- The strain with the fewest / smallest clear zones has the greatest resistance, is best adapted, survives, reproduces, passes on the beneficial alleles and becomes the largest percentage of the population.
- The same sequence explains: insecticide resistance in insects (also credited: immigration of insects; less insecticide being sprayed); poison resistance in rats (a larger dose is needed to kill - the LD50 rises, slowly at first then faster); drug resistance in the malaria parasite (the older drug is no longer as effective; newer drugs are more effective / cheaper / have fewer side effects).

ARTIFICIAL SELECTION (SELECTIVE BREEDING)
- Selection is by humans (the farmer / breeder).
- Choose / select individuals with the desirable feature (name it: more milk, more fibre, largest and most colourful flowers, better wool).
- Cross / breed them together (both parents with the desired feature) - cross-breeding / cross-pollinating / cross-fertilising / hybridisation (ignore self-; reject crosses between species); repeated over many years / generations.
- Select the offspring with the best of the feature to breed from.
- Repeat for many generations / many years.
- Offspring vary because of mutation and different combinations of genes; the genes are passed to the offspring; back / test crosses can be used to find homozygotes.
- For a named economically important plant or animal: name it; name the selected feature; state why it is economically important / the financial reward; a danger is inbreeding / harm to the organism (e.g. breeds with breathing problems).
- In flowering plants: transfer pollen from the anther (stamen) of one chosen plant to the stigma (carpel) of the other; fertilisation combines the genetic material; grow the seeds; select the plants with the best combination of characteristics. Ignore self-pollination; reject crossing between species.
- Artificial selection differs from natural selection in that humans choose which individuals breed.
- Examples: mating bulls with the cows that give most milk; picking the highest-yielding offspring of a cross for many generations; saving seed from the best crop plants; milk yield rising over centuries. Results such as seedless grapes, friendly dogs and disease-resistant crops come from artificial, not natural, selection.
- Aims: increased yield / growth rate; decreased need for fertiliser / pesticide; seed from resistant crops improves yield without insecticide.
- Choosing parents from data: pick the type with the largest size and the type with the highest sugar content.
- A feature the crop does not possess at all (e.g. nitrogen fixation in a cereal) cannot be obtained by selective breeding alone.
- A second way to obtain a new variety is genetic modification: transfer / insert / alter a gene so that the plant shows the feature.
- Artificial insemination supports selective breeding: semen from a male with desirable features can be transported anywhere; the farmer need not keep male animals; it promotes cross-breeding / prevents inbreeding; improves the chance of fertilisation; is quicker / more convenient; and gives an economic benefit.
- Calculation: increase per animal x number of animals (e.g. (9400 - 4500) x 250 = 1 225 000 = 1.225 x 10^6 kg per year); a part mark for the difference or the multiplication; give standard form when asked.
- Commercial plants are often reproduced asexually (as clones of one parent): this is artificial selection / under human control; it reproduces the selected / desirable features (or an example).
