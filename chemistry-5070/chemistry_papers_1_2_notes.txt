## CHAPTER 1: STATES OF MATTER — KINETIC PARTICLE THEORY (1.1–1.2)

**General principles**
- Matter consists of particles in continual motion; increasing temperature increases their average kinetic energy.
- Particle descriptions have THREE distinct features: separation, arrangement and motion. Give all three if the question asks for all three.
- Changes of state alter spacing, arrangement and freedom of motion, not the chemical identity of the particles.

**Solids**
- **Arrangement:** regular, ordered positions in a fixed pattern.
- **Separation:** very close together, usually touching.
- **Motion:** vibrate about fixed positions; they do not travel freely through the substance.
- **Properties:** definite shape and volume; difficult to compress because little empty space separates particles.
- **On heating:** vibration becomes more energetic; at the melting point enough attractions are overcome for particles to leave fixed positions.

**Liquids**
- **Arrangement:** irregular/random rather than a fixed regular lattice.
- **Separation:** close together, with some touching.
- **Motion:** move and slide around one another, allowing flow.
- **Properties:** definite volume but take the shape of the container; difficult to compress.

**Gases**
- **Arrangement:** random, with particles distributed throughout the container.
- **Separation:** far apart, with substantial empty space.
- **Motion:** rapid random movement, approximately straight between collisions with other particles or the container walls.
- **Properties:** neither fixed shape nor fixed volume; expand to fill the vessel and compress readily.
- **Pressure:** results from collisions of gas particles with container walls.

**Changes of state**
- **Melting (solid → liquid):** energy is absorbed; particles vibrate more, leave fixed positions and become able to move past one another while remaining relatively close.
- **Freezing (liquid → solid):** energy is released; particles stop sliding and become fixed in more ordered positions but continue vibrating.
- **Boiling (liquid → gas):** energy is absorbed; particles throughout the liquid overcome attractions and move far apart. It occurs at a fixed boiling point for a pure substance at a specified pressure.
- **Evaporation:** sufficiently energetic particles escape from the *surface* at temperatures below the boiling point; loss of higher-energy particles can cool the remaining liquid.
- **Condensation (gas → liquid):** particles lose energy, slow and come much closer together; attractions keep them in the liquid.
- **Sublimation (solid → gas):** a direct solid–gas change without a liquid stage when the conditions allow it.
- On a heating or cooling curve, **slopes** show changing temperature/average kinetic energy; **plateaus** show a phase change at approximately constant temperature while energy changes attractions and arrangement.
- Label which phases exist before, during and after a plateau; during a phase-change plateau two phases may coexist.

**Gas-volume explanations**
- **Higher temperature at constant pressure → larger volume:** faster particles exert greater pressure unless a movable boundary expands; expansion restores the external-pressure balance.
- **Higher pressure at constant temperature → smaller volume:** particles are forced closer together; their own size does not shrink.
- **Higher temperature at fixed volume → higher pressure:** more frequent and/or more forceful wall collisions.
- State what is held constant. A rigid vessel changes pressure rather than volume when heated.

**Diffusion**
- Diffusion is **net** movement of particles from higher concentration to lower concentration due to random motion.
- At uniform concentration particles still move randomly; there is no net movement in one direction.
- At the same temperature and under comparable conditions, a gas with lower **relative molecular mass** generally diffuses faster than a heavier gas.
- Higher temperature raises particle speed and diffusion rate. Compare both temperature and molecular mass when both vary.
- Check that both substances are actually gases at the stated temperature before ranking gaseous diffusion.
- If asked for an explanation, give random motion and the concentration gradient, not merely “the gas spreads out.”

### Extended mark-point reference

**Distinguishing features, one point per line**
- Solid shape remains fixed unless a force deforms the solid.
- Solid volume remains approximately fixed under ordinary pressure.
- Liquid shape adapts to the lower part of its container.
- Liquid volume remains approximately fixed at a given temperature.
- Gas shape adapts to the entire accessible container.
- Gas volume changes readily with its container or external pressure.
- Solid particles are packed very close together.
- Liquid particles remain near one another.
- Gas particles are much farther apart than liquid particles.
- Solid arrangement is ordered.
- Liquid arrangement is disordered.
- Gas arrangement is random throughout available space.
- Solid motion is vibration around fixed positions.
- Liquid motion includes sliding past neighbours.
- Gas motion includes rapid travel between collisions.
- A particle's speed increases on average as temperature rises.
- Kinetic energy describes energy associated with particle motion.
- Substances have particles even when no motion is visible to the eye.
- Compressing a gas closes empty spaces more than it changes particle size.
- Compressing a liquid causes much less volume change than compressing a gas.
- Compressing a solid causes little volume change under ordinary conditions.

**State-change response points**
- Identify the starting state before describing particle motion.
- Identify the final state after describing particle motion.
- Melting begins when the solid reaches its melting point at the stated pressure.
- During melting, particles overcome enough attraction to move out of fixed positions.
- Melting does not require particles to move far apart like gas particles.
- Freezing reverses the change from sliding motion to fixed-position vibration.
- Heating before melting increases average kinetic energy.
- Heating during the melting plateau changes arrangement without raising temperature.
- Heating a liquid before boiling increases average kinetic energy.
- Heating during a boiling plateau separates particles without raising temperature.
- Gas formation at boiling occurs throughout the bulk liquid.
- Surface evaporation can occur without bulk boiling.
- Evaporation preferentially removes particles with relatively high kinetic energy.
- Cooling by evaporation concerns the average energy of particles remaining behind.
- Condensation is associated with a large reduction in particle separation.
- Condensation releases energy to the surroundings.
- Freezing releases energy to the surroundings.
- Sublimation is a direct transition from a solid to a gas.
- Deposition is the reverse direct transition from a gas to a solid.
- A heating curve's horizontal section need not mean heating stopped.
- A cooling curve's horizontal section need not mean cooling stopped.
- A flat line on a phase-change curve represents constant temperature.
- A diagonal line represents temperature changing with time or energy supplied.
- A graph of temperature against time requires reading the axes before assigning a state.
- Pure-substance phase-change temperature is fixed at a specified pressure.
- Changes of state are physical changes if chemical identities remain unchanged.

**Gas behaviour and diffusion**
- Gas wall collisions exert pressure on container walls.
- More frequent wall collisions can raise pressure at fixed volume.
- More forceful wall collisions can raise pressure at fixed volume.
- Higher temperature at constant pressure can expand a gas.
- Higher external pressure at constant temperature can compress a gas.
- Higher temperature at constant volume tends to raise pressure.
- Lower temperature at constant pressure tends to contract a gas.
- The effect of pressure on gas volume is not a chemical reaction.
- Net diffusion requires a difference in concentration between regions.
- Random motion continues after diffusion reaches uniform distribution.
- Uniform distribution means no sustained net movement in one direction.
- Molecules may diffuse through spaces among other gas molecules.
- Gases of lower relative molecular mass generally diffuse faster under comparable conditions.
- Compare molecular rather than just elemental atomic mass for gaseous molecules.
- Compare gases at a common temperature if ranking by molecular mass alone.
- Check a substance's melting and boiling points before assuming it is gaseous.
- Increased temperature increases average particle speed and diffusion rate.
- A diffusion explanation should mention random particle movement.
- A diffusion explanation should identify the higher-concentration starting region.
- A diffusion explanation should identify the lower-concentration destination region.
- Rate of diffusion is a particle-motion concept, not the same as reaction rate.

### Targeted practice: interpreting particle information
- Given a sketch, count whether particles occupy the entire vessel.
- Given a sketch, inspect whether regular rows are present.
- Given a sketch, inspect whether particles touch.
- Given a sketch, distinguish vibration arrows from translation arrows.
- On a heating graph, identify the first plateau as melting if the starting material is solid.
- On a heating graph, identify the later plateau as boiling if liquid is heated into gas.
- On a cooling graph, identify condensation before freezing if the starting material is gas.
- At a plateau, name the two coexisting phases if the question asks for both.
- Explain that particles remain the same chemical substance during physical state changes.
- Use the term “average kinetic energy” for the temperature-linked quantity.
- Do not claim every particle has the same kinetic energy.
- Do not say individual particles expand when a gas expands.
- At constant pressure, a heated flexible gas container can expand.
- At constant volume, a heated sealed rigid container cannot expand.
- At constant temperature, added external pressure can reduce gas volume.
- A smaller gas volume means less average separation between particles.
- A higher gas temperature generally means higher average particle speed.
- A lower gas temperature generally means lower average particle speed.
- A diffusion diagram should show mixing rather than particles settling by mass.
- Particles of both gases move; one gas does not need to push the other.
- The heavier gas can still diffuse even though it diffuses more slowly.
- Compare speeds under identical temperature before using relative molecular mass.
- Compare diffusion observations over equal distances when ranking time taken.
- A change in concentration can create a new net diffusion direction.
- Even at equilibrium random particle motion persists.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning
- **Compression distractor:** if pressure rises while temperature is fixed, a gas occupies less volume but a liquid changes volume very little; particle size itself does not change.
- **Balloon cooling:** a lower gas temperature means slower particles and lower pressure before the flexible balloon contracts; it does not imply helium condensed under ordinary cooling.
- **Diffusion ranking:** compare BOTH gas temperature and whole-molecule mass. A lighter gas at higher temperature is a clear faster candidate; do not compare atom count alone.
- **State check:** a low Mr cannot make a liquid the fastest *gas* diffuser if that substance is not gaseous at the stated temperature.
- **Heating-curve trap:** a flat temperature segment means energy is still being transferred but is used to change state, not increase average kinetic energy.
- **Random motion trap:** diffusion reaching uniform concentration means zero *net* migration, not that particles stop moving.
- **Gas-pressure reasoning:** more frequent wall collisions may arise from compression; more forceful collisions may arise from heating. Keep the stated constant variable in view.

## CHAPTER 2: ATOMS, ELEMENTS, COMPOUNDS AND BONDING (2.1–2.7)

**Elements, compounds and mixtures**
- **Element:** substance containing only one type of atom; cannot be decomposed into simpler substances by chemical means.
- **Compound:** two or more *different* elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio; separation into elements requires chemical change.
- **Mixture:** substances physically mixed without chemical combination in a fixed ratio; composition may vary and physical separation is possible.
- A molecular element can still contain two or more atoms *of the same element*. Being molecular does not make it a compound.
- For a particle diagram, first identify which unlike atoms are bonded; then decide whether one substance or several substances are present.

**Atomic structure**
- The nucleus contains protons and neutrons; electrons occupy surrounding shells. Almost all atomic mass is in the nucleus.
- **Proton:** relative charge +1; relative mass 1.
- **Neutron:** relative charge 0; relative mass 1.
- **Electron:** relative charge −1; relative mass approximately 1/1836 (often treated as negligible).
- **Proton/atomic number (Z):** number of protons; fixes the identity of the element.
- **Mass/nucleon number (A):** protons + neutrons. Hence neutrons = A − Z.
- A neutral atom has electrons = protons. A positive ion has fewer electrons than protons; a negative ion has more.
- For the first twenty elements, distribute electrons in shells using the syllabus convention (first shell up to 2, second up to 8, third up to 8 before the next shell).
- In Groups I–VII the group number gives outer-shell electrons; period number gives the number of occupied shells.
- Noble gases have a full outer shell: helium has 2 in its first shell; the other familiar noble gases have 8 outer electrons.

**Isotopes and nuclide notation**
- Isotopes are atoms of the **same element** with the same proton number but different numbers of neutrons, and hence different mass numbers.
- Neutral isotopes of one element have the same electron configuration and therefore very similar chemical properties; their masses and some physical properties can differ.
- Read a nuclide symbol by its upper mass number and lower proton number; an ion's superscript charge separately changes its electron count.
- Ion formation never changes the nucleus: changing electrons does not create a new isotope.
- Relative atomic mass is the abundance-weighted mean mass relative to 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
- **Weighted mean:** Ar = Σ(isotopic mass × abundance) / Σ(abundance). Divide by 100 for percentage abundances, but by the total frequencies for peak heights or ratios.
- An answer should lie between the smallest and largest isotope masses; a non-integer Ar is normal.

**Ions and ionic bonding**
- **Cation:** positive ion, usually formed when a metal atom loses electron(s).
- **Anion:** negative ion, usually formed when a non-metal atom gains electron(s).
- In an ionic dot-and-cross diagram show the transferred outer electrons, each completed outer shell, **square brackets and the charge on each ion**.
- Oppositely charged ions form a giant, regularly alternating three-dimensional lattice. The ionic bond is strong **electrostatic attraction** between opposite charges.
- Ionic formulas use the smallest whole-number ratio balancing total positive and negative charge; put brackets around multiple polyatomic ions.
- **High melting/boiling point:** much energy is required to overcome electrostatic attractions throughout the lattice.
- **Solid does not conduct:** ions exist but are fixed in position. **Molten or aqueous conducts:** ions are mobile and carry charge.
- Do not explain conduction of an ionic solution by free electrons moving through that solution.

**Covalent bonds and simple molecules**
- A covalent bond is a **shared pair of electrons**, allowing atoms to attain stable outer shells (two for H; normally eight for the common non-metals here).
- In dot-and-cross diagrams use different marks to trace each atom's outer electrons; show shared pairs and all required lone pairs.
- One shared pair is a single bond, two a double bond and three a triple bond.
- Be able to draw H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃, HCl, CH₃OH, C₂H₄, O₂, CO₂ and N₂, plus analogous molecules specified in a question.
- Oxygen in H₂O has two bonds and two lone pairs; nitrogen in NH₃ has three bonds and one lone pair; bonded Cl has three lone pairs.
- O₂ has a double bond; N₂ has a triple bond; CO₂ has two C=O bonds; ethene has a C=C bond.
- Simple molecules have strong covalent bonds **within** each molecule but relatively weak attractions **between** molecules.
- **Low melting/boiling points:** overcoming intermolecular attractions needs relatively little energy; melting does *not* usually break the internal covalent bonds.
- **Poor electrical conductivity:** neutral molecules have no mobile ions or delocalised electrons. “No electrons” is false.

**Giant covalent structures**
- **Diamond:** each C bonds to four other C atoms in a three-dimensional network. Many strong bonds give great hardness and a high melting point; useful for cutting tools. No mobile electrons, so it does not conduct.
- **Graphite:** each C bonds to three neighbours within a layer; one electron per C is delocalised and mobile, enabling electrical conduction and electrode use.
- Weak attractions between graphite's layers allow them to slide, making graphite soft and useful as a lubricant; strong covalent bonds *within* layers account for its high melting point.
- **Silicon(IV) oxide:** a three-dimensional giant Si–O covalent network; like diamond it is hard, has a high melting point and lacks mobile electrons.
- Do not describe giant covalent SiO₂ as separate little SiO₂ molecules; the formula expresses the overall ratio.

**Metallic bonding and alloys**
- A metal is a giant lattice of **positive metal ions** attracted to a sea of **mobile delocalised electrons**.
- Mobile electrons carry charge (electrical conduction) and help transfer energy (thermal conduction).
- **Malleable** means deformable into sheets; **ductile** means drawable into wires. Metal-ion layers can slide while attraction to the electron sea persists.
- Strong metallic attraction often gives relatively high melting/boiling points, though values vary among metals.
- An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with other element(s); differently sized particles disrupt regular layers and hinder sliding, often making it harder than the pure metal.
- Do not call an alloy an ionic compound or say metallic bonding disappears when a metal bends.

**Structure/property comparison**
- Ionic: high melting point from electrostatic attraction; conducts molten/aqueous by mobile ions, not as a solid.
- Simple molecular: low melting point from weak intermolecular forces; generally does not conduct.
- Giant covalent: high melting point from many strong covalent bonds; graphite is a conducting exception because it has mobile electrons.
- Metallic: often high melting point from metal-ion/electron attraction; solid metal conducts because delocalised electrons move.

### Targeted practice: structure and bonding distinctions
- In an atom diagram, electron symbols should appear in shells, not in the nucleus.
- Two atoms of the same element can have different neutron numbers.
- Two ions of different elements can have equal numbers of electrons.
- Equal electron counts do not establish equal proton numbers.
- Relative atomic mass describes a mixture of isotopes, not one nucleus.
- An isotope symbol does not itself show a charge unless a charge is written.
- Ionic formation involves electron transfer rather than sharing.
- Covalent formation involves sharing rather than complete transfer.
- Metallic bonding involves delocalisation over a lattice.
- Strong bonds within molecules are not the same as forces between molecules.
- A simple molecule may contain strong covalent bonds but still have low melting point.
- A lattice has a continuous repeating arrangement, not isolated molecules.
- The formula of an ionic salt is a charge-balanced ratio.
- The formula of a molecular substance gives actual atom counts.
- A dot-and-cross diagram distinguishes the original atoms' electrons.
- The electrons themselves are physically identical despite different diagram symbols.
- Check a bond drawing for the correct total number of outer electrons.
- A noble-gas-like outer shell may be achieved by ion transfer or covalent sharing.
- Conductivity demands a mobile charged species, not merely the presence of charges.
- Graphite's charge carriers are electrons, not mobile carbon ions.
- Molten ionic compounds conduct through ions, not the same carriers as graphite.
- In metallic solids mobile electrons move while the ion lattice remains in place.
- Weak forces between graphite layers enable sliding.
- Strong bonds within graphite layers resist melting.
- Alloying disrupts regular packing but does not eliminate mobile electrons.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning
- **Mixture or compound:** a specimen containing two unbonded substances remains a mixture even when its main ingredient is a named compound; count distinct elements separately from distinct substances.
- **Total versus bonding electrons:** sum atomic numbers for all electrons in a neutral molecule; count two electrons per covalent bond for bonding electrons only. Lone-pair electrons count in the first total but not the second.
- **Hydride trap:** H⁻ has gained an electron and has a full two-electron first shell; it does not have zero or eight outer electrons.
- **Isoelectronic does not mean isotopic:** two ions may have the same electron arrangement yet have different proton numbers and hence be different elements.
- **Shell versus group:** a full first shell can have two electrons (helium); “every noble gas has eight” is therefore false.
- **Metal bonding wording:** metallic bonding attracts positive *ions*, not neutral atoms, to mobile delocalised electrons.
- **Structure matching:** a substance with giant covalent bonding may be hard without conducting; graphite is the mobile-electron exception, not a model for diamond.
- **Ionic conduction:** a dissolved or molten salt conducts because ions move; a solid salt still contains ions but cannot conduct by their migration.
- **Formula representation:** distinguish a formula unit's simplest atom ratio from the charge-balanced ionic formula notation used to show separate ions.

## CHAPTER 3: STOICHIOMETRY (3.1–3.3)

**Formulae, equations and states**
- Know the correct formulas of syllabus elements, compounds and common ions before balancing equations.
- **Molecular formula:** actual number of each type of atom in one molecule. **Empirical formula:** simplest whole-number ratio of atoms or ions.
- Determine an ionic formula from charge balance; a formula unit is not necessarily a discrete ionic molecule.
- When reading a model, count atoms of each type before reducing the ratio for an empirical formula.
- A word equation names reactants and products. A symbol equation gives correct formulas and uses **coefficients**, never altered subscripts, to balance atoms.
- State symbols: (s) solid; (l) liquid; (g) gas; (aq) dissolved in water. They describe the stated conditions, not the substance's appearance in an unrelated setting.
- Net ionic equations omit spectator ions, but retain reacting solids and their state symbols; check charge as well as atom balance.
- Relative atomic mass Ar is the isotopic weighted average relative to carbon-12; relative molecular/formula mass Mr is the sum of the constituent Ar values.
- Include all bracketed groups and waters of crystallisation when finding formula mass. Ar and Mr are relative quantities without units; molar mass is in g mol⁻¹.

**Core calculations**
- **Moles from mass:** n = m/M; rearrange to m = nM or M = m/n.
- **Avogadro constant:** 1 mol contains 6.02 × 10²³ specified particles; number N = n × 6.02 × 10²³.
- Name the particles requested—atoms, ions, molecules or formula units. A molecule with several atoms produces several atomic particles per molecule.
- **Gas volume at r.t.p.:** n = V/24 if V is in dm³; equivalently n = V/24 000 if V is in cm³.
- **Volume conversion:** 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. Divide cm³ by 1000 to get dm³.
- **Solution concentration:** c (mol dm⁻³) = n/V (dm³); n = cV. Concentration can also be given in g dm⁻³; use molar mass to convert.
- **Percentage composition:** mass of named element/component in one formula unit ÷ total formula mass × 100.
- **Percentage purity:** mass of desired pure component ÷ total mass of original impure sample × 100.
- **Percentage yield:** actual product yield ÷ maximum theoretical product yield × 100.

**Empirical and molecular formula method**
1. Convert each elemental mass or percentage to a mass; for percentages, assume a convenient 100 g sample.
2. Divide each mass by its Ar to obtain amounts in moles.
3. Divide all mole amounts by the smallest; multiply every ratio by the same small integer if needed for whole numbers.
4. Write the simplest empirical formula and calculate its relative formula mass.
5. If Mr is known, find multiplier = Mr ÷ empirical-formula mass; multiply *all* empirical subscripts to obtain molecular formula.

**Reacting amounts and limiting reagents**
1. Write and balance the chemical equation; identify the substance whose amount is known.
2. Convert known mass, gas volume or solution volume/concentration into moles.
3. Apply the **coefficient** ratio to find moles of the requested reactant/product.
4. Convert that amount to the requested mass, volume, concentration or particle count, with units.
- With two measured reactants, compare available moles divided by their respective coefficients: the smaller reaction extent identifies the limiting reactant.
- The excess reagent is left over; it does not increase product after the limiting reagent is exhausted.
- When a reactant is impure, calculate its chemically available mass before using the equation.
- If a measured yield is apparently over 100%, recheck arithmetic, formula mass, moisture or purity assumptions.
- Keep extra digits in intermediate steps and round only the final answer to the requested significant figures.

**Titration calculations and hydrates**
- Use cV to find moles in the known measured solution, apply the balanced acid–base ratio, then divide moles of the unknown by its volume in dm³.
- An endpoint volume is a titre; do not assume every acid/alkali pair reacts 1:1.
- For a hydrated salt, calculate moles of water lost and moles of anhydrous salt remaining, then take their simplest whole-number ratio.
- Dehydration calculations require confidence that the heating removed water rather than decomposing the salt.

**Chemical equation discipline**
- Start with correct species formulas.
- Write reactants on the left.
- Write products on the right.
- Count each element's atoms on both sides.
- Change coefficients until counts match.
- Never repair balance by changing a compound's subscript.
- Check polyatomic groups that survive unchanged as groups.
- Add state symbols where the question requests them.
- Check whether water is liquid or gaseous in stated conditions.
- Check whether a soluble salt should be aqueous.

- Confirm total charge is equal on both sides.

- Confirm an electron-transfer half-equation balances electrical charge.

**Numerical checks**
- Convert grams into moles using the correct molar mass.
- Convert moles into grams by multiplying by molar mass.
- Convert cm³ into dm³ by dividing by 1000.
- Convert dm³ into cm³ by multiplying by 1000.
- Use 24 dm³ per mole of gas at r.t.p. when specified.
- Use 24 000 cm³ per mole if gas volume is in cm³.
- Convert solution concentration in mol dm⁻³ into amount using n = cV.
- Convert mass concentration in g dm⁻³ into molar concentration by dividing by M.
- Convert molar concentration into mass concentration by multiplying by M.
- Use Avogadro's constant for particle count only after identifying particle type.
- Multiply molecule count by atoms per molecule for an atom count.
- Do not mix grams and kilograms in one mass ratio.
- Do not mix cm³ and dm³ in n = cV.
- Check whether a reaction produces one or several gas species.
- Check whether collected gas is measured at r.t.p. before using 24 dm³ mol⁻¹.
- Apply balanced-equation coefficients to convert between reagents.
- Divide available moles by the reagent coefficient to compare limiting reagents.
- A leftover reactant is excess, not a second limiting reagent.
- The maximum theoretical yield is based on limiting reactant.
- The actual yield is measured product, not starting material.
- Percentages must use a clearly identified numerator and denominator.
- An empirical ratio is made of whole numbers after scaling.
- A molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of its empirical formula.
- A hydration number is a mole ratio of water to dry salt.
- An isotope weighted mean uses total abundance as denominator.
- Keep sufficient intermediate precision before rounding.
- Give the final answer with the requested significant figures.
- Include g, mol, dm³, cm³ or mol dm⁻³ as appropriate.

**Useful formula identities**
- n = m/M.
- m = nM.
- N = n × (6.02 × 10²³).
- n(gas at r.t.p.) = V(dm³)/24.
- n(gas at r.t.p.) = V(cm³)/24 000.
- n(solute) = c(mol dm⁻³) × V(dm³).
- c = n/V(dm³).
- Mass concentration = molar concentration × M.
- Percentage yield = 100 × actual/theoretical.
- Percentage purity = 100 × pure part/original sample.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning
- **Count the requested particles:** moles of water molecules are not moles of atoms; multiply the molecule count by the atoms per molecule only if the question asks for atoms.

- **Ionic state symbols:** an insoluble solid carbonate in acid is not free CO₃²⁻(aq); retain the solid formula in the net ionic equation.
- **Relative atomic mass:** the defining denominator is one-twelfth of carbon-12's mass; it is not carbon-12's whole mass.
- **Empirical versus ionic formula:** an empirical atom ratio may be expanded from an ionic formula to count all atoms; do not confuse atom counts with ion charges.
- **Limiting reagent:** equal *mass* of different reactants does not imply equal moles; convert each using its own molar mass first.
- **Mole ratio:** products' coefficients, not formula subscripts, control reaction extent.

## CHAPTER 4: ELECTROCHEMISTRY (4.1–4.2)

**Electrolysis fundamentals**
- Electrolysis is decomposition of a molten or aqueous ionic compound by the passage of electric current; the ionic substance undergoing it is the **electrolyte**.
- **Anode:** positive electrode; oxidation/loss of electrons takes place. **Cathode:** negative electrode; reduction/gain of electrons takes place.
- Cations move towards the cathode; anions move towards the anode. Mobile ions carry charge in the electrolyte, while electrons carry charge through the external circuit.
- Electrode labels depend on the **power-supply connections**, not on whether an electrode is drawn left or right.
- In a molten binary salt there is no water competing for discharge: a metal usually forms at the cathode and a non-metal at the anode.
- In aqueous solution water also contributes possible products. Consider both the metal ion/hydrogen at the cathode and halide/oxygen at the anode.
- Half-equations must balance both atoms and electric charge; electrons are reactants in cathode reduction and products in anode oxidation.

**Required electrode systems and observations**
- **Molten lead(II) bromide:** Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb at the cathode; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode. Lead appears at the cathode and brown bromine forms at the anode.
- **Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (inert electrodes):** hydrogen bubbles at the cathode, chlorine at the anode; the solution remaining becomes alkaline. Cathode reduction may be written 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻; anode 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
- **Dilute sulfuric acid (inert electrodes):** hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode; approximately twice as much H₂ as O₂ by volume. Anode equation may be written 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ when appropriate to the scheme.
- **Aqueous copper(II) sulfate (carbon/graphite electrodes):** Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu at the cathode; oxygen bubbles at the anode; loss of dissolved copper(II) can make the blue solution paler.
- **Aqueous copper(II) sulfate (copper electrodes):** Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ at the anode, so it loses mass; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu at the cathode, so it gains mass. Copper ions are replenished by the anode.
- For a **dilute aqueous halide**, oxygen may form at the anode where concentrated solution yields halogen; never omit concentration from a product prediction.
- Inert graphite/platinum electrodes conduct and do not themselves take part in the intended electrode reaction; a reactive copper anode behaves differently.

**Electroplating**
- Purpose: improve appearance and/or resistance to corrosion by depositing a thin metal layer.
- Connect the object to the **negative cathode**; use an electrolyte containing ions of the metal to be deposited.
- A suitable positive anode of plating metal can dissolve to replace ions deposited at the cathode.
- If the object were the anode, oxidation rather than the desired metal-ion reduction would occur there.

**Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells**
- Hydrogen and oxygen are supplied to produce electrical energy; water is the only chemical product of the cell's specified reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
- A fuel-cell vehicle has no CO₂ or CO exhaust from the cell at its point of use, unlike a petrol engine; production and transport of hydrogen are separate environmental questions.
- Hydrogen storage/distribution and refuelling infrastructure can be disadvantages. Do not confuse an unqualified statement that hydrogen is 'dangerous' with a precise storage explanation.
- A fuel cell continues while reactants are supplied; it does not merely discharge a fixed closed stock of chemicals once.

## CHAPTER 6.4: REDOX

**Definitions and identification**
- **Oxidation:** gain of oxygen, loss of electrons, or increase in oxidation number.
- **Reduction:** loss of oxygen, gain of electrons, or decrease in oxidation number.
- **Redox reaction:** oxidation and reduction happen simultaneously; electrons lost by one species are gained by another.
- **Oxidising agent:** oxidises another species and is itself reduced. **Reducing agent:** reduces another species and is itself oxidised.
- Name the actual atom, ion or molecule involved and explain its change; “iodine loses electrons” is not interchangeable with “iodide ions lose electrons” in an iodide reaction.

**Oxidation-number rules**
- Uncombined element: oxidation number 0. Monatomic ion: oxidation number equals its charge.
- In a neutral compound, the oxidation numbers sum to 0; in a polyatomic ion they sum to that ion's charge.
- Use Roman numerals in compound names to identify oxidation states where required; an oxidation number is not automatically the charge on the whole compound.
- A rise in oxidation number identifies oxidation; a fall identifies reduction. Check both changes before assigning oxidising/reducing agents.

**Colour observations**
- Acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII), initially purple, can be reduced to a colourless solution by a reducing agent.
- Aqueous iodide can be oxidised to iodine, giving a brown solution; identify which species is oxidised and which oxidising agent caused it.
- Colour alone is not a universal redox explanation: relate an observation to the reagent and the electron or oxidation-number change.

---

### Redox mark-point reference
**Redox bookkeeping**
- Oxidation and reduction occur together.
- Oxidation may be identified by electron loss.
- Reduction may be identified by electron gain.
- Oxidation may be identified by oxygen gain.
- Reduction may be identified by oxygen loss.
- Oxidation number increases on oxidation.
- Oxidation number decreases on reduction.
- A reducing agent itself undergoes oxidation.
- An oxidising agent itself undergoes reduction.
- An uncombined element has oxidation number zero.
- A monatomic ion has oxidation number equal to ionic charge.
- Oxidation numbers total zero in a neutral compound.
- Oxidation numbers total ionic charge in a polyatomic ion.
- Roman numerals can identify oxidation number in compound names.
- Name the species gaining or losing electrons.
- Do not substitute an element name for a specific reacting ion when precision matters.
- Purple acidified manganate(VII) can become colourless on reduction.
- Aqueous iodide can become brown when iodine forms by oxidation.
- Identify the oxidising agent from its own reduction, not from its name.

### Targeted practice: tracking electron and oxidation-number changes

- Mark a decrease as reduction.
- Identify the atom or ion whose oxidation number changes.

- When a positive metal ion gains electrons to form metal, reduction occurs.
- When a halogen molecule becomes halide, reduction occurs.
- When a halide becomes halogen, oxidation occurs.
- A redox equation should have both an electron donor and an electron recipient.

- Assign agent labels after assigning oxidation and reduction, not before.

### Oxidation-number assignment and explanation practice
- Start with any elemental reactant assigned oxidation number zero.
- Assign the oxidation number of a monatomic cation from its charge.
- Assign the oxidation number of a monatomic anion from its charge.
- For a neutral formula, use the condition that the oxidation-number sum is zero.
- For an ion, use the condition that the oxidation-number sum equals ionic charge.
- Track a named element through reactant and product formulas.
- Repeat for the partner element to locate the corresponding reduction.
- Do not infer redox from the mere presence of oxygen in a formula.
- Do not infer redox from a precipitate forming.
- Do not infer redox from a reversible arrow.
- If a species gains oxygen from another, identify which lost oxygen.
- If an ion loses electrons, the electron appears among half-equation products.
- If an ion gains electrons, the electron appears among half-equation reactants.
- The charge of a product ion can differ from the reactant without changing elemental identity.
- A positive metal ion becoming neutral metal has been reduced.
- A neutral metal becoming a positive ion has been oxidised.
- A neutral halogen becoming halide has been reduced.
- A halide becoming neutral halogen has been oxidised.
- Name the *oxidising agent* as the species that accepts electrons.
- Name the *reducing agent* as the species that supplies electrons.
- Naming the species alone may not earn an explanation mark requiring an electron transfer.
- Explaining electron transfer alone may not earn a mark requiring the species name.
- If colours are requested, give initial and final colours.
- If roles are requested, say which reagent causes the observed colour change.
- Acidified manganate(VII) reduction is a specific test observation, not a general colour rule for every oxidant.
- Formation of iodine from iodide is oxidation despite iodine itself being an element in the product.
- A compound's Roman numeral indicates the named element's oxidation state.
- Roman numerals belong in the compound name, not in an ion's charge superscript.
- In a complex ion, the total charge is not necessarily the central atom's oxidation number.
- Balance whole equations after assigning redox changes when a question asks for an equation.
- In a paired half-equation method, equalise electron numbers before adding the two halves.
- Cancel only species that appear unchanged on both sides after combination.

- Use a colour observation as supporting evidence, not as a replacement for conservation checks.
- Distinguish the oxidation number of an element in its pure state from the oxidation number it has inside a compound.
- If one species is oxidised, another must be reduced even if the latter is not explicitly named in the stem.
- Oxygen gain/loss language is most useful when oxygen is actually transferred.

### Redox equations to recognise
- Mg + Cu²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Cu is a metal-displacement redox equation.
- Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ is its oxidation half-equation.
- Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu is its reduction half-equation.
- Chlorine displacement of iodide can be written Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
- Iodide oxidation in that equation is 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻.
- Chlorine reduction in that equation is Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
- Chlorine is the oxidising agent in this displacement.
- Iodide is the reducing agent in this displacement.
- In halide displacement, both halves must exchange the same number of electrons.
- In a metal displacement, the oxidised metal and reduced metal ion are different species.
- An element can act as an oxidising agent in one reaction and a reducing agent in another context.
- Hydrogen peroxide can act as an oxidising agent against iodide.
- Hydrogen peroxide can act as a reducing agent against acidified manganate(VII).
- Oxidation-number changes support these roles, but the partner reagent determines the observed role.
- A half-equation showing electrons among products indicates oxidation.
- A half-equation showing electrons among reactants indicates reduction.
- In a full redox equation, electrons should not remain as uncancelled external products.
- An oxidising agent does not itself have to contain oxygen.
- A reducing agent does not itself have to be a metal.
- Colour-change tests should specify the starting colour and final observation.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning
- **Agent reversal:** the species that causes oxidation is the oxidising agent but is itself reduced. The species that causes reduction is itself oxidised.
- **Colour identification:** purple acidified manganate(VII) becoming colourless shows its reduction; another dissolved reactant may be oxidised even if that reactant was initially colourless.
- **Halide identity:** brown iodine from iodide is an oxidation of I⁻, while a chlorine molecule that takes the electrons is reduced.
- **Oxidation numbers:** a redox decision requires a change in oxidation number, not merely the presence of an element written with a Roman numeral.
- **No-change trap:** a reaction producing a precipitate, gas or water can be non-redox if no oxidation numbers change.
- **Elemental molecule:** a diatomic elemental gas has oxidation number zero for each atom, not −1 merely because that element forms an anion in other compounds.

## CHAPTER 7: ACIDS, BASES AND SALTS (7.1–7.3)

**Definitions, strength and indicators**
- An **acid** produces H⁺ ions in water and donates protons. A **base** accepts protons; a soluble base is an **alkali**, producing OH⁻ in water.
- Metal oxides and hydroxides can act as bases. A base need not itself dissolve in water.
- A **strong acid** dissociates completely in aqueous solution; a **weak acid** only partially dissociates. Strength is not the same as concentration.
- HCl, HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ are strong-acid examples; carboxylic acids are weak-acid examples. Show complete ionisation with a forward arrow and partial ionisation with a reversible arrow.
- **Litmus:** acids turn blue to red; alkalis turn red to blue. **Methyl orange:** red in acid, yellow in alkali. **Thymolphthalein:** colourless in acid, blue in alkali.
- **Universal indicator/pH:** below 7 acidic, 7 neutral and above 7 alkaline in the usual syllabus setting. Compare hydrogen-ion concentration using the colour chart; pH alone does not define whether an acid is intrinsically strong or weak.

**Characteristic reactions**
- Acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen; verify H₂ using a lighted splint and a squeaky pop.
- Acid + basic oxide/hydroxide → salt + water. Neutralisation is acid plus base, not an electron-transfer reaction by definition.
- Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide; CO₂ turns limewater milky.
- Acid + alkali has the net ionic equation H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). Remove genuine spectator ions, but not reacting solids from other ionic equations.
- Warm an ammonium salt with alkali to release ammonia; NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue.

**Oxides**
- **Acidic:** CO₂ and SO₂ react with bases/alkalis and can make acidic solutions.
- **Basic:** CuO and CaO react with acids to form salts and water.
- **Amphoteric:** Al₂O₃ and ZnO react with both acids and bases; do not classify them solely by whether they are metals' oxides.
- Insolubility does not imply inability to react with an acid or alkali.

**Solubility rules**
- Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble; **all nitrates** are soluble.
- Chlorides are generally soluble except silver and lead chlorides.
- Sulfates are generally soluble except barium, calcium and lead sulfates.
- Carbonates are generally insoluble except sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates.
- Hydroxides are generally insoluble except sodium, potassium and ammonium hydroxides; calcium hydroxide is partly soluble.
- Apply both cation and anion when selecting reagents, rather than memorising only one half of the salt name.

**Preparing salts**
- **Insoluble salt by precipitation:** choose TWO soluble solutions containing the desired ions; mix to precipitate, filter, wash the solid with water and dry it.
- **Soluble salt from acid + excess insoluble base/carbonate/metal:** add excess solid until reaction ends, filter unreacted excess, evaporate filtrate gently until saturated, cool to crystallise, then filter and dry crystals.
- **Soluble salt from acid + soluble alkali:** titrate to find exact reacting volumes; repeat with these volumes but without indicator, then concentrate, cool and crystallise.
- Excess insoluble solid can be removed by filtration. Excess soluble alkali cannot, which is why the titration route differs.
- Stop evaporation at a crystallisation point rather than heating all the liquid away. A precipitate is washed to remove soluble impurities.
- A **hydrate** contains chemically combined water of crystallisation in a defined ratio; **anhydrous** means containing no water. Wet surface water is not by itself water of crystallisation.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning

- **No-precipitate mixing:** after exchanging ions, check the solubility of BOTH possible products. If both remain soluble there need be no visible change.

## CHAPTER 8: THE PERIODIC TABLE (8.1–8.5)

**Arrangement**
- Elements are arranged in increasing **proton number**. Groups are vertical columns; periods are horizontal rows.
- Same-group elements have the same number of outer-shell electrons and similar chemical properties; the number of occupied shells matches the period number for the first twenty elements.
- Across a period, character generally shifts from metallic to non-metallic. A main-group element's position can predict likely ionic charge and properties.
- To predict an unfamiliar member, use its electron configuration and the supplied direction of a group trend rather than assuming every property is identical.

**Group I — alkali metals**
- Lithium, sodium and potassium are relatively soft metals with one outer electron and form +1 ions.
- Down the group: reactivity with water generally **increases**, melting point **decreases**, density generally **increases**.
- Reaction with cold water produces hydrogen and metal hydroxide (alkaline solution): 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂.
- State observations (fizzing, movement, warmth) separately from the explanation that the outer electron is lost more readily down the group.

**Group VII — halogens**
- Chlorine is a pale yellow-green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a grey-black solid at r.t.p.; elemental halogens are **diatomic**.
- Down the group: density and melting/boiling points generally increase, while chemical reactivity decreases.
- A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from solution: X₂ + 2Y⁻ → 2X⁻ + Y₂ when X is more reactive than Y.
- Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide; bromine displaces iodide but not chloride under ordinary conditions.
- In redox terms, the displaced halide ion loses electrons and the more reactive halogen gains electrons.
- Distinguish the colour of the pure halogen from the colour seen in the actual aqueous test.

**Transition elements**
- Compared with Group I metals, transition elements typically have higher density and melting points and may be harder.
- They often form **coloured compounds**, show **variable oxidation numbers** in compounds and act as **catalysts** as elements or compounds.
- Physical-property questions require physical properties; do not substitute “acts as a catalyst” for “high density.”
- A catalyst increases reaction rate without being a net reactant; a transition metal's oxidation states are not the same thing as its physical hardness.

**Noble gases**
- Group VIII/0 noble gases are monatomic and relatively unreactive because their outer electron shells are full.
- Helium has a full first shell of two electrons; other familiar noble gases have a full outer shell of eight.
- Down the group there are more occupied shells, but each atom retains a filled outer shell.

## CHAPTER 9: METALS (9.1, 9.2, 9.4 AND LISTED ALUMINIUM EXTRACTION)

**Properties and uses**
- Metals are usually good conductors of heat/electricity, malleable and ductile; non-metals are commonly poorer conductors and brittle as solids, with structural exceptions.
- Conductivity arises from mobile delocalised electrons. Malleability/ductility arises because metal-ion layers slide while metallic attraction remains.
- Aluminium's **low density** suits aircraft and overhead cables; good electrical conductivity also suits cables; corrosion resistance suits food containers.
- Copper's good conductivity and ductility suit electrical wiring.
- The protective aluminium oxide layer explains apparent unreactivity/corrosion resistance despite aluminium's high position in the reactivity series.

**Reactivity series and reactions**
- Learn: **K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > C > Zn > Fe > H > Cu > Ag > Au**. Carbon and hydrogen are reference positions, not metals.
- More reactive metals lose electrons/form positive ions more readily and displace less reactive metal ions from aqueous solutions.
- Potassium, sodium and calcium react with cold water to give metal hydroxide and hydrogen; magnesium reacts with steam to give its oxide and hydrogen.
- Magnesium, zinc and iron can release hydrogen from suitable dilute hydrochloric acid; copper, silver and gold do not under ordinary comparison conditions.
- Rank unfamiliar metals using actual water, acid or displacement evidence; account for an oxide coating that may delay visible reaction.
- A metal above hydrogen is not guaranteed identical speed with every acid: concentration, oxide layers and conditions also matter.

**Aluminium extraction**
- Electrolyse purified aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite. Cryolite lowers operating temperature; it is not the metal being produced.
- At the cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. At the anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.
- Oxygen produced at the anode reacts with the carbon anodes, causing them to lose mass and require replacement.
- Aluminium ions and oxide ions must be mobile in the hot electrolyte; solid aluminium oxide alone is unsuitable for conduction.

## CHAPTER 12: EXPERIMENTAL TECHNIQUES AND CHEMICAL ANALYSIS (12.1–12.5)

**Apparatus and experimental design**
- Time: stopwatch; temperature: thermometer; mass: balance; gas volume: gas syringe or appropriate displacement method.
- A volumetric pipette transfers an accurately fixed volume; a burette delivers a measured *variable* volume; a measuring cylinder is less precise.
- State the independent variable changed, dependent variable measured and relevant variables controlled for a fair comparison.
- Consider leaks, loss of gas before timing, solubility of gas, reading precision and repeatability when assessing a method.
- **Solute:** dissolved substance. **Solvent:** dissolving medium. **Solution:** solute(s) dissolved in solvent. **Saturated solution:** maximum dissolved solute at a specified temperature.
- **Residue:** material left behind by a separation; **filtrate:** liquid that passes through the filter.

**Titration**
1. Transfer a known volume of one solution using a volumetric pipette; add a suitable indicator.
2. Add the second solution from a burette until the indicator's endpoint colour change.
3. Calculate titre = final burette reading − initial reading. Repeat and use suitable consistent titres.
4. For concentration, find moles of known solution, apply the balanced reaction ratio, then divide moles of unknown by its volume in dm³.
- An indicator endpoint is an observable colour change; do not assume every acid–base reaction has a 1:1 mole ratio.

**Filtration, crystallisation and distillation**
- **Filtration:** filter paper traps insoluble solid residue; the liquid containing dissolved substances passes through as filtrate.
- **Crystallisation:** gently heat a solution to saturation, leave it to cool, then filter and dry the resulting crystals.
- **Simple distillation:** evaporate a volatile liquid from a mixture and condense it; useful for a solvent separated from non-volatile solute.
- **Fractional distillation:** a column gives repeated evaporation/condensation, separating miscible liquids with different boiling points; the lower-boiling component generally reaches the top and distils first.
- Water enters a Liebig condenser jacket through its lower port and leaves at the upper port for effective cooling.
- Use the physical properties supplied: filtration will not separate two substances that are both dissolved in the same solution.

**Paper chromatography**
1. Draw a *pencil* baseline and place small sample/reference spots on it.
2. Place the paper in suitable solvent with the liquid level **below** the baseline; allow solvent to rise and mark its front.
3. Use a locating agent to reveal colourless spots if required; compare unknown and reference positions under identical conditions.
4. Calculate Rf = distance from baseline to spot centre ÷ distance from baseline to solvent front. Rf has no units and normally lies between 0 and 1.
- Different spots can indicate a mixture; one spot under one method does not absolutely prove purity. An insoluble sample can remain at the baseline (Rf = 0).

**Purity, water and gas collection**
- Pure substances have characteristic sharp melting points and fixed boiling points at stated pressure; impurities generally broaden melting range and alter the temperatures.
- White anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns blue with water; blue anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride turns pink.
- Choose gas collection according to solubility in water and density relative to air. A gas syringe measures volume directly, but it must be connected without leaks.
- A drying agent must not react with the gas; do not use a basic drying agent to dry an acidic gas or concentrated sulfuric acid to dry ammonia.
- In an upright jar, a dense gas introduced near the bottom displaces air upwards. For a less-dense gas, arrange collection so it displaces air downwards. Check the drawing's inlet/outlet rather than relying on an ambiguous delivery-method name.

**Anion tests**
- **CO₃²⁻:** add dilute acid; evolved CO₂ turns limewater milky. Effervescence alone is not a conclusive gas identification.
- **Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻:** acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate: white AgCl, cream AgBr, yellow AgI.
- **NO₃⁻:** warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide and aluminium foil; reduction releases ammonia, which turns damp red litmus blue. Check for NH₄⁺ before adding aluminium if both could be present.
- **SO₄²⁻:** acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous barium nitrate; white barium sulfate precipitate.
- **SO₃²⁻:** decolourises purple acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII).

**Cation tests with aqueous alkali and ammonia**
- **NH₄⁺:** warm with aqueous NaOH; released ammonia turns damp red litmus blue.
- **Al³⁺:** white precipitate with NaOH, soluble in excess; white precipitate with NH₃, insoluble in excess.
- **Ca²⁺:** white precipitate with NaOH, insoluble in excess; generally no precipitate with NH₃ under the stated test conditions.
- **Cr³⁺:** grey-green precipitate, soluble in excess NaOH but insoluble in excess NH₃.
- **Cu²⁺:** light-blue precipitate with either reagent; insoluble in excess NaOH but dissolves in excess NH₃ to a dark-blue solution.
- **Fe²⁺:** green precipitate with NaOH or NH₃, insoluble in excess; may turn brown on standing.
- **Fe³⁺:** red-brown precipitate with NaOH or NH₃, insoluble in excess.
- **Zn²⁺:** white precipitate with NaOH or NH₃; soluble in excess of either to a colourless solution.
- A white precipitate is not by itself a unique ion identification: observe what happens with **excess** reagent and compare both reagents.

**Gas and flame tests**
- **NH₃:** damp red litmus turns blue. **CO₂:** limewater turns milky.
- **Cl₂:** bleaches damp litmus (which may first turn red). **H₂:** squeaky pop with a lighted splint. **O₂:** relights a glowing splint.
- **SO₂:** acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) changes purple to colourless.
- **Flame-test colours:** Li⁺ crimson, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Ba²⁺ apple-green, Cu²⁺ blue-green.

### Paper 1: tricky MCQ reasoning
- **Two soluble solids:** after both dissolve in water, ordinary filtration cannot separate them; chromatography can work if their movement with the solvent differs.
- **Chromatogram height:** compare spot distance from the BASELINE relative to the solvent front; a higher spot has higher Rf only when the same front is used.
- **Mixture inference:** a spot from an unknown matching one standard does not prove its *other* spot matches a different standard; inspect every spot independently.
- **Apparatus choice:** a burette measures an adjustable titre; a pipette transfers a fixed accurate volume; a measuring cylinder is not interchangeable when high precision is needed.
- **Acidic gas drying:** a basic drying agent can react with an acidic gas; select a non-reactive drying agent and verify the gas jar's inlet position for displacement of air.
- **White precipitate ambiguity:** a white metal hydroxide alone does not identify a unique cation; compare behaviour with excess NaOH and excess NH₃.
- **Halide versus sulfate:** acidified silver nitrate tests halides; acidified barium nitrate tests sulfate. Do not swap the identifying cation.
- **Ammonium versus nitrate:** ammonia before aluminium suggests ammonium; ammonia emerging only after aluminium reduction can indicate nitrate.
- **Gas-test specificity:** 'bubbles' is not a gas identity; use a lighted or glowing splint, limewater or damp litmus as appropriate.
