1 STATES OF MATTER

1.1 Solids, liquids and gases
- Solid: fixed shape and volume; particles close together in regular positions; vibrate about fixed positions.
- Liquid: fixed volume but takes container's shape; particles close together, irregularly arranged, moving/sliding past one another.
- Gas: no fixed shape or volume, fills container; particles far apart, randomly arranged, moving rapidly/randomly.
- For liquid-particle diagrams/descriptions, separately give random/not ordered arrangement and close separation (some particles touching); for gases, separately give random/rapid motion and far-apart separation.
- Freezing a liquid can release energy as stronger attractions form while particles remain similarly close; condensation gas → liquid changes their average separation substantially.
- A three-point melting description can separately require particles to move further apart, arrangement to change from ordered to random, and motion from vibration to travel/sliding. Freezing reverses each: closer, random to ordered, travel to vibration.
- Melting: heat increases particle energy sufficiently to overcome some attractions so particles leave fixed positions; temperature remains constant during the change of state.
- Boiling: particles throughout liquid gain enough energy to overcome attractions and form gas; boiling occurs at a fixed temperature at a given pressure.
- Evaporation: higher-energy particles escape from the liquid surface; can occur below boiling point; remaining liquid cools because its average kinetic energy decreases.
- Freezing/condensing: particles lose kinetic energy, move more slowly and become closer or fixed in place; energy is released during the change of state.
- Heating/cooling curve: sloping portion shows temperature/average kinetic energy changing; flat portion shows change of state at constant temperature, energy going into overcoming attractions or being released as attractions form.
- Higher gas temperature at constant pressure: particles have greater average kinetic energy, move faster and gas expands; at fixed volume pressure increases because collisions with the walls are more frequent/forceful.
- Higher gas pressure at constant temperature: particles are compressed into smaller volume; collisions with walls become more frequent.

- For a three-mark gas particle description, distinguish separation (far apart), arrangement (random/irregular) and motion (rapid/random movement); simply saying “not fixed” is insufficient for arrangement or motion.
- For a three-mark solid description, give touching/closely packed particles, regular/ordered arrangement and vibration about fixed positions; stating “fixed position” alone does not replace the motion mark.

1.2 Diffusion
- Diffusion: NET movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration by random motion until evenly distributed. Once evenly distributed the particles continue moving randomly, but there is no net diffusion in one direction.
- Three chlorine atoms independently chosen from chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 give four possible relative molecular masses for C2H3Cl3 (zero, one, two or three chlorine-37 atoms).
- At a common temperature, compare molecular masses even if formulas differ: CO (Mr 28) and N2 (Mr 28) have approximately equal diffusion rates; for CH4 (16), NH3 (17), N2 (28), CO2 (44), rate decreases in that order.
- Under identical conditions, NO2 diffuses more slowly than NO because NO2 has greater molecular mass; lowering temperature reduces molecular kinetic energy and hence slows diffusion. Fluorine molecules diffuse more rapidly than heavier halogen molecules at the same temperature.
- For gases at the same pressure, higher temperature increases particle speed and diffusion rate; compare both temperature and gas identity (nitrogen at 50 °C diffuses faster than nitrogen or oxygen at 25 °C and oxygen at 50 °C).
- Gas particles diffuse because they move randomly and can travel through spaces between other particles.
- Before comparing diffusion rates, first check that each substance is a GAS at the stated temperature (using melting/boiling points); at −50 °C H2S is gaseous while NH3 is liquid, so do not choose ammonia simply because its Mr is lowest. Lighter gas molecules diffuse faster than heavier gas molecules at the same temperature; lower relative molecular mass corresponds to higher average speed.

2 ATOMS, ELEMENTS AND COMPOUNDS

2.1 Elements, compounds and mixtures
- Element: contains only one type of atom; cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.
- Compound: two or more different elements chemically bonded in fixed proportions; properties differ from constituent elements; separation requires chemical change.
- A particle diagram for a mixture of nitrogen and argon must show N2 as pairs of identical atoms and argon as separate single atoms, without bonding nitrogen to argon.
- Mixture: substances not chemically bonded; proportions can vary; components retain their properties and can be separated physically.

2.2 Atomic structure and the Periodic Table
- Atom: small central nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells.
- Proton: relative charge +1, relative mass 1. Neutron: charge 0, relative mass 1. Electron: charge −1, relative mass about 1/1836.
- Proton (atomic) number = number of protons; neutral atom has equal numbers of electrons and protons.
- Mass (nucleon) number = protons + neutrons; neutrons = mass number − proton number.
- For elements with proton numbers 1–20, distribute electrons into shells using 2,8,8,2 as appropriate; add electrons for anions and remove them for cations.
- Group I–VII number gives number of outer-shell electrons; period number gives number of occupied shells; Group VIII/noble gases have a full outer shell.

2.3 Isotopes

- An ion with 20 electrons and charge +3 comes from an atom with 23 protons (vanadium); proton count = electron count + positive charge.
- For two charged species, calculate electrons as proton number minus positive charge: 54Fe2+ has 24 electrons and 59Co3+ has 24 electrons, but differing protons and neutrons mean they are not isotopes.
- For an isotope written 35Cl, a chloride ion has 17 protons, 18 neutrons and 18 electrons; potassium-39 loses one electron to form K+ (19 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons).
- Radon-220 and radon-222 each have 86 protons and the same electron arrangement but respectively 134 and 136 neutrons. Polonium-209 and polonium-210 respectively have 125 and 126 neutrons.
- An ion of silver-109 with +1 charge has 47 protons, 62 neutrons and 46 electrons; a sodium-23 cation has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 10 electrons.
- For a 31P3− ion: protons 15, neutrons 16 and electrons 18; for 15N3−: protons 7, neutrons 8 and electrons 10. The extra three electrons complete the outer shell. Mg2+ has a full outer shell after losing two electrons; Mg3N2 and Ca3P2 have charge-balanced formulae.
- An atom and an ion of the same element have the same number of protons, but different electron numbers; do not call the atom and its ion isotopes.
- Isotopes of one element have the same proton number but different numbers of neutrons, hence different mass numbers.
- Isotopes have the same chemical properties because they have the same number and arrangement of electrons / same electronic configuration.
- In nuclide notation, upper number is mass number and lower number is proton number; ion charge shows gain or loss of electrons, not a change to the nucleus.
- When abundance is a graph with relative heights 1 at mass 20 and 5 at mass 22, divide (1×20+5×22) by (1+5) = 21.7; the denominator is TOTAL relative abundance, not automatically 100.
- Relative atomic mass = sum of (isotopic mass × fractional abundance), or sum of (isotopic mass × percentage abundance) / 100. To recover missing isotope mass, subtract the known contributions then divide by its remaining fractional abundance: 51.8 = 0.40(50)+0.20(55)+0.40x gives x=52.
- Example: titanium with isotope percentages 9% mass 46, 7% mass 47, 75% mass 48, 9% mass 49: [(9 × 46)+(7 × 47)+(75 × 48)+(9 × 49)]/100 = 47.84. Numerator and denominator are separately credited.
- Example: sulfur with 95.04% mass 32, 0.75% mass 33, 4.20% mass 34 and 0.01% mass 36: [(95.04 × 32)+(0.75 × 33)+(4.20 × 34)+(0.01 × 36)]/100 = 32.09. Correct numerator and denominator can be separate marks.

2.4 Ions and ionic bonds
- Against MgO, NaCl has singly charged ions (Na+, Cl−); Mg2+ and O2− have larger opposite charges, stronger electrostatic attraction and a higher melting point. Name the charge difference and stronger attraction separately when marks are split.
- Metal atoms lose electrons to make positive cations; non-metal atoms gain electrons to make negative anions; electrons transfer rather than protons moving.
- Ionic bond: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- When asked for a lattice description, state a regular/ordered arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions; “giant lattice” alone does not earn the description mark when already given.
- For excess-insoluble-reactant salt preparation, check limiting reagent first: 3.50/81=0.0432 mol ZnO requires 0.0864 mol HCl, but 50.0 cm3 of 1.20 mol dm−3 HCl supplies 0.0600 mol; ZnO is in excess, then filter it off. Similarly 4.50/125=0.0360 mol ZnCO3 requires 0.0720 mol HNO3, while 50.0 cm3 of 1.30 mol dm−3 supplies 0.0650 mol; carbonate is in excess.
- When explaining a high melting point, give BOTH the giant ionic lattice and the strong attractions between opposite ions; neither statement alone earns both marks.
- Ionic compounds form giant regular lattices with alternating cations and anions; formula gives smallest whole-number ion ratio ensuring zero overall charge.
- Dot-and-cross diagram: show transferred outer electrons and the resulting charges/brackets around ions; use different symbols to distinguish original atoms' electrons.
- High melting/boiling points: strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions throughout lattice require much energy to overcome.
- Molten or aqueous ionic compound conducts because ions are free to move and carry charge; solid does not because ions are fixed in the lattice.

2.5 Simple molecules and covalent bonds
- Covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons between atoms; shared electrons enable full outer shells (or two electrons for hydrogen).
- In dot-and-cross diagrams, distinguish each atom's electrons and show one shared pair for a single bond, two for double, three for triple; include unshared outer electrons where relevant.
- Be able to draw H2, Cl2, H2O, CH4, NH3, HCl, CH3OH, C2H4, O2, CO2 and N2 with correct bond count and outer electrons.
- In a property explanation, identify the weak forces as intermolecular forces, not weak covalent bonds; these are separately tested in simple-molecule questions.
- Simple molecular substances generally have low melting/boiling points because relatively weak forces between molecules require little energy to overcome; covalent bonds within molecules are not broken on melting/boiling.
- In a covalent substance lacking electrical conductivity, state there are no MOBILE electrons/charged particles; “no electrons” would be wrong.
- Simple molecules usually do not conduct electricity because they lack mobile ions and delocalised electrons.

2.6 Giant covalent structures
- Diamond: each carbon covalently bonded to four carbons in a three-dimensional giant lattice; many strong covalent bonds make it hard and suitable for cutting tools.
- Graphite: each carbon bonded to three carbons in layers; weak forces between layers permit sliding (lubricant); one electron per carbon is delocalised and can carry charge (electrode).
- Silicon(IV) oxide: giant three-dimensional covalent network with silicon bonded to oxygen and oxygen bridging silicon; like diamond, strong covalent bonds throughout give high melting point/hardness.

2.7 Metallic bonding
- Metallic bond: electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions in a giant lattice and delocalised electrons.
- Delocalised electrons MUST be stated to be mobile/able to move through metal to explain electrical conduction; merely saying “delocalised electrons” or “electrons carry charge” without mobility is insufficient in the newer scheme.
- Alloys can be harder than pure metals because differently sized atoms/ions disrupt the layers, so the layers cannot slide as easily; merely saying atoms cannot slide, or calling an alloy a compound, is insufficient in the newer scheme.
- Metal layers can slide while attraction to the delocalised electrons remains, explaining malleability and ductility.

3 STOICHIOMETRY

3.1 Formulae and equations
- Molecular formula states actual numbers/types of atoms in one molecule; empirical formula gives simplest whole-number ratio of atoms or ions.
- Determine formula from particle models by counting each type and, for an empirical formula, reducing the ratio.
- In a state-symbol ionic-equation question, correct balancing and correct state symbols may be separate marks; for example Ni(s) + 2H+(aq) → Ni2+(aq) + H2(g), with state-symbol credit dependent on correct formulae.
- For KO2, the ion paired with K+ is O2− (superoxide), not KO2−; keep the entire O2 group when writing its charge.
- For a compound containing K+, Cr3+ and SO4 2−, one K+ and one Cr3+ give total +4 so two sulfates give KCr(SO4)2.
- Example: aluminium sulfate has Al3+ and SO4 2− in a 2:3 ratio, Al2(SO4)3; its empirical atom-count formula can also be written Al2S3O12.
- For ionic formulae, balance positive and negative ion charges to give zero overall charge; use brackets for more than one polyatomic ion.
- Write word equations with correct reactant/product names; balance symbol equations by changing coefficients, not formula subscripts.
- State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous; include them when requested.
- Ionic equations omit unchanged spectator ions, retain charges, and balance atoms and charge on both sides.

3.2 Relative masses
- Relative atomic mass is the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element relative to 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
- Relative molecular mass / relative formula mass is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in the formula. If X has average mass twice a carbon-12 atom, Ar(X)=24; if Y equals carbon-12, Ar(Y)=12; if Z is 4/3 of Y, Ar(Z)=16. Formula XYZ3 has Mr = 24+12+3(16)=84.

- For 13.6 g NaN3 (Mr 65), calculate 13.6/65 = 0.20923 mol; use the 1:1.5 NaN3:N2 mole ratio to get 0.31385 mol N2; multiply by 24 dm3 mol−1 to get 7.53 dm3 (three significant figures).
- For 3.24 g Al with 50 cm3 of 2.50 mol dm−3 H2SO4: Al = 3.24/27 = 0.120 mol, requiring 0.180 mol H2SO4; available acid = 0.050 × 2.50 = 0.125 mol. Therefore acid is limiting and aluminium is in excess.
- For 13.0 g KO2 (Mr 71), calculate 13/71 = 0.18310 mol; use the 1:0.75 KO2:O2 mole ratio to get 0.13732 mol O2; multiply by 24 to obtain 3.30 dm3 (three significant figures).
- Hydrated magnesium sulfate calculation: 250 cm3 × 0.50 mol dm−3 = 0.125 mol Mg2+; therefore 0.125 mol MgSO4·7H2O. Its Mr is 246, so required mass = 30.75 g.
- At room temperature and pressure, one mole of gas occupies 24 dm3 = 24 000 cm3; amount = gas volume / molar gas volume.
- Concentration (mol dm−3) = amount (mol) / volume (dm3); concentration (g dm−3) = mass (g) / volume (dm3).
- Convert cm3 to dm3 by dividing by 1000; multiply dm3 by 1000 to obtain cm3.
- For H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl, 10 cm3 H2 uses 10 cm3 Cl2 and makes 20 cm3 HCl; if final gas volume is 40 cm3, unreacted Cl2 is 20 cm3 and initial Cl2 was 30 cm3.
- For 100 cm3 C2H4 + 400 cm3 O2, complete combustion consumes 300 cm3 O2 and makes 200 cm3 CO2; gaseous products plus excess O2 occupy 300 cm3 at r.t.p. (water is liquid).
- From 258 g hexane (Mr 86), 3.00 mol yields 3.00 mol cyclohexene (Mr 82), or 246 g, in C6H14 → C6H10 + 2H2; the alternative dehydrogenation C6H14 → C6H12 + H2 yields 252 g.
- For mixed gas reactants, first identify limiting reagent, then subtract consumed gas and exclude liquid product from final gas volume: 2 g H2 (1 mol) + 32 g O2 (1 mol) leaves 0.5 mol O2 and produces liquid water; final gas volume is one-quarter of initial two-mole gas volume.
- Use balanced equation coefficients as mole ratios; calculate amounts from given data, identify the limiting reactant, then calculate required product/reactant amount. Example: 3.24 g Zn / 65 = 0.04985 mol; it needs 0.0997 mol HCl for Zn + 2HCl, but 50 cm3 of 1.50 mol dm−3 HCl supplies only 0.075 mol, so HCl is limiting and zinc is in excess.
- Example of separately marked gas-volume calculation: 230 cm3 CO2 = 230/24000 = 0.00958 mol; in 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2, amount NaHCO3 = 2 × 0.00958 = 0.0192 mol; mass = 1.6 g (to two significant figures).
- Example: for 2.2% H and 34.5% O in nickel compound, calculate 63.3% Ni first; respective mole amounts about 2.2, 2.16, 1.07 give 1:2:2 and empirical formula NiH2O2.
- Example of separately marked empirical formula: for 34.5% Ni, 28.1% C and 37.4% O, calculate mole ratio 1:4:4 and formula NiC4O4; initial percentage, mole conversion and final formula may earn separate marks.
- Ethanoic-acid titration example: 25.0 cm3 of 0.0100 mol dm−3 Ca(OH)2 = 0.000250 mol; 2CH3COOH per base gives 0.000500 mol acid; in 12.5 cm3 acid, concentration = 0.0400 mol dm−3.
- Carbonate titration example: 20.0 cm3 of 0.0250 mol dm−3 Na2CO3 = 0.000500 mol; two HCl per carbonate gives 0.00100 mol HCl; in 15.5 cm3 acid, concentration = 0.0645 mol dm−3.
- Another titration example: 25.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 NaOH contains 0.00250 mol; H2SO4 + 2NaOH requires 0.00125 mol H2SO4, so 16.2 cm3 acid has concentration 0.00125/0.0162 = 0.0772 mol dm−3.
- Titration example: 25.0 cm3 of 0.050 mol dm−3 NaOH contains 0.00125 mol; for 3NaOH + H3PO4 → Na3PO4 + 3H2O, H3PO4 = 0.00125/3 mol; in 20.0 cm3, concentration = 0.0208 mol dm−3.
- For soluble ammonium chloride from ammonia and HCl, first titrate with indicator to find exact neutralising volumes, repeat without indicator, concentrate to crystallisation point, then cool/filter/dry.
- Titration: use mean concordant titre, convert volume to dm3, calculate moles from known concentration, then apply equation ratio to unknown concentration.
- Empirical formula: convert masses or mass percentages to moles using atomic masses, divide by smallest mole amount, and convert to whole-number ratio.
- Molecular formula: find empirical formula mass, divide given molecular mass by it, multiply every empirical subscript by that factor.
- For a NaHCO3/NaCl heated mixture, mass loss is CO2 plus H2O: 2NaHCO3 (168 g) loses 62 g; 1.5 g loss implies (1.5/62)×168 ≈ 4.06 g NaHCO3 in 6.0 g mixture, i.e. about 68% by mass.
- For an ore-purity MCQ, use mass lost as gaseous product: 3.00 g sample leaves 2.20 g, so CO2 = 0.80 g; 0.80/44 mol CuCO3 corresponds to 2.25 g pure CuCO3, giving 75.2% purity.
- With 10.7 g NH4Cl (0.200 mol) and 14.8 g Ca(OH)2 (0.200 mol), 2NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 gives 0.100 mol CaCl2 (theoretical 11.1 g) because NH4Cl limits; 10.0 g actual gives 90.1% yield.
- From CaO + 2HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + H2O, 8.00 g CaO yields theoretically (8/56)×164 = 23.43 g Ca(NO3)2; 12.65 g actual means 54.0% yield.
- A 5.00 g portion C2H5Br (Mr 109) could make (5/109)×46=2.11 g ethanol; 1.59 g actual is about 75% yield.
- For N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3, 60 kg hydrogen could theoretically produce (60/6)×34=340 kg ammonia; 60 kg actual is 17.6% yield.
- For Al2O3 → 2Al, 102 tonnes oxide can give 54 tonnes Al; 27 tonnes Al therefore require 51 tonnes oxide at 100% yield.
- For 10 cm3 of 1.0 mol dm−3 H2O2, 2H2O2 → O2 gives maximum 0.0050 mol O2 = 120 cm3 at RTP; 90 cm3 actual gives 75% yield.
- For 0.0600 mol HCl in 2HCl + Ba(OH)2 → BaCl2 + 2H2O, maximum BaCl2 is 0.0300 mol ×208 = 6.24 g; at 75% yield mass is 4.68 g. For Ag2O + 2HNO3 → 2AgNO3 + H2O, 30.0 cm3 of 0.150 mol dm−3 HNO3 gives 0.00450 mol AgNO3 ×170 ×0.80=0.612 g.
- For 20.0 cm3 of 0.550 mol dm−3 Ba(NO3)2 with excess sulfate, maximum BaSO4 is 0.0110 mol ×233 = 2.563 g; compare actual mass against this to obtain the yield. For 20.0 cm3 of 0.650 mol dm−3 H2SO4 neutralised to K2SO4, maximum 0.0130 mol ×174=2.262 g; an actual yield near 76.1% corresponds to about 1.72 g.
- Mix 50.0 cm3 of 0.10 mol dm−3 AgNO3 (0.0050 mol) with 150.0 cm3 of 0.05 mol dm−3 NaCl (0.0075 mol): AgNO3 is limiting, leaving AgCl(s), excess dissolved NaCl and dissolved NaNO3.
- For BaSO4 precipitated from 25.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol dm−3 Na2SO4, maximum 0.0250 mol × 233 = 5.825 g; an actual mass of 5.36 g means yield ≈92%.
- Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2: 14.4 g Fe2O3 is 0.0900 mol, makes 0.180 mol Fe = 10.1 g and 0.270 mol CO2 = 6.48 dm3 at RTP. Conversely 3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2: 39.2 g Fe = 0.700 mol, giving 0.233 mol Fe3O4 = 54.1 g and 0.933 mol H2 = 22.4 dm3.
- Fe2O3 has Mr 160 and contains 112 parts iron by mass: 500 kg pure haematite can yield at most 500×112/160 = 350 kg Fe.
- For Fe2O3 → 2Fe, 80 tonnes oxide (Mr 160) yields at most 80×112/160=56 tonnes Fe; 50 tonnes actual gives 89% yield.
- Percentage yield example: 100 cm3 of 1.00 mol dm−3 KOH is 0.100 mol; it produces 0.0500 mol K2SO4 (Mr 174), theoretical 8.70 g; 3.48 g actual = 40% yield.
- Percentage yield example: 8.00 g CaO gives (8/56)×164 = 23.43 g theoretical Ca(NO3)2; 12.65 g actual gives 12.65/23.43×100 = 54.0%.
- Percentage yield = actual yield / theoretical yield × 100; percentage purity = mass of pure substance / sample mass × 100; percentage composition = mass of specified element in formula / formula mass × 100.
- Show intermediate amounts, stoichiometric ratios and final units; distinguish percentage yield from percentage purity.

4 ELECTROCHEMISTRY

4.1 Electrolysis
- Electrolysis is decomposition of an ionic compound molten or dissolved in water by passing an electric current.
- Anode is positive, cathode negative, electrolyte is molten or aqueous ionic substance; cations move to cathode, anions to anode.
- Reduction/gain of electrons occurs at cathode; oxidation/loss of electrons occurs at anode; electrons travel through external circuit while ions carry charge through electrolyte.
- Concentrated aqueous copper(II) bromide with graphite electrodes gives bromine at the anode and copper at the cathode. Graphite conductivity explanation can earn separate points for each carbon using three valence electrons to form covalent bonds and the remaining electrons being mobile between layers; graphite is inert as an electrode.
- Molten lead(II) bromide: lead(II) ions form lead at cathode (Pb2+ + 2e− → Pb); bromide ions form bromine at anode (2Br− → Br2 + 2e−); brown bromine and silvery/grey lead may be observed.
- Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride with inert electrodes: hydrogen at cathode (bubbles), chlorine at anode (bubbles); sodium hydroxide remains in solution.
- Dilute sulfuric acid with inert electrodes: 4OH− → O2 + 2H2O + 4e− at anode and 2H+ + 2e− → H2 at cathode; hydrogen at cathode and oxygen at anode; anode:cathode volumes 1:2 (oxygen:hydrogen). Concentrated aqueous NaCl gives anode:cathode chlorine:hydrogen volumes 1:1.
- Aqueous copper(II) sulfate with inert electrodes: copper deposits as a pink/brown solid at cathode; oxygen bubbles at anode; blue colour fades as Cu2+ is removed.
- In a four-observation table, with copper electrodes the anode gets smaller and the cathode gets larger/coated pink; with graphite electrodes the anode bubbles/fizzes and the cathode becomes coated in pink/brown solid. These are four separate observations, not merely product names.
- Aqueous copper(II) sulfate with copper electrodes: copper anode dissolves (Cu → Cu2+ + 2e−), copper deposits at cathode (Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu); electrolyte concentration/blue colour stays approximately unchanged.
- Solid ionic compound does not conduct because ions cannot move; molten/aqueous ions can move.
- Dilute aqueous CaBr2 yields oxygen (and water) at anode and hydrogen at cathode, not bromine or calcium metal.
- For concentrated aqueous KI the anode product is iodine; in dilute aqueous KI it is oxygen. Both have hydrogen at the cathode (four independently awarded entries). Separately, in molten potassium iodide the half-equations are 2I− → I2 + 2e− and K+ + e− → K; do not confuse the molten cathode product with the aqueous one.
- In molten barium iodide, for example, 2I− → I2 + 2e− at the anode and Ba2+ + 2e− → Ba at the cathode. Inert electrodes do not react with electrolyte or products and conduct electricity well; platinum is an example. In an explanation, “less reactive” alone is insufficient to establish inertness.
- Molten binary salt: metal at cathode and non-metal at anode; aqueous halides: concentrated chloride/bromide/iodide can discharge halogen at anode, while dilute solution may yield oxygen instead.
- Explicit aqueous potassium iodide comparison: concentrated solution gives iodine at anode and hydrogen at cathode; dilute solution gives oxygen at anode and hydrogen at cathode.
- Explicit aqueous zinc chloride comparison with carbon electrodes: dilute solution gives oxygen at anode and hydrogen at cathode; concentrated solution gives chlorine at anode and hydrogen at cathode.
- Explicit aqueous sodium chloride comparison: dilute NaCl gives oxygen at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode; concentrated NaCl gives chlorine at the anode and hydrogen at the cathode. The two anode products and two cathode products can be marked separately.
- Half-equations must balance both atoms and charges, and show electrons on correct side (cathode reactant, anode product).
- Electron-transfer examples separately credited in a theory paper: K → K+ + e−; Li → Li+ + e−; F2 + 2e− → 2F−; Br2 + 2e− → 2Br−.
- Electroplating silver: statue/object is the cathode in a solution such as aqueous AgNO3 (soluble silver salt), not an anode or an insoluble AgCl solution. In general, electroplating: object to coat is cathode, plating metal is anode, electrolyte contains ions of plating metal; metal ions are reduced onto object; used to improve appearance/corrosion resistance.

4.2 Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells
- In a simple galvanic cell, external electrons travel from the more reactive metal electrode to the less reactive: arrows X→Y and Y→Z mean X>Y>Z, exemplified by Zn>Cu>Ag.
- Hydrogen and oxygen react to generate electricity; water is the only chemical product; overall equation 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O.
- Vehicle advantage: no carbon dioxide at point of use and no carbon monoxide from fuel cell; compare with petrol combustion.
- Vehicle disadvantages credited in the newer scheme include difficulty storing hydrogen under pressure and limited availability/few filling stations; a fuel cell may have shorter life or lower efficiency at low temperature. Do NOT rely on hydrogen being flammable/explosive, expensive or unsafe: the 2026 scheme ignores these for its comparison question.

6.4 REDOX
- Oxidation number is indicated with Roman numerals in compound names where needed, e.g. iron(III).
- Redox means oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.
- For iodide reacting with copper(II) ions, say iodide ions lose electrons (or iodine oxidation number increases) and copper(II) ions gain electrons (or copper oxidation number decreases); “iodine loses electrons” and “copper gains electrons” do not identify the correct species in the newer scheme.
- When describing a specific redox reaction, name the species AND what happens: magnesium atoms lose electrons (oxidation) while copper(II) ions gain electrons (reduction); a half-equation alone is insufficient in the newer explanation scheme.
- Oxidation: gain of oxygen, loss of electrons, or increase in oxidation number.
- Reduction: loss of oxygen, gain of electrons, or decrease in oxidation number.
- Oxidising agent oxidises another species and is itself reduced; reducing agent reduces another species and is itself oxidised.
- In 2I− + Cl2 → I2 + 2Cl−, iodide loses electrons (oxidised) and chlorine gains electrons (reduced); in 2Br− + Cl2 → Br2 + 2Cl− bromide likewise loses electrons. An answer merely naming oxidation/reduction without species and electron change can miss separate marks.
- For a halogen molecule such as Cl2 or Br2 oxidation number is 0; for its halide Cl− or Br− it is −1, so forming halide involves gaining electrons (reduction).
- Uncombined element has oxidation number 0; a monatomic ion's oxidation number equals its charge; oxidation numbers sum to 0 in neutral compound and to ion charge in polyatomic ion.
- Identify species that increase/decrease oxidation number and therefore which are oxidised/reduced and which are reducing/oxidising agents.
- Acidified potassium manganate(VII) changes purple to colourless when reduced; iodide ions can be oxidised to iodine, producing a brown solution. Hydrogen peroxide can act as a REDUCING agent against acidified manganate(VII) and as an OXIDISING agent against iodide; these are two separate marking points in the same question.

7 ACIDS, BASES AND SALTS

7.1 Characteristic properties
- Aqueous acids produce H+ ions; aqueous alkalis produce OH− ions; acid is proton donor, base is proton acceptor. When explaining acid reaction with water, explicitly state proton transfer from acid to water; for a weak acid also state that the reaction does not go to completion/only partial dissociation.
- Alkali is soluble base; metal oxides and hydroxides can act as bases.
- CuO + 2CH3COOH → (CH3COO)2Cu + H2O is an example of acid + insoluble base; a salt containing Cu2+ needs TWO ethanoate ions.
- Acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen; acid + base → salt + water; acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
- Base + ammonium salt on warming → ammonia (with water and a salt); test ammonia with damp red litmus turning blue.
- Neutralisation ionic equation: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l).
- Acid turns blue litmus red; alkali turns red litmus blue. Methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in alkali; thymolphthalein is colourless in acid and blue in alkali. During acid-in-flask titration with alkali from burette, methyl orange changes red toward yellow and thymolphthalein changes colourless toward blue (not the reverse).
- Strong acid dissociates completely in water; weak acid only partially dissociates. Use one-way arrow for complete dissociation and reversible arrow for partial dissociation.
- Examples of strong acids: HCl, HNO3 and H2SO4; weak acid example: CH3COOH ⇌ H+ + CH3COO−.
- Universal indicator and pH: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline; lower pH corresponds to greater H+ concentration/greater acidity; higher pH to greater alkalinity.

7.2 Oxides
- Acidic oxides include CO2 and SO2 (non-metal oxides); basic oxides include CaO and CuO (metal oxides).
- Amphoteric Al2O3 and ZnO react with both acids and bases to form a salt and water.

7.3 Preparation of salts
- Soluble salt from acid + alkali: use titration to find neutralising volumes, repeat without indicator, then evaporate and crystallise.
- Soluble salt from acid + excess insoluble metal/base/carbonate: add excess to consume acid, filter away excess solid, gently evaporate filtrate, cool to crystallise, filter and dry crystals.
- For a question with three preparation marks, ensure the drying method is explicit: filtration, washing and drying with filter paper, a desiccator or a suitable oven after washing can count; simply saying “leave to dry” may not.
- To crystallise from an existing solution: warm/evaporate until saturated or crystals first appear; cool or leave to evaporate slowly; filter off the crystals, wash and specify a drying method (e.g. filter paper). Do not heat solution to dryness.
- For BaSO4 from BaCO3, dissolve the carbonate in dilute HCl first, precipitate sulfate by adding dilute H2SO4, then filter and WASH the insoluble precipitate; do not attempt to crystallise an insoluble salt.
- Insoluble salt: mix suitable soluble salt solutions to precipitate product, then filter, wash and dry precipitate.
- Sodium, potassium, ammonium salts and nitrates are soluble.
- Chlorides are soluble except silver and lead chlorides; sulfates soluble except barium, lead and calcium sulfates.
- Carbonates are insoluble except sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates; hydroxides are insoluble except sodium, potassium and ammonium hydroxides, with calcium hydroxide partially soluble.
- Fractional distillation can separate liquid air into nitrogen and oxygen; filtration only separates insoluble solid from a liquid, not two solids or two gases.
- To separate a soluble salt from an insoluble solid, add water, stir and filter; crystallise the soluble salt from the filtrate. Do not heat a hydrated salt until all water is driven off if hydrated crystals are required.
- A temperature range during distillation rather than a single sharp boiling point supports that the collected liquid is a mixture; do not infer specific boiling points of its components from the range alone.
- To identify a metal sulfate by precipitate, use soluble barium salt with sulfate (insoluble BaSO4); a chloride can form insoluble PbCl2 with lead(II) nitrate, while nitrates and Group I salts are soluble.
- To test for water, anhydrous CuSO4 turns white to blue, whereas anhydrous CoCl2 turns blue to pink. A salt solution can boil above 100 °C at 1 atm, unlike pure water.
- Hydrated crystals contain water chemically combined; anhydrous substance contains no water; water of crystallisation is water incorporated into crystal structure, e.g. CuSO4·5H2O and CoCl2·6H2O.

8 PERIODIC TABLE

8.1 Arrangement
- An older historical Group 1 comparison table places Cu and Ag with alkalis; do not infer these metals are modern Group 1. In modern chemistry Group 0/VIII noble gases were absent from the earliest Mendeleev table.
- Periodic Table is arranged by increasing proton number; horizontal rows are periods, vertical columns are groups.
- Metallic character generally decreases and non-metallic character increases across a period.
- Group number indicates outer electrons for Groups I–VII, and these electrons explain similar group chemistry; period number gives occupied shells.
- Common ion charges: Group I +1, Group II +2, Group III +3, Group V −3, Group VI −2, Group VII −1; ions form through electron loss/gain to obtain full outer shell.
- Use group position and trends to predict properties of unfamiliar elements, distinguishing an observed trend from an extrapolation.

8.2 Group I
- Lithium, sodium and potassium are relatively soft alkali metals; going down the group melting points decrease, densities generally increase and reactivity increases.
- Reactivity increases down Group I because outer electron is farther from nucleus/more shielded and easier to lose.

8.3 Group VII
- Chlorine, bromine and iodine are diatomic non-metals; down the group density increases and reactivity decreases.
- Chlorine: pale yellow-green gas; bromine: red-brown liquid; iodine: grey-black solid at room temperature and pressure.
- When chlorine is added to aqueous iodide, iodine forms and the initially colourless solution goes brown; chlorine with bromide gives bromine and a lithium/potassium chloride solution where applicable.
- More reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from aqueous solution; e.g. chlorine displaces bromide/iodide, bromine displaces iodide, iodine displaces neither chloride nor bromide.
- Halogen reactivity decreases down group because increased electron-shell distance and shielding make electron gain harder.

8.4 Transition elements
- In an exam asking for two additional physical properties of nickel after conductivity, malleability and ductility have already been stated, choose high melting/boiling point and high density rather than repeating the given properties.
- A transition metal may have more than one oxidation state in its compounds; nickel catalyses addition of hydrogen to an alkene. State that it forms coloured compounds, not just that the metal itself is coloured or that it forms coloured solutions.
- Transition elements have high densities and high melting points; variable oxidation numbers, coloured compounds, and frequent catalytic activity as elements or compounds.

8.5 Noble gases
- Noble gases are unreactive and monatomic because they have full outer electron shells and therefore little tendency to gain, lose or share electrons.

9 METALS

9.1 Properties
- Metals typically conduct heat and electricity, are malleable and ductile, and have generally higher melting/boiling points than non-metals; recognise exceptions when interpreting data.
- Metals react with dilute acids to form salt and hydrogen when above hydrogen in reactivity series; may react with cold water or steam to form hydroxide/oxide and hydrogen; react with oxygen to form oxides.

9.2 Uses
- In a two-mark use-and-property answer, name the use AND link it to the correct property; “lightweight” alone is insufficient where the scheme asks for low density.
- Aluminium aircraft: low density; aluminium overhead cables: low density and good electrical conductivity; aluminium food containers: corrosion resistance; copper wiring: high conductivity and ductility.

9.4 Reactivity series and extraction
- For Cu + 2Ag+ → Cu2+ + 2Ag, copper dissolves and silver crystals accumulate while aqueous copper(II) turns solution blue; TWO positive silver ions are replaced by ONE copper(II) ion, so total positive-ion count need not remain unchanged.
- Air trapped with moist iron filings loses oxygen as iron rusts: from 100 cm3 air the volume falls towards about 80 cm3 and levels off, rather than reaching zero.
- Memorise order: potassium > sodium > calcium > magnesium > aluminium > carbon > zinc > iron > hydrogen > copper > silver > gold.
- In preparations using excess metal, its purpose is to use up all the acid; remove leftover metal by filtration (not evaporation or distillation) before crystallising the filtrate.
- More reactive metal forms positive ions more readily and displaces a less reactive metal from its aqueous salt; no displacement when the solid metal is less reactive.
- Potassium/sodium/calcium react with cold water forming metal hydroxide and hydrogen; magnesium reacts with steam forming magnesium oxide and hydrogen.
- Magnesium, zinc and iron react with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce metal chlorides and hydrogen (rate varies with reactivity); copper, silver and gold do not displace hydrogen from dilute HCl.
- Aluminium seems unreactive because it has an oxide layer that is non-porous/impermeable to water and oxygen; in a two-mark explanation identify both the layer and its barrier action. Merely calling it a physical barrier is insufficient without naming what is excluded.
- Derive an order from experimental displacement or reaction-rate observations rather than assuming it from appearance alone.
- Cryolite can also increase the electrical conductivity of the electrolyte; do not claim that it lowers the melting point of aluminium itself.
- Aluminium extraction: dissolve aluminium oxide in molten cryolite to lower operating temperature/energy demand; Al3+ + 3e− → Al at cathode; oxide ions lose electrons at anode: 2O2− → O2 + 4e−.
- Carbon anodes need replacement because oxygen formed reacts with hot carbon, forming carbon dioxide.

12 EXPERIMENTAL TECHNIQUES AND CHEMICAL ANALYSIS

12.1 Experimental design
- Measure time with stopwatch, temperature with thermometer, mass with balance, liquid volume with measuring cylinder/pipette/burette and gas volume with gas syringe.
- Volumetric pipette accurately measures a fixed volume; burette accurately delivers and measures variable volume; measuring cylinder is less precise but convenient.
- Compare methods by accuracy, precision, sensitivity, suitability for required volume, and possible loss of gas, heat or material.
- Solvent dissolves solute; solute is dissolved; solution is homogeneous mixture of dissolved solute(s) and solvent.
- If excess solid glucose remains after repeated stirring in ethanol, glucose is the solute and the solution is saturated (at that temperature), not ethanol as the solute.
- Saturated solution holds the maximum dissolved solute at specified temperature; residue remains after separation; filtrate passes through the filter.

12.2 Acid–base titrations
- Measure a fixed volume into flask with volumetric pipette; add a suitable indicator; fill/read burette with other solution; add while swirling until indicator changes at endpoint.
- Read burette at eye level; titre = final − initial reading; repeat to obtain concordant titres and calculate their mean.

12.3 Chromatography
- Draw pencil baseline; spot samples on line; place paper in suitable solvent with baseline above solvent surface; solvent moves up and separates dissolved substances.
- Use locating agent to reveal colourless spots; match unknowns with standards by spots at same positions under same conditions.
- If two dye spots coincide, try a DIFFERENT solvent to resolve them; measuring Rf, using a locating agent for already-coloured dyes, or merely running longer does not necessarily separate overlapping spots.
- A pure sample gives one spot (in a suitable solvent); a mixture gives more than one spot; identical Rf can support identification when conditions match.
- For a chromatogram, calculate EACH spot’s Rf against the SAME solvent front, then match to reference values; a colourless three-spot sample may be located with a locating agent before measurement.
- Rf = distance from baseline to centre of spot / distance from baseline to solvent front; both distances in same units, Rf has no unit and cannot exceed 1. The same substance can give different Rf values in different solvents; compare standards under the same conditions.

12.4 Separation and purification
- Select solvent dissolving one component but not another; filter to separate insoluble solid from liquid.
- Crystallisation: concentrate solution by gentle evaporation, allow to cool, filter crystals and dry; do not simply heat to dryness when crystals are required.
- Simple distillation separates solvent from solution or liquids with widely separated boiling points; vapour is condensed and collected.
- Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with closer boiling points using fractionating column and differences in boiling point.
- A sample of water with a boiling point above 100 °C at standard pressure may be identified as impure (provided pressure is appropriate).
- Pure substance melts/boils at a sharp characteristic temperature; impurities generally lower and broaden melting range and alter boiling behaviour.

12.5 Identification of ions and gases
- Carbonate: add dilute acid; carbon dioxide produced turns limewater milky.
- In precipitation questions, describe both initial precipitate and behaviour in excess reagent: Cu2+ with aqueous ammonia gives pale blue precipitate, dissolving in excess to dark blue solution; Zn2+ with aqueous ammonia gives white precipitate, dissolving in excess to colourless solution.
- Chloride/bromide/iodide: acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate: chloride white precipitate, bromide cream precipitate, iodide yellow precipitate.
- Nitrate: warm with aluminium foil and aqueous sodium hydroxide; ammonia evolved turns damp red litmus blue. To distinguish nitrate from ammonium, test on warming with alkali before adding aluminium: if no ammonia appears until aluminium is added, nitrate is indicated rather than ammonium.
- Sulfate: acidify with dilute nitric acid, then aqueous barium nitrate gives white precipitate.
- Sulfite: add acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII); purple colour is decolourised.
- Combined ion-test example: a green Fe2+ precipitate with NaOH, no gas on warming before aluminium, then ammonia after adding aluminium indicates Fe2+ and NO3−. A white precipitate dissolving in excess NaOH followed by ammonia after aluminium can indicate Al3+ and NO3−.
- With aqueous NaOH: Al3+ white precipitate soluble in excess; Ca2+ white precipitate insoluble in excess; Cr3+ grey-green precipitate soluble in excess; Cu2+ light blue precipitate insoluble in excess; Fe2+ green precipitate insoluble in excess (turns brown on standing); Fe3+ red-brown precipitate insoluble in excess; Zn2+ white precipitate soluble in excess.
- With aqueous ammonia: Al3+ white precipitate insoluble in excess; Ca2+ generally no precipitate; Cr3+ grey-green precipitate insoluble in excess; Cu2+ light blue precipitate dissolves in excess to give dark blue solution; Fe2+ green precipitate insoluble; Fe3+ red-brown precipitate insoluble; Zn2+ white precipitate dissolves in excess to give colourless solution.
- Ammonium: warm with aqueous sodium hydroxide; ammonia turns damp red litmus blue.
- Acidic CO2 from carbonate turns damp blue litmus red; basic NH3 from warmed ammonium salt turns damp red litmus blue (blue litmus remains blue).
- Gas tests: ammonia turns damp red litmus blue; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; chlorine bleaches damp litmus (may turn it red first); hydrogen gives squeaky pop with lit splint; oxygen relights glowing splint; sulfur dioxide decolourises acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII).
- Flame colours: Li+ crimson red; Na+ yellow; K+ lilac; Ca2+ orange-red/brick red; Ba2+ apple green; Cu2+ blue-green.

Rates of reaction and thermal decomposition
- On a gas-volume-versus-time decomposition curve, the first horizontal point marks completion; a hotter flame using the same initial carbonate quantity gives a curve shifted left that begins at the origin and reaches the SAME final gas volume, never above it. Organic chemistry and energy
- An ethene dot-and-cross diagram needs TWO shared electron pairs in C=C and ONE in each of four C–H bonds. Hydrate ethene with steam over phosphoric acid catalyst at roughly 200–400 °C and 30–100 atmospheres; balance C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH.

Ionic and metallic structure
- An isotope of an element has the same proton and neutral-atom electron counts but a different neutron count; a Group I atom loses one electron to form +1 ion while oxygen gains two to form oxide. Ionic solids have high melting points, fail to conduct when solid but conduct when molten, and may be soluble in water.
- Metallic conduction requires regular layers of positive ions and interspersed mobile/delocalised electrons; in a diagram show at least two layers of ions and label electrons.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — BONDING AND ESTER CONTRASTS
- A room-temperature WATER-INSOLUBLE solid should be removed from liquid water by filtration even if it has a relatively low melting point (e.g. 53°C); do not choose distillation merely because its boiling point resembles water's. In MgO higher 2+/2− charges produce stronger ionic attraction and higher melting point than 1+/1− NaCl.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — FERTILISER, CARBONATES AND METAL IONS
- Calcium hydroxide liming raises acidic soil pH, ammonium sulfate supplies N fertiliser; do NOT mix simultaneously: Ca(OH)₂ + (NH₄)₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O, volatilising nitrogen as ammonia. ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — HISTORIC PARTICLE AND REACTIVITY CROSS-CHECK
- A sodium chloride SOLUTION boils above pure water's boiling temperature at the same pressure; solid ionic lattice does not conduct, dissolved/molten ions do; metallic copper conducts via mobile electrons among positive ions. In an electric field, electrons deflect to POSITIVE plate and protons to NEGATIVE, neutrons not at all.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — NOBLE GASES, HYDRATES, NITROGEN CYCLE
- Noble gases have complete outer shells, but He has TWO rather than eight outer electrons; Xe can form XeF₄ with fluorine under specified 400°C conditions. Relative atomic mass does NOT determine element ordering when isotope averages cause inversion; atomic/proton number does. He- and Ne-filled balloons float above denser Ar, and Kr below Ar in the same diagram.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — DIFFUSION, ALKENES, GAS VOLUMES
- When heated ammonium chloride produces NH₃ and HCl, lighter NH₃ diffuses faster through a porous plug: damp red litmus turns blue first, then red as HCl arrives. Do not mistake this for bleaching.
- For complete combustion with gaseous water retained, total gaseous product volume per hydrocarbon volume is number of CO₂ molecules PLUS water-vapour molecules in balanced equation: propane gives 3+4=7 volumes.
- Different positions of the double bond in four-carbon alkenes yield but-1-ene and but-2-ene, same C₄H₈ formula; distinguish both from saturated branched butane and four-carbon dienes.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CARBON STRUCTURES, CELLS, GLASS
- Carbon nanotube: hexagonal three-coordinate C and mobile delocalised electrons; diamond: four-coordinate tetrahedral giant network with no mobile electrons. Both have strong covalent bonds within their structures; graphite also conducts. Buckminsterfullerene is a cage-like molecular allotrope.
- In alkaline hydrogen fuel cells, anode H₂+2OH⁻→2H₂O+2e⁻ and cathode O₂+2H₂O+4e⁻→4OH⁻. In acidic cells, anode H₂→2H⁺+2e⁻ and cathode O₂+4H⁺+4e⁻→2H₂O. Either gives overall 2H₂+O₂→2H₂O. Distinguish oxidation at anode from the location of hydrogen in a simple cell.
- In soda-lime glass, sodium/calcium ions disrupt the regular open silicate network and some oxygens attach covalently to only one silicon. Molten glass conducts because Na⁺/Ca²⁺ ions MOVE; a solid covalent silicate lacks this mobile ionic mechanism. Lead ions with hydroxide form white Pb(OH)₂, Pb²⁺+2OH⁻→Pb(OH)₂.
- When comparing acid and metal quantities, calculate BOTH moles and apply balanced-equation mole ratio before declaring excess. For equal masses magnesium produces more H₂ than heavier zinc if acid is in excess. In gas-volume rate experiments distinguish collecting CO₂ volume at timed intervals from simply using a stopwatch.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — SOLUTIONS AND POLYESTER
- Extra dilute reactant solution added to a reaction can both lower initial concentration/rate and INCREASE final product amount if additional reactant moles have been introduced; catalyst/temperature alone changes rate but not amount from fixed reactant moles.
- A dicarboxylic acid HOOC–R–COOH plus diol HO–R′–OH gives polyester repeat –CO–R–CO–O–R′–O–; both carbonyl C atoms from acid and both ester O atoms adjoining diol segment must remain.
- Oxidation state of sulfur in SO₂ is +4; in elemental sulfur zero. When SO₂ reacts with H₂S to give sulfur, SO₂ is reduced and is the OXIDISING agent. Acid + ammonia to ammonium salt is neutralisation without changing oxidation states.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CONCRETE, BAUXITE, ISOTOPES
- CaCO₃ decomposes to BASIC CaO, which hydrates to Ca(OH)₂. In porous/cracked concrete atmospheric CO₂ neutralises Ca(OH)₂ especially near surface or crack, lowering surface pH; deeper material has less CO₂ contact and higher pH.
- Aqueous NaOH dissolves amphoteric Al₂O₃ but not basic Fe₂O₃, so separate by filtration/settling. Cryolite dissolves alumina and lowers the mixture's operating/melting temperature; protective Al₂O₃ layer explains aluminium's corrosion resistance, not absence of aluminium reactivity.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ISOTOPES, RING COMPOUNDS
- With protium ¹H and deuterium ²H, H₂O molecules can weigh 18, 19 or 20 relative mass units depending on the two hydrogen isotopes; mean atomic mass does not preclude mixed molecules.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — AIR, OZONE, GLASS-FURNACE REACTIONS
- Air approximates 79% nitrogen and 20% oxygen in one historical scheme. Argon protects reactive sodium during titanium production by EXCLUDING oxygen, not merely by being unreactive. Xenon compound empirical formulas require dividing each element's mass by its relative atomic mass before finding integer ratio.
- Ozone absorbs UV radiation, limiting skin cancer/cataracts. 2O₃→3O₂. A CFC-production time-series may not track ozone loss/recovery instantly: consider dated peak and decline and distinguish measured ozone fluctuations from CFC trend.
- Blast-furnace limestone CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂; basic CaO reacts SiO₂→CaSiO₃ slag, preventing impurities clogging furnace. Fe₂O₃+3CO→2Fe+3CO₂ at upper/central furnace; Fe₂O₃+3C→2Fe+3CO near bottom. Blow O₂ through molten pig iron to REMOVE excess C for mild steel.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — APPARATUS AND ALKENE IDENTITY
- In a porous-pot U-tube, gas diffusing IN faster than enclosed gas diffuses OUT raises pot pressure, pushing the water down the connected limb and up the open limb. Always inspect which side of the manometer is named.
- Count single bond as ONE shared electron pair and double bond as TWO, including carbonyl C=O, in displayed ester bonding-pair questions. Position a fractional-distillation thermometer bulb at the outlet sidearm to measure vapour entering condenser.
- A straight-chain but-1-ene drawing can be reversed, rotated or bent without creating a new isomer; compare connectivity (C=C at terminal bond), not page layout. Distinguish methylpropene branched alkene, but-2-ene internal bond, and unsaturated carboxylic acids containing BOTH C=C and –COOH.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PHOSPHINE AND BIOPOLYMERS
- Phosphine PH₃ has THREE shared P–H electron pairs and ONE nonbonding pair on P; P₄ + aqueous NaOH generates PH₃ with a 4P:1PH₃ mole ratio, so use ratio before gas volume at r.t.p. Phosphonium salts warmed with NaOH liberate PH₃, analogous to ammonium salts. Calcium phosphide has P³⁻.
- Proteins and nylon share –CONH– amide linkages but proteins may incorporate varied amino-acid side groups (>two monomer types). Protein hydrolysis to amino acids can use concentrated HCl with heat/reflux OR protease at suitable temperature. Chromatographed amino acids often require locating agent before comparison of Rf with references.
- Do not infer a universal numerical Haber 'accepted range' from one paper: mark schemes can specify narrower or broader ranges for a question. Distinguish equilibrium yield explanations from reaction-rate explanations in equilibrium questions.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ZINC CHLORIDE AND CARBON MONOXIDE
- Molten ZnCl₂ conducts by MOBILE ions, gives Zn at negative cathode and Cl₂ at positive anode; dilute aqueous ZnCl₂ can instead oxidise OH⁻ at anode: 4OH⁻→O₂+2H₂O+4e⁻. Zn²⁺ test: add aqueous NaOH or NH₃, white precipitate dissolves in excess to colourless solution.

- Reaction rate graph average over a time interval = change in product volume/time, with correct volume/time units. When acid is excess but metal consumed, catalyst alters initial rate and time to plateau, not the final total gas volume. Thermal carbonate decomposition becomes slower/harder for more reactive metals under comparable heat conditions.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — TITRATION, AIR AND ENERGY
- To REUSE a titration reaction flask rinse it with tap water then distilled water; residual distilled water does not change moles of carefully pipetted alkali added next, whereas rinsing flask with alkali may leave extra unmeasured moles. Burette/pipette rinsing requirements differ.
- Reject initial collected gas in an apparatus if it contains DISPLACED AIR from the apparatus (nitrogen), not because reaction catalyst generates another gas. Rusting wet steel wool in inverted tube consumes only about one-fifth trapped air oxygen so water rises partly inside tube, not to top.
- Photosynthesis converts light energy to chemical energy; carbohydrate combustion releases stored chemical energy as heat. For two allotropes to demonstrate same element, equal masses fully combusting to same CO₂ amount supports same elemental composition; conductivity difference alone does not establish composition.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PHOTO-OXIDATION, CROWN ETHERS
- Sodium ANION Na⁻ has 12 electrons, shell arrangement 2,8,2 (distinct from Na⁺ 2,8). A crown ether composed of C₁₂H₂₄O₆ simplifies to empirical C₂H₄O.
- Oxidation half-equation for water to oxygen must balance both matter and charge: 2H₂O→O₂+4H⁺+4e⁻. Acidified hydrogen peroxide oxidation of iodide gives iodine and water; an observed rate table may show iodide concentration proportional to rate but acid concentration NO effect under specified conditions. Do not assume every reactant's concentration affects rate alike.

Hydrated Na₂SO₄·10H₂O Mr322; water tests: white anhydrous CuSO₄→blue or blue anhydrous cobalt chloride→pink.
- Poly(glycollic acid) is a condensation polyester formed from an –OH and –COOH in the same monomer (hydroxycarboxylic acid). It shares –COO– ester linkages with Terylene. When comparing equal molarity HF and HCl, weaker HF partially ionises so has higher pH and less H⁺; distinction is ionisation degree, not acid concentration.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — AIR SEPARATION AND ENVIRONMENT
- Fractional distillation of air: liquefy air by cooling, then warm so lower-boiling nitrogen leaves while oxygen remains liquid; oxyacetylene very hot flame welds metals. Ozone forms 3O₂→2O₃ and upper-atmospheric layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation, reducing skin cancer risk.
- Ammonium fertilisers + calcium hydroxide release gaseous ammonia that escapes, removing nitrogen; dissolved fertilisers leach through soil/groundwater into lakes, causing eutrophication. For insoluble sulfate from soluble barium salt, add soluble sulfate/acid, filter precipitate, wash with water, then dry.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — FUEL CELLS, NITRATES, SILANE
- In alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell hydrogen-side negative electrode oxidises H₂+2OH⁻→2H₂O+2e⁻, electrons flow externally to positive oxygen electrode. Oxygen-generating alkaline electrode equation 4OH⁻→O₂+2H₂O+4e⁻ applies to electrolysis, NOT to fuel-cell oxygen reduction. Hydrogen storage can be explosive/need strong pressurised tanks; fuels made using fossil fuel may not be renewable.

Eutrophication sequence: nitrate/phosphate leach, algae bloom, light blocked, plants die, decomposing bacteria proliferate and consume dissolved oxygen, aquatic animals die.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — NICKEL AND IODOMETRY
- Nickel carbonyl Ni(CO)₄ is volatile: impure nickel reacts with CO to form vapour while non-reacting impurities remain; heat carbonyl to decompose into pure nickel and CO. Electrolytic nickel refining: impure Ni positive anode, pure Ni negative cathode, aqueous nickel salt electrolyte, DC supply.
- In iodometric thiosulfate titration I₂+2S₂O₃²⁻→2I⁻+S₄O₆²⁻: calculate thiosulfate molarity using Mr of its HYDRATED salt if present, then iodine mol is half thiosulfate mol. For cold dilute NaOH, Cl₂+2NaOH→NaClO+NaCl+H₂O.
- Acid–alkali fertiliser ammonium sulfate crystallisation: titrate aqueous ammonia and dilute sulfuric acid using indicator to find equivalence, repeat without indicator, evaporate partially then cool to crystallise. Ammonium nitrate NH₄NO₃ contains 28/80×100=35% nitrogen by mass.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CALCIUM AND ALLOYS
- Calcium atom 2,8,8,2; Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ both 2,8,8 in CaCl₂. Molten CaCl₂ electrolysis yields calcium cathode and chlorine anode; DILUTE aqueous CaCl₂ yields hydrogen cathode. Alloying Fe with larger Mn atoms disrupts regular iron layers, preventing easy sliding and reducing malleability.
- In extracting iron, assess blast-furnace carbon burning to CO₂, reduction of CO₂ to CO by coke, Fe₂O₃ reduction by CO, limestone CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂ and SiO₂ impurity removal as CaSiO₃ slag. For finite acid/metal, identify limiting reagent before calculating H₂ volume.
- Enzyme-catalysed peroxide decomposition increases in rate with temperature until yeast catalase denatures at high temperature, rapidly slowing the reaction. Ozone absorbs harmful UV; halogenoalkane damage of ozone increases risk of skin cancer.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ALUMINIUM EXTRACTION AND EQUILIBRIUM
- Aluminium extraction: molten purified Al₂O₃ dissolved with cryolite (lower operating temperature/increase conductivity); graphite/carbon electrodes, Al³⁺+3e⁻→Al at cathode, 2O²⁻→O₂+4e⁻ at anode; oxygen can consume carbon anode forming CO₂. Protective Al₂O₃ coat can suppress an otherwise feasible displacement with aqueous copper(II) sulfate; disrupting the coat can permit redox.
- Dynamic equilibrium in a closed system: forward and reverse reactions continue at EQUAL rates and concentrations remain constant, not necessarily equal to each other. For endothermic 2HI⇌H₂+I₂, heating favours products and raises H₂/I₂ fraction. Test iodide using aqueous silver nitrate or lead nitrate: yellow precipitate.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CHROMATOGRAPHY, CARBON TRACERS, SILICONES
- Chromatography baseline/spots ABOVE solvent; co-run standards, match travelled distances or Rf. Rf = distance spot from baseline ÷ distance solvent front from baseline; locating agents can reveal invisible/colorless spots. Cu²⁺ with NH₃ gives pale blue ppt initially, dissolves in excess to deep blue solution; with OH⁻: Cu²⁺(aq)+2OH⁻(aq)→Cu(OH)₂(s).
- Labelled ¹⁸O in water used in photosynthesis appears in released O₂, not the glucose oxygen in the illustrated isotope-tracing equation (which comes from CO₂). Oxygen-18 neutral atom has 8 protons, 10 neutrons and 8 electrons.
- Silicone fluid with –O–Si(C₂H₅)₂– chain can derive from dihydroxy monomer HO–Si(C₂H₅)₂–OH; do not confuse Si–O backbone with carbon–carbon addition polymer. Acidified bromate(V)+bromide consumes H⁺ while generating orange/brown bromine: BrO₃⁻+5Br⁻+6H⁺→3Br₂+3H₂O.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — HYDRIDES AND FRACTIONAL COMPOSITION
- Hydride H⁻ reacts with water by H⁻+H₂O→OH⁻+H₂; both NaH and CaH₂ follow the same ionic change. Group I density GENERALLY rises down group (potassium is less dense than sodium); reactivity with water rises down the group.
- For seawater salt quantification, convert MgCl₂ mass concentration into molarity using Mr95 then DOUBLE for chloride-ion molarity. For precipitation of sulfate, 96g SO₄²⁻ generates 233g BaSO₄; scale the mass from dm³ to measured cm³ aliquot.
- Melting points of homologous alkanes beyond ethane generally rise with increasing carbons but may show odd–even zigzag. For Haber reaction N₂+3H₂⇌2NH₃, pressure increases ammonia equilibrium yield while temperature decreases it; practical settings compromise rate/yield/cost, and catalyst lowers activation energy without moving equilibrium.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — BORON NITRIDE, OZONE, GLYCOLIC ACID
- Hexagonal boron nitride has layered sheets weakly attracted between layers; sliding produces slipperiness as in graphite. Graphite has strong covalent bonds IN sheets and delocalised electrons that conduct; weak BETWEEN-sheet forces do not imply low melting point.
- Ozone photochemical decomposition 2O₃→3O₂. In acidic Fe²⁺ oxidation, 2Fe²⁺+2H⁺+O₃→2Fe³⁺+H₂O+O₂. Chlorofluorocarbons deplete protective stratospheric ozone, permitting excessive UV and raising skin-cancer/cataract risk.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PHOSPHORUS CHLORIDES AND PERSPEX
- PCl₅(g)⇌PCl₃(g)+Cl₂(g): reduced pressure favours two-gas-molecule product side; increased chlorine shifts toward PCl₅. If heating increases equilibrium %PCl₃ then forward dissociation is endothermic. Hydrolysis: PCl₅+4H₂O→H₃PO₄+5HCl.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — GRAPH INTERPRETATION AND SALT COUNTING

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — WATER IONISATION AND RATE APPARATUS
- Heating water shifts its endothermic self-ionisation toward equal additional H⁺ and OH⁻, not selectively acidic or alkaline; neutrality is a relative equality, not an invariant concentration.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ALCOHOLS, PEROXODISULFATE AND TITRATION
- For homologous alcohols, higher carbon number generally raises boiling point and viscosity; larger/longer molecules give stronger intermolecular forces and entanglement. A substance is liquid at ambient temperature when its melting point is below and boiling point above ambient.
- Peroxodisulfate oxidises iodide: S₂O₈²⁻+2I⁻→2SO₄²⁻+I₂; it also oxidises Fe²⁺: S₂O₈²⁻+2Fe²⁺→2SO₄²⁻+2Fe³⁺. Fe³⁺ oxidises iodide while Fe³⁺ itself gains an electron to Fe²⁺. Use original experiment table to compare independent concentration changes.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — UNUSUAL RATE DEPENDENCE AND ALLOYS
- For acid-catalysed iodination of propanone, the tabulated initial-rate comparisons show rate proportional to propanone concentration and acid concentration separately, but independent of iodine concentration in those conditions. Do not assume every reactant concentration must affect measured rate identically.
- Alloys with differently sized atoms disrupt the regular metal lattice, hindering layer sliding; in the measured brass compositions, higher zinc percentage correlates with higher strength. Preserve the scope of a composition table rather than extrapolating without evidence.
- Group I down-group trends: decreasing melting/boiling point and hardness, increasing reactivity and generally increasing density; list two distinct trends if requested. A relative atomic mass depends on isotope abundances, not strictly on proton number.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — REVERSE ACTIVATION AND FERMENTATION
- Forward activation energy is reactants→transition-state peak; reverse activation energy is products→same peak. For endothermic forward reaction subtract positive ΔH from forward barrier to find reverse barrier; do not make activation energy negative.
- In fermentation yeast enzymes are temperature-sensitive: increasing to 100°C does not imply faster fermentation. Ethene steam hydration and sugar fermentation both use catalysts but have different feedstock, conditions and product concentration.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — NEUTRAL SALTS AND HYDRATES
- Exact acid–alkali neutralisation leaves dissolved salt ions and water's tiny equal H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations; neutral does NOT mean those ions are entirely absent. Water self-ionises even in neutral salt solution.
- In hydrate mass loss compare anhydrous salt Mr with full hydrate Mr before multiplying initial sample mass. For total constituent atoms in samples multiply molecular atom count by number of moles; molecules count alone does not suffice.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — BIFUNCTIONAL MONOMERS AND METAL OXIDES
- A molecule with both C=C and –OH/–COOH can support addition polymerisation through C=C and condensation polyester formation through ester links; when drawing at least two units show correct backbone, side groups and continuation bonds.
- An aqueous copper(II) sulfate solution contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻ AND water-derived H⁺, OH⁻; inert-electrode electrolysis removes Cu²⁺ at cathode and oxidises OH⁻ at anode, leaving solution progressively less blue and more acidic.
- Use ease of metal oxide reduction to order reactivity: easiest oxide decomposition corresponds to least reactive metal, oxide resistant to carbon reduction corresponds to more reactive metal.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — BRINE, CHROMATOGRAPHY AND CATALYSIS
- In concentrated aqueous NaCl electrolysis, H⁺ reduces instead of Na⁺ at cathode, Cl⁻ oxidises at anode, aqueous OH⁻ persists with Na⁺ and solution becomes alkaline. Aqueous electrolyte includes both water-derived H⁺ and OH⁻.
- Chromatography: apply sample on pencil starting line above solvent, run solvent, mark solvent front before it disappears, measure dye travel and solvent travel, compute Rf and compare known samples; separate colours alone may not identify unknown dyes.
- A catalytic intermediate can be consumed then reformed (e.g. NO₂→NO→NO₂), so catalyst is unchanged overall. Energy profiles for catalyst and no catalyst share reactant/product levels but have lower catalysed activation-energy peak.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — TITRATION AND HALIDE CHROMATOGRAPHY
- Rinse titration conical flask with distilled water after emptying; do not rinse with measured titrant or analyte because that changes reactant moles. A colourless chromatographic spot may need locating agent while a coloured transition-metal spot may be directly visible.
- Ionic extraction by electrolysis requires a molten electrolyte or conducting solution, not a solid ionic oxide with fixed ions. For metal X less reactive than carbon, carbon reduction of the oxide can be available even if a more reactive metal cannot reduce X.
- A cycloalkane can be saturated yet have formula CnH2n and NOT be an isomer of the acyclic alkane CnH2n+2. Read ring vs chain bonds before classifying an illustrated molecule.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — MELTING PURITY AND POLYESTERS
- A pure crystalline material has a sharp melting temperature; mixtures/impurities melt over a range. A sharp 0°C melting sample is also pure, not only a high-melting sample.
- Natural fat and synthetic Terylene both contain ester links, whereas natural protein and synthetic nylon contain amide links. Poly(lactic acid) is a condensation polyester, not an ethene addition polymer.
- For mass measurement on combustion retain solid product in vessel/crucible: burning Mg held in tongs loses powder and cannot accurately measure gain from oxygen.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CHROMATE AND MULTIFUNCTIONAL ACIDS
- Acidification shifts 2CrO₄²⁻+2H⁺⇌Cr₂O₇²⁻+H₂O from yellow chromate toward orange dichromate. Alkali removes H⁺, reversing toward yellow; pressure has no equilibrium effect without gases.
- A dicarboxylic acid has TWO acidic protons per molecule for complete neutralisation, requiring twice as many OH⁻ moles as acid moles. Reduction by acidified iodide can release brown I₂ from initially colourless solution.
- Beware scales awarding two marks for four listed water-vs-hydrogen-sulfide contrasts: water liquid, neutral, nonflammable, not reducing and not reacting with aqueous alkali under the specified comparison.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PHOSPHINE AND BISMUTH HYDROLYSIS
- PH₃ has three P–H bond pairs and one P lone pair; NH₃ is more soluble in water, alkaline to damp red litmus and stable on gentle warming compared with PH₃. Thermal phosphine decomposition: 2PH₃→2P+3H₂; ammonia diffuses faster due lower Mr.
- Hydrolysis BiCl₃(aq)+H₂O(l)⇌BiOCl(s)+2HCl(aq) gives white precipitate; adding concentrated HCl dissolves precipitate by shifting left. Pressure does not shift equilibria with no gases. A bridged chloride bismuth complex can be Bi₂Cl₈²⁻ when drawn with eight Cl.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — DRY GAS COLLECTION AND ISOMERIC ALCOHOLS
- To dry acidic SO₂ use concentrated sulfuric acid rather than basic CaO, which reacts with SO₂. Collect denser-than-air gas by downward delivery into upright vessel, avoiding apparatus that loses gas.
- C₄H₁₀O has four alcohol structural isomers (straight-chain 1- and 2-butanol; branched 1- and 2-methylpropanol). Distinguish this from ether isomers when question restricts functional class.
- With inert electrodes, aqueous CuSO₄ loses Cu²⁺ and fades blue; dilute sulfuric acid electrolysis consumes water and acid concentration changes. Electrolyte identity alone does not imply constant concentration.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — TRANSITION IODIDE AND PHASE-CONDUCTIVITY
- A coloured X²⁺ transition-metal ion can oxidise iodide to iodine while itself being reduced to X⁺, giving insoluble XI: 2X²⁺+4I⁻→2XI+I₂. Distinguish agent and ion oxidation-number changes.
- A metal conducts in solid AND molten states through mobile electrons; molten ionic compound may conduct but the solid does not. Heating endothermic ammonium nitrate solution absorbs energy from water, making surroundings cooler.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — DISTILLATION, HALOGEN TRENDS AND POLYMER LINKAGES
- Distillation of an aqueous salt uses a flask connected to a condenser: heat the solution, lower-boiling water vaporises alone then condenses as liquid; separate excess insoluble oxide first by filtration.
- Group VII estimates can interpolate boiling points and liquid densities; halogen colour darkens down the group, with astatine black/grey-black/purple-black. Chlorine disinfects water and oxidises iodide to iodine; iodine cannot displace chloride.
- A molecule containing amino, carboxyl AND hydroxyl groups can condense into polymers with either amide bonds (amino–carboxyl) or ester bonds (hydroxyl–carboxyl). Identify polyester monomers as matching dicarboxylic acid and diol around the ester repeat.
- Chemical ozone depletion by chlorofluorocarbons permits more harmful ultraviolet radiation to reach Earth, increasing skin cancer/eye cataracts. Non-biodegradable plastics cannot be broken down by organisms: disposal may obstruct drains, trap or be eaten by animals; burning may produce toxic gases or greenhouse emissions.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — FRACTIONATION AND CHROMATOGRAPHY
- Fractional distillation of crude oil: vaporise and introduce petroleum near bottom of long column; column hot below and cooler above; different boiling points cause fractions to condense at different heights. Kerosene serves aircraft engines/heating/cooking; naphtha is feedstock for chemical manufacture.
- Use pencil chromatogram baseline because ink dissolves, travels and separates; spray invisible amino-acid spots with a locating agent; Rf is distance travelled from baseline by solute divided by solvent-front distance from baseline.
- Protein hydrolysis to amino acids may use heating/reflux with hydrochloric acid. Amide-linked polypeptide repeats include both the N–C(=O) linkages and continuation bonds; serine also bears an alcohol OH so can form ester links with carboxyl groups.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — IONISATION AND ELECTRODE MASS
- In water H2O ⇌ H⁺+OH⁻, heating an endothermic ionisation raises BOTH ion concentrations without making pure water acidic or alkaline. In aqueous brine H⁺ discharge at the cathode leaves OH⁻ and raises pH, unlike molten sodium chloride products.
- In aqueous copper(II) sulfate with copper electrodes, at constant current the copper anode loses mass steadily/linearly as Cu→Cu²⁺+2e⁻.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — HYDROLYSIS AND HALOALKANE SUBSTITUTION
- Acid anhydride hydrolysis uses one molecule water to yield two carboxylic acid molecules; ethanol esterifies ethanoic acid to ethyl ethanoate. UV-initiated alkane chlorination can substitute more than one hydrogen if the resulting haloalkane retains correct carbon valences and original chain length.
- Nylon/polyamides contain nitrogen, so burning can yield nitrogen oxides, unlike polymers composed only of carbon/hydrogen/oxygen.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CARBIDES, TRANSITION METALS AND NITRATE CRYSTALS
- Calcium carbide is ionic (Ca²⁺ and C₂²⁻) with strong attractions unlike discrete carbon dioxide molecules with weak intermolecular attractions. CaC₂+2H₂O→C₂H₂+Ca(OH)₂; ethyne can add one or two equivalents of hydrogen, producing C₂H₄ or C₂H₆.
- During aqueous copper(II) sulfate electrolysis with inert graphite, oxygen bubbles at anode, copper coats cathode pink and blue solution fades. Copper(II) with excess ammonia forms a dark blue soluble complex after initial pale blue precipitate, but with sodium hydroxide the blue precipitate remains insoluble.
- Hydrazine has two N–H bonds at each N, one N–N bond and a lone pair on each nitrogen; nitrate thermolysis of barium nitrate yields BaO, NO₂ and O₂. Acidified potassium manganate(VII) purple→colourless indicates a reducing substance.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — OXIDISING TESTS, FUEL CELLS AND FLUE GAS
- Add aqueous potassium iodide to test for an oxidising agent: oxidation of iodide gives brown iodine. Flue-gas desulfurisation can use calcium carbonate to remove acidic sulfur gases.
- Hydrogen fuel cell electrodes generate electricity as H₂ and O₂ form water; schemes may credit reduced harmful exhaust and greater efficiency compared with petrol engines, while hydrogen can be manufactured by hydrocarbon cracking or water electrolysis. Distinguish fuel production from fuel renewability in the particular question.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CHROMATOGRAPHY RESOLUTION AND GAS VOLUMES
- If coloured chromatogram spots overlap, repeat with a different solvent to resolve them; locating agent instead reveals invisible spots and cannot separate co-travelling dyes.
- When gas-volume stoichiometry specifies room conditions after methane combustion, subtract consumed oxygen and include leftover gaseous oxygen and carbon dioxide; exclude condensed liquid water.
- For acid/diamine condensation polyamides, count carbon atoms in each entire dicarboxylic acid and diamine monomer, including carboxyl carbons; distinguish polyester O-linkages from polyamide N–H linkages.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — FRACTIONAL DISTILLATION, SOLUBILITY AND WATER PURIFICATION
- Pure solid after crystallisation generally has a sharper, higher melting point than its impure sample; industrial oxygen from air requires fractional distillation of liquefied air rather than filtration.
- Desalination removes dissolved salts (distillation works); filtration removes solids, chlorination disinfects, activated carbon removes tastes/odours. Phosphates encourage plant growth but excess may cause eutrophication.
- In an exothermic equilibrium making fewer gas molecules, higher pressure and lower temperature favour product; a catalyst changes rate, not equilibrium position.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ALKENYL ACIDS AND PHOSPHORUS HALIDES
- An unsaturated carboxylic acid bearing C=C can undergo addition polymerisation leaving pendant carboxylic-acid groups, or esterify with methanol to retain C=C and replace –COOH with –COOCH₃.
- Phosphorus(III) chloride hydrolyses in water: PCl₃+3H₂O→H₃PO₃+3HCl. Heating hydroxide with aluminium reduces nitrate ions to ammonia; eutrophication proceeds algal bloom→light blocked→plants die→decomposer oxygen consumption→fish death.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — MOLECULAR PHOSPHINE AND SODIUM PHOSPHIDE
- Ionic sodium phosphide has a strongly bonded giant lattice and mobile ions only when molten; PH₃ is a small covalent molecule with weak intermolecular attractions, three P–H bonds and one P lone pair. 6Na+N₂→2Na₃N forms sodium nitride.
- Catalytic exhaust converters convert NOx with carbon monoxide to nitrogen and carbon dioxide; unburnt hydrocarbon alongside CO in exhaust signals incomplete combustion. Lower concentration of limiting acid with excess metal reduces BOTH initial gradient and final hydrogen-volume plateau.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ESTIMATED PURITY AND REACTANT GRAPHS
- Percentage yield = isolated mass / stoichiometric theoretical mass ×100; use limiting reagent, not all of the excess reactant. If metal is limiting and acid in excess, H₂ volume plateaus while acid concentration falls to a NONZERO level.
- Bromine water and redox schemes depend on which substance is explicitly underlined: Cu²⁺→Cu is reduction, whereas CO→CO₂ is oxidation even when both occur in same equation. Sulfate precipitate preparation may use soluble nitrate and dilute sulfuric acid (e.g. PbSO₄).

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — DRY GAS AND POLYAMIDE MONOMERS
- To prepare dry hydrogen containing soluble H₂S impurity, pass gas THROUGH water first to remove H₂S, then THROUGH concentrated sulfuric acid to remove water vapour; inspect dip-tube entry/exit orientation, not labels alone.
- BF₃ has three shared electron pairs and boron only six surrounding electrons (incomplete octet). Amino acids bearing NH₂ and COOH can self-condense to polyamide with matching –C(=O)–NH– repeat.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — HALOGENS, POLYESTERS AND SACRIFICIAL ZINC
- For fully specified halogen ionic equations with state symbols, ensure Br₂(aq)+2I⁻(aq)→I₂(aq)+2Br⁻(aq); a less reactive halogen cannot displace a more reactive halide. Hydrated sulfate crystal water is water of crystallisation.
- A polyester repeat –O–(CH₂)₆–O–C(=O)–(CH₂)₆–C(=O)– requires hexane-1,6-diol HO(CH₂)₆OH AND octanedioic acid HOOC(CH₂)₆COOH: count carboxyl carbons separately.
- Zinc sacrificially protects iron because Zn is more reactive and oxidises preferentially even if coating is damaged; ships' hulls and underground pipes are applications. Rusting requires oxygen AND water.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — AMMONIUM FERTILISERS AND HYDRATED NICKEL
- Avoid adding an alkaline lime such as Ca(OH)₂ immediately after ammonium fertiliser: hydroxide liberates NH₃ gas which escapes, wasting nitrogen. Anhydrous NiCl₂ can be rehydrated with water to green NiCl₂·6H₂O.
- In metal electroplating or copper refining, metal ions gain electrons at cathode and deposit a layer that can improve corrosion resistance or surface hardness. Correct ionic equations need both charge and state symbols when explicitly requested.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — BROMINE EQUILIBRIUM AND RATE SAMPLING
- Br₂(aq)+H₂O ⇌ OBr⁻+Br⁻+2H⁺: adding acid or bromide shifts left and darkens orange bromine; diluting with water makes paler. A rate-change graph requires repeated observations at different times, not just a single gas-volume reading at one time.
- Oxidation-number questions: N in HNO₂ and NF₃ both +3; bond energies give ΔH = bonds broken − bonds formed, and reverse activation energy = forward activation energy − forward ΔH. H₂/O₂ fuel cells electrochemically produce electricity without burning hydrogen in a flame.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — DIFFUSION AND IDENTIFICATION OF ALUMINIUM
- Distinguish ongoing random motion from net diffusion: once dissolved particles are evenly distributed there is no net concentration-gradient transport. Melting plateau has solid and liquid together.
- Al³⁺ gives white hydroxide with both NaOH and NH₃; it dissolves in excess NaOH but NOT excess NH₃, unlike Zn²⁺ whose white precipitate dissolves in both.
- Chromatogram Rf distances must both start at baseline, not the bottom of paper: (spot level−baseline)/(front level−baseline). Fractional distillation leaves residue in heated flask.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — ACID TITRATION AND COPPER IN OXYGEN
- Copper may react with sulfuric acid in the presence of oxygen even though it does not displace hydrogen from ordinary dilute acid alone: 2Cu+2H₂SO₄+O₂→2CuSO₄+2H₂O. A zinc-limited HCl reaction at higher acid concentration ends at the SAME hydrogen volume but has a steeper initial gradient.
- In concentrated aqueous CaBr₂, bromine at anode and hydrogen at cathode; in MOLTEN CaBr₂, bromine at anode but calcium at cathode. Acidified silver nitrate gives cream bromide precipitate.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PRESSURE AND CONTACT CONVERTERS
- For CO(g)+2H₂(g)⇌CH₃OH(g) exothermic, pressure increase shifts right (3→1 gas molecules), heating shifts left; exhaust catalytic converters may follow 2CO+2NO→2CO₂+N₂.
- Concentrated aqueous magnesium iodide yields iodine and hydrogen on electrolysis, while molten magnesium iodide yields iodine and magnesium. Iodide reduces MnO₂ by donating electrons; phosphorus(III) iodide PI₃ has one P lone pair.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — EQUILIBRIUM AND REACTION PATHWAYS
- Endothermic pathway products lie above reactants, ΔH points upward; activation energy points from reactant energy to peak. In Fe(CO)₅(l)⇌Fe(s)+5CO(g), decreasing pressure favours CO-rich right side, and allowing CO to escape shifts reaction toward more iron.
- When comparing moles for limiting reagent, divide available water moles by three for S₂Cl₂+3H₂O: if calculated possible S₂Cl₂ exceeds supplied S₂Cl₂ then water is in excess.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — METAL OXIDES AND POLYMER RECYCLING
- Fe₃O₄+8HCl→FeCl₂+2FeCl₃+4H₂O: mixed-valence oxide produces both iron(II) and iron(III) salts. In 3Fe(s)+4H₂O(g)⇌Fe₃O₄(s)+4H₂(g), pressure does not alter equilibrium position: four gaseous moles on each side.
- PET can be chemically recycled by breaking the polymer into monomers and polymerising those monomers again. For diol+dicarboxylic acid polyesters, connect alternating monomers through –O–C(=O)– ester linkages in correct orientation.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — HYDRIDE AND GAS COLLECTION
- In ionic calcium hydride CaH₂, each H⁻ has a two-electron filled first shell. Hydroxylamine NH₂OH has four single bonds/eight bonding electrons, and total electrons count includes all nuclei's atomic numbers.
- Dry an acidic, denser-than-air gas such as SO₂ using concentrated sulfuric acid rather than reactive calcium oxide; displace air with inlet reaching toward bottom of upright collecting vessel, not delivery through the top.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — NET IONIC CARBONATE EQUATIONS
- For insoluble solid calcium carbonate reacting with aqueous acid, retain CaCO₃(s) in net ionic equation: CaCO₃(s)+2H⁺(aq)→Ca²⁺(aq)+H₂O(l)+CO₂(g). Do not replace solid carbonate with dissolved CO₃²⁻.
- In acid electrolysis O₂:H₂ gas volumes at anode:cathode are 1:2; concentrated brine Cl₂:H₂ are 1:1. An excess carbonate mass-loss experiment must plateau at nonzero mass even if CO₂ escapes.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — CYCLIC ISOMERS AND TRANSITION EMPIRICAL FORMULAE
- Saturated cyclic cyclopropane and unsaturated propene share C₃H₆ molecular formula despite differing structures/functional behaviour; a saturated hydrocarbon need not be an alkane.
- On deriving empirical formula from mass percentages, find missing metal percentage by subtracting others from 100, divide each percentage by Ar, then scale ratios; nickel carboxylate example can have empirical NiC₄O₄.

ADDITIONAL CHAPTER-WISE CHECKLIST — PEROXIDE AND WATER QUALITY
- Hydrogen peroxide can act as reducing agent (reduces acidified purple MnO₄⁻ to colourless) AND as oxidising agent (oxidises aqueous I⁻ to brown iodine). Peroxide O₂²⁻ with Ba²⁺ forms BaO₂; molecular H₂O₂ has single O–O bond and two lone pairs per O.
- Water treatment sedimentation/filtration removes insoluble solids, carbon removes odour/taste, chlorination kills microbes. Dissolved oxygen is beneficial for aquatic life; sewage microbes, toxic metals, microplastics, nitrate/phosphate runoff may harm water quality.
